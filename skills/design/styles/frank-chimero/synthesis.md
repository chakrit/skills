# Frank Chimero: synthesis

The design model behind `style.md`, drawn from four research streams. Citations name the
note and its section: `own M5` is move M5 in `notes/own.md` and `own §2` its "Process
and thinking"; `work Move 4` and `work table` refer to `notes/work.md`; `talks 1.8` and
`talks §2` to `notes/talks.md`; `accounts 3` is move 3 in `notes/accounts.md`, other
`accounts` sections by heading. *The Shape of Design* (2012) is free to read online under
CC BY-NC-SA; the `own` stream read it in full and its findings are cited as `own §2` with
the chapter.

Evidence marks: **(seen)** checked by eye on a live page on 2026-11-23; **(Wayback)**
seen only in an Internet Archive capture, viewed before the research stopped using
Wayback; **(CSS)** read from stylesheets or markup; **(said)** his own stated view;
**(reported)** someone else's account, with no visual check.

## 1. The core idea: design as articulation

He describes three ideas of design he has held in turn: decoration (his editorial
illustration years), construction (interfaces), and articulation, the one he settled on:
describe the problem as accurately as possible, wait for its grain to show, and make the
work "ever more definite, nuanced, and clear" (own §2 "Design as articulation"; talks §2)
(said). He asks whether you can write a design, and chases verbs over nouns (own §2)
(said). His editor reads his book's central claim the same way: design is a response to
a situation, filtered through the designer's disposition, not a solution (accounts,
"Design as response, not solution") (reported).

Three consequences run through everything else:

- **Make it look like what it is.** His 2013 holding page asked "What does it want to
  be?" and "Make it look like what it is." (own §2) (Wayback); he repeats the second
  line about his own navigation (talks 1.6) (said).
- **Clarity over simplicity.** "Simple things are weak"; he wants "clear and evident
  things", "full of seams", and treats legible source as part of the design (talks 1.12)
  (said).
- **One thing per piece, with a light touch.** Choose the one thing a piece should do
  (own §2 "One thing per piece", from "Ten Principles") (said); find "the one or two
  uniquely true things" about a client and express them "with a light touch"; "a great
  photo, memorable illustration, or pitch-perfect sentence does most of the work" (own
  M13; talks 1.10) (said).

## 2. Where ideas come from

- **Questions first.** Well-phrased questions are "heat-seeking missiles for clarity";
  he wants clarity at 10,000 feet and at two feet, and "everything in between becomes the
  opportunity" (talks §2 "Questions first") (said).
- **The feeling of the thing, carried by one structural departure.** Do Lectures sorted
  talks by tone as well as topic because a visitor wants to know how a talk will make
  them feel; Thinking for a Living was turned on its side for the iPad and aimed at "a
  calm environment suitable for reading"; Web Press printed web ephemera as a newspaper
  because both are cheap and transient (work Move 3) (said; TFAL Wayback).
- **Unlike things side by side.** He calls his tone "sprawling": two things that seem
  unrelated, brought together (talks 1.13) (said). Aspirin and GUIs, Hockney and
  responsive design, Spirited Away and AI (talks 1.13); his heroes cross disciplines
  (own §3) (said).
- **An odd, concrete object to open and close.** Talks open on a specific artefact (pills
  padded with filler, the dial-up modem sound, a 41,000-year-old handprint) and return
  to it changed at the end (talks 1.4) (said).
- **Words unlock the image.** Naming his talk "The Eye Knows" made the poster click
  (work §2) (said); he builds a talk by adding connective sentences between images
  (talks 1.1, §2) (said).
- **Respond to the site.** Like Robert Irwin, study the context before deciding; many
  good answers exist, as the Shaker, Eames and Gehry chairs show (own §2, book ch6)
  (said).
- **Why before how.** Copying a finished surface without its purpose is a mockingbird
  imitating a car alarm (own §2, book ch1) (said; FINE's review repeats it, accounts,
  "How and Why").

## 3. Process

1. **Write the purpose.** Write the objectives, put them away, start with deliberately
   bad ideas to get moving, then bring the objectives back (own §2, book ch3) (said).
2. **Set limits, then "yes, and" inside them.** Limits of content, structure and tone;
   expect waste; cheap paper so bad ideas cost nothing (own §2) (said). He uses loose
   copier paper and a wooden pencil, each sheet numbered so the sequence of ideas shows
   (talks §2 "Pencil piles") (said).
3. **A wall, then a fake deck.** Index cards and printouts taped to a wall, sorted into
   groups until meaning emerges; then a deck as a visual outline, one image or one
   sentence per slide, with connective slides to make a story (talks §2 "Building a
   talk"; work §2) (said).
4. **Message, tone, format as separate levers.** Tone is not style; style is a tool for
   tone; new formats make breakthroughs (own §2, book ch4) (said).
5. **Narrow screen first.** Choose the order, stack it, settle hierarchy by colour, face,
   size and link style; wider layouts inherit it (talks 1.7) (said).
6. **Lock-ups before boxes.** Compose groups of elements first, then assign them to a
   space; design how pieces build up, not how they break down (own M12; talks 1.8)
   (said).
7. **To markup fast.** A drawing tool only to see colours side by side and roll through
   faces; once structure, faces, sizes and hierarchy are set, move to markup, where the
   real thing becomes the sketch (talks §2 "From canvas to markup fast") (said).
8. **Design from the most limiting medium outward.** *The Shape of Design* started from
   Kindle: the face, the etching style and the cover were chosen for the worst
   environment, then applied to print and web (own M14; talks 1.9) (said).
9. **Change the design, not the toolchain.** Design the house that is easy to build
   rather than hire cranes (own M13; talks 1.10) (said; read by others as a working
   method, accounts 5) (reported).
10. **Crutch check.** Change course when the work leans on a crutch in how things look
    or how he is thinking (talks §2) (said).

## 4. Type

- **One size, hierarchy from space, case and colour.** *The Shape of Design* in print,
  ebook and web is set in one face at one size; headings are the text size, set apart by
  space, capitals and colour (own M1; talks 1.9) (said). The book site keeps it: Lyon
  Text 20/27px, the chapter name set apart only by a burnt orange (own M1) (seen, CSS).
  His current site sets everything at 14px, the post title marked only by bold, centring
  and space; dates go grey (own M1) (seen, CSS).
- **Monospace as the whole voice.** The current site is plain Courier everywhere with
  old-style figures, 14px/1.5 (own M4) (seen, CSS); on the book site a mono did the
  utility jobs beside a serif (own M4) (CSS). His slides use typewriter-like caps (talks
  1.1, §3) (seen).
- **Or: a light serif voice and a small plain utility face, skipping the middle sizes.**
  Studio Frank: one Signifier Light sentence, Söhne buttons; the 2021 design index:
  Newsreader Light client names up to 92px against 14px system sans captions; the book
  site's serif cover against typewriter quotes (work Move 5) (seen, CSS). Client pairs
  he listed follow the same split: Charter + Source Sans, Georgia + Gotham + Knockout,
  Tiempos Text + Calibre, Leitura + Maison Neue + Druk (work Move 5) (said, Wayback).
- **Serif text faces with character and a plain sans beside them:** Quadraat with
  Quadraat Sans, Tiempos with Lato (accounts, Taste) (reported, Fonts In Use).
- **New plain materials.** Defaults such as Helvetica, Times and link blue carry
  baggage, so a designer keeps looking for new plain faces; Klim's Untitled Sans and
  Serif became studio staples (own M10) (said).
- **Fallbacks sized alike.** He tested his site with web fonts removed, falling back to
  system faces sized to match, and liked it "nearly as much" (talks 1.10) (said).
- **Giant thin display only between movements.** In the art-directed essays single words
  in Lato 100 or Calibre Thin, white, 6em, tracked caps, sit far apart in tall gradient
  sections (own M6) (Wayback).

## 5. Structure and navigation

- **Show all navigation as plain words.** Seven things, seven words on screen; never a
  hamburger on a small site, and navigation stays visible on phones (talks 1.6) (said).
- **Each nav item as a two-line label:** a name, then a dimmer gloss of what and when
  ("Book · 2012", "Collages · 2017–2020") (own M3) (seen).
- **The index is the home page.** On his current site the home page is the index alone,
  with nothing on the reading side (own M2) (seen); his 2021 portfolio is a list with no
  images, names huge on black, year and one line at the sides, hairlines between rows
  (work Move 4) (seen, CSS). Earlier sites used year | item tables that end in jokes
  ("1992 Afternoons with Mario Paint") and an "Otherwise" table (own M3) (Wayback).
- **Ruled rows, dates right.** The post archive and About page use hairline-ruled rows,
  titles left, dates right (own M3) (seen).
- **Split screen for small sites.** Above about 1000px a sticky dark index on one half and
  a light, independently scrolling reading panel with a centred 36rem column on the
  other (own M2) (seen, CSS); the book site does the same with a fixed engraving beside
  scrolling chapter text, the title running up a thin vertical rail in spaced mono caps
  (own M2; work table) (seen).
- **One sentence is the whole page.** studiofrank.co is a full-bleed sky photograph, one
  yellow light-serif sentence, an email pill and a "Booking September" status dot (work
  Move 4) (seen).
- **A named frame orders a long piece.** Acts named by a parallel set (Under / Beside /
  Into / Beyond the machine), each opening on a quiet title card and an epigraph (talks
  1.3) (seen, said).

## 6. Pace: the long page as a sequence

- **Find where time passes and change the rhythm there.** Scroll, sections and line
  height are where a page keeps time; leave a void for the reader to fill (own §2, book
  ch7) (said).
- **One band per beat.** What Screens Want and The Web's Grain stack full-width sections,
  each with its own flat ground (charcoal title, pale mint foreword, white text, black
  where the argument turns), around one 30em serif column (own M5) (Wayback, CSS). Coyier
  read the slowly changing colours as the essay acting out its own point (accounts 3)
  (reported).
- **Breaks as display.** Between movements, a tall gradient section holds a few words far
  apart, so scrolling reads them one at a time (own M6) (Wayback). A key sentence may sit
  as a centred heading between short pale rules (own M7) (Wayback).
- **One thing per screen.** His slides hold one image or one short sentence, never
  bullets (talks 1.1) (seen); a page meant as an argument does the same.
- **Argue by variation.** Show the same subject many times with one variable changed (six
  horses, Muybridge's frames) so the reader sees the point (talks 1.2) (seen, said).
- **A gauge and a payoff.** Atlantis turns scroll into a descent, with a depth counter
  and a "Hi." at the bottom (work Move 1; accounts 2) (seen). This is early work and he
  later argued against showpiece sites (accounts 2, 5), so the gauge is the part to keep,
  not the spectacle.

## 7. Colour

- **Greys and one warm accent.** Burnt orange #b95b23 on the book site, gold in the
  essays, crimson link hover in 2015, red underlines in 2019, sage #5e786d today (own §3
  "Colour habits") (seen, Wayback).
- **Now and then a full saturated field** against that restraint: the 2012 coral home
  page, the peach → pink → lavender → mint gradient breaks, the 2015 collage (own §3)
  (Wayback); studiofrank.co's sky and yellow (work Move 4) (seen).
- **Flat colour and simple gradients** because they stretch to areas of unknown size; the
  web's own patterns, not a monoculture (own M11; talks 1.5) (said).
- **Slow ambient change instead of effects.** His portfolio info page cycles its ground
  through ten pastels over 130 seconds; Atlantis darkens the sea with depth (work Move 6)
  (seen, CSS).
- **Few soft colours, from screen printing**, in his illustration years (accounts 1)
  (reported, Print 2010).
- Current grounds: dark green-grey #3d4340 beside #eee (own M2) (seen, CSS).

## 8. Imagery, ornament and motion

- **Images as plates, often in pairs.** Full column width, small radius; a pair of
  portraits inside one black plate with a small mono caption (own M15) (seen).
- **Collage and layering** from archive images (the 2015 home page, Dust), after
  Hockney's joiners, which he holds up as the model of an edgeless web (own M9) (Wayback;
  Dust seen). Dust balances design's "newness and optimistic clarity" with "ambiguity
  and wistfulness" (own M9) (said).
- **Line etching** for his book, chosen for e-ink and kept as the brand (own M14) (seen).
- **Flat few-colour illustration with a hand-traced edge**, warm and cool swapped late,
  dots and seams as "spice" (talks 1.14; accounts 1) (said, reported). He left
  illustration; treat it as early taste (talks 1.14).
- **Type as signage in an invented world**, with in-world deadpan copy (Atlantis) (work
  Move 2) (seen). Fits when type is the thing shown.
- **Motion that explains.** "Medium flux": animation that shows where something came
  from or went; designing the in-between states is "half of your work"; full-screen
  spectacle is rarely right (talks 1.11; own M8) (said).
- **Short centred rules**, 10rem, not full width (own M15) (CSS).

## 9. The web's grain and the build

- The web wants vertical stacking, fluid widths, scroll, text, flat fills, horizontal
  bands, text as interface, tinted photographs with big type over them, mosaics (own
  M11; talks 1.5) (said).
- The unsolvable tension: an image beside text grows taller as it widens while the text
  grows shorter; manage it rather than fight it (own M11; talks 1.8) (said).
- Against "bicycle bear" sites (scroll-jacked, laggy product pages) and "whizzbangery"
  (talks 1.5, §3; accounts 5) (said, reported).
- A serviceable site is 100–150 lines of CSS (talks 1.5) (said); his current stylesheet
  is about 250 lines, no framework, no JavaScript (own M13) (CSS). Walton praised the
  2014 site for a traditional look and little CSS (accounts 5) (reported).

## 10. Phones

He starts on the narrow screen because it is the easy problem: order, stack, hierarchy
by colour, face, size and link style; wider screens inherit (talks 1.7) (said).
Navigation stays visible as words (talks 1.6) (said). The split screen appears only
above 1000px, so below it the index and reading stack (own M2) (CSS). One-size type
survives any reader setting (own M1) (said). Phone rendering of his pages was not
checked in the research.

## 11. Taste summary

Likes: Alan Fletcher; Hollis, Fiore and Agel's visual paperbacks; Bass, Lustig, Rand and
the Eameses; Hockney's joiners; Muybridge; Baldessari's captioned images; Calvino's
lightness; Jasper Morrison's Super Normal; the Shakers; *Kind of Blue* and J Dilla as
models of practice; plain type, grain and wood, maps, horses, gradients; print's cheap
end, newspapers and posters; "warm, content-focused design" (own §3; talks §3; work §3).
Dislikes: bicycle-bear sites and whizzbangery; the flat versus skeuomorphic argument,
since screens are aesthetically neutral; showmanship and cold over-composition;
seamlessness and simplicity as goals; spaghetti toolchains and illegible source; dark
patterns; the homogenous web; the averaging pull of AI output (own §3; talks §3).

## Cautions and gaps

- **Ban conflicts.** Several of his moves break `../../references/bans.md` as he made
  them; `style.md` leaves them out or rewords them:
  - "The padding isn't around the screens. It's *in* them." (own M7) is both an italic
    word inside a display statement and the contrastive frame. Statements between rules
    stay; that shape of copy does not.
  - Act title cards underline each line with an orange rule (talks 1.3). `style.md`
    keeps one short rule under the whole card.
  - "BUT" and "THEREFORE" slides (talks 1.1) are connective words as display. On a
    business page a lone "But." reads as dramatic fragmentation; `style.md` keeps the
    idea as full sentences that join two beats.
  - Small letter-spaced capital labels on the 2014 site (own M10) would become eyebrows
    above section headings; `style.md` keeps tracked caps for utility text only.
  - Atlantis's "DID YOU KNOW" panels (accounts 2) are boxed blurbs; left out.
  - His named frames are often triads (decoration / construction / articulation).
    Triads in every block of copy are a writing tell; name as many parts as there are.
- **Wayback.** The 2011–2019 site versions, the art-directed essays and TFAL were seen
  only in captures viewed before the research stopped using Wayback; images in What
  Screens Want did not load (own §4; work §4).
- **Early against settled.** Atlantis and the hand-traced illustration are 2009–2010
  work; from 2014 he argued for the web's grain and against showpieces (accounts 2, 5).
  `style.md` takes the gauge and the in-world voice from Atlantis, not its spectacle.
- **Client work is thin.** NPR, Abstract, Safari Flow, Meetup and the Studio Frank
  clients were not seen; their design rests on a line each (work §4). Most of the model
  comes from his own sites, essays and talks.
- **Outside accounts** describe his ideas far more than his visual work; there is no
  outside critique of any version of his site (accounts, Coverage note, Open questions).
- **Dissent.** Some readers found the What Screens Want layout off-putting; others read
  it fine on phones (accounts, Dissent). Commenters called "Everything Easy" nostalgic
  and said the web now serves applications (accounts, Dissent). Alan Jacobs argues the
  right amount of flux depends on genre (accounts, Dissent).
- **Phones** were not checked by eye; section 10 rests on what he said and the CSS.
