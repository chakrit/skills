## 1. Moves

### 1.1 One thing per slide: a full-bleed picture or one short sentence

- **What.** His slides carry either a single image (often full-bleed, often black and
  white, no caption) or a single short declarative line, never bullets. Beyond the
  Machine: two square b/w photos side by side on black with no text; a black slide with
  three centred lines of white typewriter-style caps, "ALL CONVENIENCE / WILL BE
  EXPLOITED / AT SCALE"; a full-bleed b/w photo of an Oblique Strategies card with only a
  tiny two-line caps label at the left. [observed] What Screens Want: white 4:3 slides
  each holding one centred screenshot or one picture. [observed]
- His working slides are the same: a blue line doodle, the word "BUT" set huge in
  condensed bold sans white on black, a photo of Duchamp's Fountain. [observed: Back to
  the Cave post] He calls these "declarative sentences and words like 'BUT' and
  'THEREFORE'" that join images into a story. [stated]
- **Replaces.** Bullet slides and title-plus-body templates.
- **When it fits.** Any sequence of pages or sections meant to be read as an argument:
  let one image or one sentence own a whole screen, and set connective words as display
  type in their own right.

### 1.2 Argue by a run of variations

- **What.** He makes a point by showing the same subject many times with one variable
  changed, one per slide. Six horses (colour photo, b/w photo, drawing, painting, flat,
  skeuomorphic) prove screens are "aesthetically neutral"; a James Burke clip is replayed
  with "plastic" swapped for "software" over a run of new pictures; Muybridge's twelve
  frames, then the animation. [stated, observed: What Screens Want] At XOXO: a cave
  handprint, then slides captioned only "Why does this exist?", "Or this?". [stated:
  Back to the Cave] The Spirited Away section of Beyond the Machine is about 25
  consecutive film stills with no text, narrated aloud. [observed]
- **Replaces.** Asserting a claim once with one illustrative image.
- **When it fits.** Comparison pages, case studies, explainers: a strip or sequence of
  like-for-like panels where the reader sees the variable for themselves.

### 1.3 A named frame that orders the whole piece, with title cards

- **What.** Each talk hangs on one small, explicit device: four acts named by
  preposition, Under / Beside / Into / Beyond the machine (Rubin, Eno, Herndon and
  Dryhurst, Miyazaki) [stated]; decoration / construction / articulation [stated:
  Borderlands]; low / medium / high flux [stated: What Screens Want]; Rumsfeld's known and
  unknown knowns [stated: Only Openings]; employment / collaboration / community and
  four numbered questions [stated: Back to the Cave].
- On the web page, each act of Beyond the Machine opens with a title card: two centred
  lines of condensed typewriter-like serif caps, each underlined with a thin orange rule,
  then a centred italic epigraph with its attribution. [observed]
- **Replaces.** Unmarked sections with generic headings.
- **When it fits.** Long pages: name the sections by a parallel set (prepositions, verbs,
  a three-part scale), give each a quiet typographic title card and an epigraph.

### 1.4 Open on an odd concrete object, close by returning to it

- **What.** Aspirin pills padded with filler (What Screens Want), the dial-up modem sound
  played in the page (The Web's Grain), a Calvino quote (Borderlands), Meno's question
  (Only Openings), a 41,000-year-old handprint (Back to the Cave). Each returns at the end:
  Calvino is re-quoted, Meno is answered, the cave becomes "Cave of the Hands", Rubin's
  Tao verse is revisited. [stated]
- **When it fits.** Any long-form page: lead with a specific object or artefact rather
  than a thesis, and end on it changed.

### 1.5 Go with the grain of the web

- **What.** The web "wants you to stack things vertically", be fluid, scroll vertically,
  carry "quite a bit of text", and use "flat colors or simple gradients" because they
  stretch to boxes of unknown size. [stated: RWD Podcast] Common patterns are proof of a
  shared material, "not evidence of a visual monoculture": flat colour, horizontal stripes
  of content, text as interface, blurred or tinted photos as backgrounds with big type on
  top, products shown in props, mosaics. [stated: The Web's Grain]
- "A perfectly great and serviceable website" in roughly 100 to 150 lines of CSS; old
  1990s pages "still work", they only need bigger type and a max-width for measure.
  [stated: RWD Podcast]
- **Replaces.** "Bicycle bear websites": impressive work against the medium; his example
  is Apple's Mac Pro page (scroll-jacked, laggy, fragile). [stated: The Web's Grain]
- **When it fits.** Nearly always on the web: stacked full-width bands, fluid widths,
  flat fills, text-led navigation; spend novelty on content, not on fighting scrolling.

### 1.6 Show all navigation as plain words; "make it look like what it is"

- **What.** On his own site he kept a 90s-frames-like vertical side column of links
  rather than hide navigation behind a hamburger: "there's like seven things here and
  let's just put seven words up on the screen". On mobile he keeps navigation always
  visible. "Make it look like what it is." [stated: RWD Podcast] The current site (seen
  while viewing talk pages) still does this: a dark green-grey left half with labelled
  links, each with a grey one-line description. [observed]
- **Replaces.** Collapsed menus and icon navigation on small sites.

### 1.7 Settle hierarchy on the narrow screen first, then let wider layouts inherit

- **What.** He starts on mobile not to strip things out but because it is "an easy
  problem to begin with": choose element order, stack them, then decide hierarchy by
  colour, typeface and type size, and how links look. "If your hierarchy works there,"
  wider layouts inherit it and only then put things side by side. [stated: RWD Podcast]
- **When it fits.** Any responsive page: make the single-column version carry the whole
  hierarchy without layout help.

### 1.8 Draw the content lock-up first, then the box around it

- **What.** Do not start by drawing a page rectangle and filling it. Compose groups of
  elements (his example: Kasper Laigaard's content lock-ups for Hello Monday), then assign
  them to a space; responsive design is about how elements "build up", not how they break
  down. The web is "an edgeless surface of unknown proportions" assembled from small
  variable parts, like Hockney's photo joiners, against the Mona Lisa's fixed frame.
  [stated: The Web's Grain]
- He names the root problem of responsive layout: an image beside text grows taller as
  it widens while the text grows shorter; it is unsolvable, so manage it. [stated]
- **When it fits.** Component-led layouts: design lock-ups (image + headline + meta) as
  units, then decide how many sit side by side at each width.

### 1.9 Design for the most limiting medium first; hierarchy without size changes

- **What.** For The Shape of Design (print, ebook, web) he set the whole book in one
  typeface at one size, making hierarchy with negative space and capitals, because
  e-readers rescale text and would blow up headlines. The face, Quadraat, was chosen for
  the worst environment (screen legibility, hinting, webfont licence). The line-etching
  illustration style was chosen for how it looked on Kindle e-ink; the starburst cover
  was designed to read at app-thumbnail size and became the merchandise brand. "Start
  with the most limiting factors, develop some rules, then see how those rules applied."
  "Everything all at once, everything all together." [stated: Borderlands]
- **Precedent he cites.** Richard Hollis setting *Ways of Seeing* in bold Univers so text
  carries the same colour and weight as the images, and starting the text on the cover;
  Quentin Fiore's *The Medium is the Massage* braiding text and image. [stated]
- **When it fits.** Systems that must survive user font sizes, many formats or tiny
  thumbnails: one size, hierarchy by space, case and position.

### 1.10 Change the design, not the toolchain; let one direct asset do the work

- **What.** "Instead of changing my tools or workflow, I change my design," like
  designing a house that is easy to build instead of bringing skyscraper cranes.
  "Directness is best": "a great photo, memorable illustration, or pitch-perfect sentence
  does most of the work." His philosophy: "no razzle-dazzle"; find "the one or two
  uniquely true things" about the client and express them "with a light touch". His
  stated technical needs: simple responsive layout, web fonts and nicely set text,
  performant images. [stated: Everything Easy]
- He treats each asset as a cost: he tested his site with webfonts removed (Fakt and
  Arnhem falling back to San Francisco and Georgia, sized alike) and liked it "nearly as
  much". [stated: RWD Podcast]
- **When it fits.** Small and editorial sites: one strong image or sentence per view,
  system-font fallbacks sized to match, no effects that need build tooling.

### 1.11 Motion that explains, and design the in-between states

- **What.** "Medium flux": assistive animation that clarifies a change (OS X genie,
  animated scroll to an anchor, stateful toggles, sticky labels, comment fields revealed
  with motion). "Designing the way elements adapt and morph in the in-between moments is
  half of your work." Borrow words for behaviours from animation, theatre, puppetry and
  choreography so a word can replace a prototype. He judges full-screen "high flux"
  rarely right on the web. [stated: What Screens Want]
- In practice he prototyped NPR's persistent player transitions in Keynote Magic Move,
  later a little markup and JavaScript. [stated: RWD Podcast]
- **When it fits.** Motion only where it shows where something came from or went.

### 1.12 Clarity over simplicity; show the seams

- **What.** "Design's golden calf is simplicity... Simple things are weak." "Give me clear
  and evident things over simple things." "Shun seamlessness... Make me things that are
  full of seams." [stated: Only Openings] "Convergence and simplicity make for poor goals.
  Instead, we must aim for clarity." [stated: The Web's Grain]
- Legible source is a design value: view-source taught him the web; illegible generated
  markup "pulls up the ladder". [stated: Everything Easy]
- **When it fits.** Interfaces for capable readers: expose structure (labels, sources,
  how parts connect) rather than hiding it.

### 1.13 Put unlike things side by side

- **What.** He calls his tone "sprawling": "taking two things that seemingly don't have
  anything to do with each other and discovering a way to bring them together." [stated:
  Design Matters] The talks do it constantly: aspirin and GUIs, Hockney and responsive
  design, Yellowstone bears and design practice, Spirited Away and AI. [stated]
- **When it fits.** Openers, section breaks and image choices: pair the subject with an
  unexpected outside reference rather than a stock image of the subject.

### 1.14 Rough the edges so it does not look computer-made (illustration era)

- **What.** In his 2009 States illustrations, flat vector shapes "look computerish", so
  he adds scanned textures (surfaces, photocopies, collected paper) and redraws edges with
  a fine Sharpie on cheap copy paper so they bleed, then traces them back in. He swaps
  palettes late for warm/cool contrast and adds "lines, the dots, the seams" as "spice".
  [stated: Grain Edit] He later left illustration; treat this as early taste. [stated: TGD]

## 2. Process and thinking

- **Building a talk.** A wall of index cards, inkjet printouts and quotes held with blue
  painter's tape, sorted into groups until meaning emerges. Then a Keynote "fake deck" as
  a visual outline: drop in images and quotes he is drawn to, push slides into groups, add
  "BUT" and "THEREFORE" slides to make a story; reuse unused slides from earlier talks.
  Then write two or three paragraphs per slide into presenter notes and read them aloud in
  presenter mode. The result becomes the talk and then the web page. [stated: On Margins;
  Back to the Cave post] He calls writing a talk his "most desperate state" and gives
  talks rarely because "they are a lot of work". [stated: Back to the Cave post; Extras]
- **Talk to book.** The Shape of Design began as a 40-minute Build talk; transcribed and
  typeset it came to 27 pages, and finding "the form of the book" (pacing, segues) took
  the time. Early drafts alternated story chapters with theory chapters (contrapuntal);
  editing with Mandy Brown integrated them and made the tone more austere. [stated: On
  Margins]
- **Questions first.** Well-phrased questions are "heat-seeking missiles for clarity";
  he wants "a lot of clarity at 10,000 feet, and a lot of clarity at two feet. Everything
  in between becomes the opportunity." [stated: Design Matters]
- **Pencil piles.** Favourite medium: a wooden pencil and loose copier paper, each sheet
  numbered and circled so the sequence of ideas is visible; good ideas circled, bad ones
  crumpled; the stack is thrown out at the end of a job. [stated: Design Matters]
- **From canvas to markup fast.** Sketch (or Illustrator, "it doesn't necessarily
  matter") only to see colours side by side and roll through typefaces; once structure,
  faces, relative sizes and hierarchy are set, he moves to markup. Prototyping tools are
  worth the first quarter of the work; after that the real thing is the sketch. [stated:
  RWD Podcast] In 2025: "I just type and typeset"; Figma, his fonts and Jekyll. [stated:
  People and Blogs]
- **Designer as clarifier and specifier.** Responsive work is "sets of rules", not
  artefacts, so his job is to find the project's "dark corners", decide where it is fine
  to be inconsistent, and go be "the emissary for that ill definition". [stated: RWD
  Podcast]
- **Design as articulation.** His third definition after decoration and construction:
  describe the problem as accurately as possible, wait for its "grain" to unfold, and
  make the work "ever more definite, nuanced, and clear". "Can you write a design?"
  [stated: Borderlands; Format]
- **Wolves or bears.** Ask whether a brief means eliminating the problem or managing it;
  he prefers the slow maintenance approach, being "in dialogue with the problem". [stated:
  Only Openings]
- **Conflicting assumptions.** When his and a client's assumptions clash, test them;
  "almost every single time we are both wrong." [stated: Design Matters]
- **Crutch check.** He knows it is time to change when the work leans on "a crutch in the
  way that things look, or a crutch in the way that I'm thinking". [stated: Format]
- **Own site as lab.** He redesigns his site every year to try new things. [stated: RWD
  Podcast]
- **Writing against the machine.** For Beyond the Machine he had ChatGPT draft sections
  "in the style of Frank Chimero", then "bucked hard against the output, typically
  starting from scratch". He writes bad and contradictory prompts on purpose for creative
  work, treating prompts like Oblique Strategies. [stated: Extras; Beyond the Machine]
- **Best at the start.** He is "happiest and most useful at the start of things", hence
  branding work. [stated: Creative Boom]

## 3. Taste

- **Influences named.** Alan Fletcher and *The Art of Looking Sideways* as his biggest
  influence ("No one looked more alive on the page"); Rand, Bass, Lustig, Flora as
  "stylistic influences" [stated: Grain Edit]; Saul Bass, Alvin Lustig, Paul Rand and the
  Eameses as generalist heroes [stated: Borderlands]; his professor Roman Duszek [stated:
  TGD]; John Baldessari's text-and-image work [stated: TGD]; Richard Hollis, Quentin Fiore
  and Jerome Agel's 1960s visual paperbacks [stated: Borderlands]; Hockney's joiners,
  Muybridge [stated]; Italo Calvino's *Six Memos* [stated]; Brian Eno, Holly Herndon and
  Mat Dryhurst, Miyazaki [stated: Beyond the Machine].
- **Visual register of his slides.** Black fields, black-and-white photography,
  typewriter-like caps, one accent (orange rules), film stills; the current site behind
  them is monospaced type on pale grey beside a dark green-grey column. [observed]
- **Praises.** Work that goes with its material; "a technology of grace, one that lives
  well within its role" [stated: The Web's Grain]; resonance over reach [stated: TGD];
  the "weird, ugly" signature of a medium's limits (quoting Eno) [stated: Beyond the
  Machine]; "The simple things are worth doing well because they happen every day"
  [stated: Creative Boom].
- **Dislikes.** "Whizzbangery" marketing sites that repeat Flash-era usability mistakes
  [stated: RWD Podcast]; bicycle-bear websites; the flat vs skeuomorphic argument as
  shallow because it is about looks [stated: What Screens Want]; spaghetti toolchains and
  illegible generated source [stated: Everything Easy]; seamlessness and simplicity as
  goals [stated: Only Openings]; "world changey-designy" braggadocio [stated: TGD]; the
  homogenous web of 2013 [stated: TGD]; the averaging pull of AI output [stated: Beyond
  the Machine].
