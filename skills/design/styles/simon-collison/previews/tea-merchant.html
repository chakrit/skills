who weigh it by the hundred grams, wrap it in brown paper and will happily argue about the water

Pask & Daughter has sold loose tea from the same narrow shop on Goose Gate since 1898, when Josiah Pask came home from the Liverpool auctions with more Assam than his wife thought sensible. The shop is kept now by his great-great-granddaughter, Ruth Pask, who weighs every order on his brass scales and writes the labels by hand.1

There are thirty-six teas this winter. Nine of them are printed below with their prices; the rest are on the counter, in the black tins with the cream labels. Come in and smell them before you choose. Most people do, and the tins are used to it.

Brewing, Roughly

which most customers ignore, and the tea forgives them

Use a teaspoon of leaf for each cup and one for the pot, as your grandmother said. Warm the pot first. Black teas want water just off the boil; greens want it cooler, about the heat at which you can hold a finger in it for a second.2 Taste at the shortest time and go longer if you like it stronger.

If it comes out bitter, use less leaf or less time before you blame the tea. If it comes out weak, bring the tin back and Ruth will weigh you a different one.

The Shop’s Life

a hundred and twenty-eight years on one street, in six entries

The ledgers go back to the first week. They are kept in the back room in a biscuit tin, and the dates below come from them.

  1. 1898

    Josiah Pask, grocer’s clerk, rents 14 Goose Gate and sells nothing but tea.

  2. 1931

    Goose Gate Breakfast first appears in the ledger, at a shilling and fourpence a pound.

  3. 1941

    The May raids break the front window. The tea is in the cellar and comes through dry.

  4. 1963

    First flush Darjeeling arrives by air for the first time, and costs more than the rent.

  5. 1987

    Ann Pask takes over from her father and repaints the sign.

    It has said “& Daughter” ever since.

  6. 2019

    The brass scales are sent to Sheffield to be checked and come back true to the gram.
