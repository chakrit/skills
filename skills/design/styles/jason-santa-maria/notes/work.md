## 1. Moves

### Move 1. One fixed frame, one variable per instalment

What: keep layout, type and logo constant across a series and change exactly one visible
variable per issue, book or visit: the palette. In ALA 4.0 each issue got its own small
CSS file (`/css/204.css`, `/css/210.css`) that only recolours links, the author rule and
headings (issue 204 burnt orange `#d9560f` with olive `#4a4902`; issue 210 dusty rose
`#c07796` with ink `#403f55`), while `base.css` never changes [observed]. He said the
base palette is "neutral and warm" and each issue sports its own colours, giving the
magazine "a brand new face with each issue" [stated, v3 ALA post]. Seen 2026-11-23:
issue 201 (Aug 2005) sets its centred Georgia titles in burnt orange-brown with orange
bylines; issue 209 (Dec 2005) uses deep maroon titles with olive bylines; logo panel,
nav, dashed rules and columns are identical. The issue number sits in a black serrated
seal ("No. 201") overlapping the corner of the cream logo panel, like a wax or notary
stamp. [observed (seen)] Legacy articles share
one grey scheme to mark then versus now [reported, Zeldman ALA 4.0 article]. He carried
the same device to A Book Apart: "the color palette change with each edition … I wanted
these to look like a family on your bookshelf" [stated, ABA announcement]; ABA later
moved to five-colour printing so each book's "signature hue" is a specified Pantone
[reported, ABA press post]. Loud Room (2026) pushes it to the extreme: a random
foreground/background pair per page load, regenerated until it passes WCAG AA 4.5:1,
shareable through `?bg=&fg=` [observed, colors.js]; he loves "the bit of chaos when a
garish palette shows up" [stated, Meet Loud Room].

Replaces: one brand colour applied everywhere forever, or a fresh design per item that
loses the family.

Fits: any serial content (issues, episodes, books, case studies, product drops) where
recognition and freshness must both hold. Keep everything structural fixed; let colour
carry the "this one is new" signal. Constrain the variable (contrast floor, warm/neutral
base) so randomness never breaks reading.

### Move 2. Navigation written as a sentence

What: the primary navigation of the 2007 Happy Cog site is a sentence in an `h1`: "Happy
Cog in New York City and Philadelphia: We design beautiful websites, publish our best
ideas, and speak to a worldwide community", with the verbs' objects as uppercase rust
links (`#c93b0e`, 1px underline) [observed, markup and screen.css]. Correction (seen
2026-11-23): the links are the verbs themselves, DESIGN, PUBLISH and SPEAK, plus a
closing "Find out more ABOUT us"; "in New York City and Philadelphia" shows as small
italic text beside the logo, not in the big sentence. The sentence is set large in
Georgia across the full width, over the watercolour wash, directly under the logo.
[observed (seen)] He called it "a bit
unconventional—and perhaps my favorite part of the site—serving as a strong masthead,
mission statement, and flavor copy" [stated]. Zeldman's reason: labels "separate"; the
three businesses had to read as one, so "the primary navigation interface had to be a
sentence" [reported].

Replaces: a row of nouns (Work, About, Contact).

Fits: small sites whose sections are facets of one proposition (studio, person,
product). Needs short, true copy; fails when sections grow past three or four.

### Move 3. Let the page be quiet; no focal hook

What: for ALA 4.0 he deliberately gave the homepage "no overt focus": logo, latest
articles, "Nothing screams because it doesn't need to"; he also suppressed visited-link
states so it felt "more than a website", like "a book you've been waiting to pore over"
[stated, Stuff and Nonsense interview]. CSS backs it: grey `#333` Verdana at 13px with
1.8em leading, centred Georgia titles with an ornamental byline separator, dashed
hairline dividers, diamond bullets [observed]. Loud Room 2026 is the same stance at a
different volume: no portfolio grid, just one huge thin headline and a few paragraphs,
and he values "the small amount of friction in asking someone to read a few paragraphs"
[stated, observed].

Replaces: the hero with a call to action and a card row designed to convert in seconds.

Fits: editorial and reference sites, studios, anything whose value is in reading.
Readers who want three-second answers are explicitly not the audience.

### Move 4. Type pairing: a workhorse serif for reading, a characterful display voice

Across projects the reading face is a calm, often old-style serif and the display voice
is something with strong personality, frequently condensed, light, or script:

- ALA 2005: Verdana text, Georgia display, Garamond logo [stated, observed].
- A Book Apart 2010: Yoga (text) + Yoga Sans (captions, quotes) + Titling Gothic Regular
  Skyline for covers and heads, chosen because its condensation lets long titles stay
  large on a narrow page; Consolas after print tests [stated].
- The Morning News 2011: Minion 17/23 body, Facit at weight 200 for 62px headlines and
  33px pull quotes [observed].
- Editorially 2013: Adelle text, Facit UI [observed].
- AEA 2013: Freight Sans text with tiny Pacifico script headers [observed].
- Slate 2018: Retina (text) + Register (heads), both chosen for "delightful
  idiosyncrasy … economical in their proportions" [stated].
- The Type Founders 2026: ATF Super Condensed caps, Meno Display/Text, Grtsk utility
  [reported, observed].
- Loud Room 2026: HEX Franklin Narrow Thin/Light with VCTR Mono italic for navigation
  [observed].

Zeldman's line on him: "What the Ramones did with three chords, he does with two system
fonts" [reported]. He picks faces for the job's real content, not taste alone: ABA needed
OpenType numerals and good code and caption faces [stated].

Replaces: one geometric sans for everything.

Fits: always pick the text face first for the reading conditions, then a display face
with a stated reason (width economy, period voice, personality). Light or condensed
display at very large size is a recurring signature.

### Move 5. Extreme scale contrast between display and text

What: headlines or display words at many times body size, often thin or condensed, so
the scale jump itself is the composition. Loud Room: h1 `clamp(54px … 150px)` in HEX
Franklin Narrow Thin, weight 100, letter-spacing −0.3rem, against 20–40px body
[observed]. TMN: 62px weight-200 headline against 17px Minion [observed]. Type Founders:
uppercase condensed headings at 5rem with line-height 0.9, an "about" heading at
`min(12rem, 40vw)` and line-height 0.8 [observed; seen 2026-11-23: "PROTECTING.
GROWING." stacked in black super-condensed caps against a calm serif paragraph, under a
hero collage of huge cropped letters from member typefaces in overlapping blue, purple,
green, yellow and red, a green italic Meno headline, and round badges with text set on a
circle]. Loud Room seen 2026-11-23: the thin headline spans the grid while the
paragraph starts about a third in; the logo stacks LOUD ROOM in letters built from
horizontal pill-shaped bars. [observed (seen)] Megazoid: display sizes up to
`clamp(3rem, 14vw, 30rem)` and `80vw` [observed]. ABA: condensed Titling Gothic so cover
titles stay large [stated].

Replaces: a gentle 1.25 modular scale where nothing is really big.

Fits: covers, mastheads, landing statements. Use a light or condensed cut so huge size
does not become heavy; keep body text large and generous too (Loud Room body grows to
40px).

### Move 6. One image or one artefact sets the palette

What: the Happy Cog 2007 site is "a very simple layout, topped by a vibrant visual of a
watercolor wash … to set the palette and lead visitors through the site" [stated]; the
CSS colours (cream `#fffdee`, browns `#2b1404`/`#4e3114`, olive `#40550d`, rust
`#c93b0e`, tan `#963`) read as sampled from a painting [observed]. Seen 2026-11-23: the
wash is loose blotches of olive green, orange and red on cream, with a deckled, torn
bottom edge; the rust links and brown bar below do pick up its colours. The logo is a
cog roundel with a heavy rounded slab-serif lowercase wordmark. [observed (seen)]
Zeldman: "The painting at the top … makes the design" [reported]. Megazoid:
colours drawn from VHS, cassette, Polaroid and Trapper Keeper packaging that go "right up
to the line of 'clashing' while still feeling energetic" [stated]. Citywide: the whole
typeface grows from one flea-market roll sign on his wall [stated].

Replaces: a palette picked from a UI kit.

Fits: when there is a commissioned illustration, a photograph, or a period reference,
sample the palette from it and keep the layout simple enough to let it lead.

### Move 7. Print cues carried into the web frame

What: ALA 4.0 "takes some cues from traditional letterpress printing" with a strong,
wider grid and "healthy whitespace" [stated]. Evidence in CSS: Garamond logo on a laurel,
a numbered issue stamp like a limited edition, centred serif titles over an ornamental
separator, small-caps-like Verdana labels letterspaced 0.33em, an ISSN line in the footer,
diamond bullets [observed]. AIGA: "lovingly set typography (like the drop caps)" and the
grid used to art direct each article [stated]. TMN: pull quotes hang 130px into the
margin, left or right, in red [observed]. Slate: overlapping and piled materials and
marginalia to show "the editor's hand" [stated]. Seen 2026-11-23 on the 2018-02 home
page: a near-black aubergine masthead band with the white SLATE wordmark; the lead
headline in a maroon heavy serif, others in a bold sans; an "ALSO" label in pink italic
with a hand-drawn curling arrow; a podcast promo built as a collage of torn, overlapping
paper with pink scribbles, on a diagonally hatched band. [observed (seen)] ABA: title
wraps from spine onto the back for texture [stated]. Seen 2026-11-23 on the 2016 ABA
site and his current home page: each cover is one flat hue (red, orange, coral) with a
numbered circle, black on the main series, breaking the top edge on the Briefs, and the
title in condensed caps over a short rule and the author's name; the family holds by
layout, the hue names the book. [observed (seen)]

Replaces: flat UI cards and centred text blocks with no reference to publishing.

Fits: editorial work. Use the specific devices of the medium (issue numbers, drop caps,
marginal quotes, colophons, running labels) rather than vague "print feel". He now also
says CSS "finally gave the web its own grain" and the screen is a medium with its own
affordances, not a translation of print [stated, 2026]; so the print devices are sources,
not costumes.

### Move 8. Offset the text column on a coarse grid

What: Loud Room uses a 6-column grid (75rem max, 1.25rem gutter); the logo spans columns
1–2, the nav sits alone in column 6, the h1 spans all six, and every paragraph starts at
column 3 and runs to 6, leaving a deliberate empty left third [observed]. The footer
repeats the 2|4 split with a single top rule [observed]. ALA 2005 used a 210px left
offset for the main column under the logo panel and a fixed 750px body with comfortable
measures [observed]; he changed ALA from fluid to fixed after the first design rounds
[stated].

Replaces: a centred single column, or text spanning full width.

Fits: statement pages and articles. Hanging the text off-centre gives white space a job
(it frames the big headline that does span the grid).

### Move 9. Randomness inside rules

What: controlled variation per visit. AIGA 2007: a new masthead each visit, pulled at
random from the AIGA Design Archives [stated]. Seen 2026-11-23: the home page showed a
cropped orange Push Pin Group piece and the article page a red Phaidon cover, each a
full-width band with a small caption box naming the archive item; the article page has
a large grey drop cap, a bigger lead paragraph, and a red tab marking the current
section. [observed (seen)] Loud Room: random colour pair with a contrast floor
[observed, stated; seen 2026-11-23: two reloads gave peach on royal blue, then blue on
lime]. Megazoid: "Almost everything on the site reacts to
hover or touch — letters bounce, shift, and extrude", using the font's own variable
depth and stroke axes as the interaction [stated, observed `font-variation-settings:
"DPTH" -300, "STRK" 40` with 0.2–0.3s transitions].

Replaces: a static hero.

Fits: when the content has a deep archive or the product is itself expressive (fonts,
art). Always bound the randomness (archive curation, contrast check).

### Move 10. Start small, then spread the system

What: at Slate he began with one article design published outside the CMS, iterated in
public, then took the lessons to the brand, homepage, podcasts and social; he named the
project "Redux" rather than "redesign" and called it "a Trojan horse" for changing how
editorial, design, development, product and sales worked together [stated]. At WordPress
he designed a handful of key pages and the Automattic team extrapolated the rest
[stated]. At ALA one visual system also covered AEA and ABA [stated, reported].

Replaces: the big-bang redesign of every template.

Fits: large organisations and design systems. Design the highest-value template deeply,
then derive.

## 2. Process and thinking

- Sketch first, always: designers "need to draw to understand how they think"; a
  sketchbook is "the most useful visual thinking tool" [stated, TGD].
- Revise rather than create: from Anne Lamott's "Shitty First Drafts" he took the belief
  that "it's often much easier to revise than to create" [stated, TGD]. Citywide's
  lowercase spent "a long time on the wrong side" of the line between harmony and
  staleness before it clicked [stated].
- Full comps, then the browser: for ALA he made PSDs for most pages, "obsessive … making
  sure things are even and on their grid", because a departure has to be sold with flat
  comps; hover and link behaviour he found by "playing with live XHTML and CSS" [stated].
- Treat each kind of content specially: ALA had "many varieties of information" and he
  did not want "to be hindered by the strict system of templates" [stated].
- Ego-light collaboration: Zeldman's first job for him was finishing another designer's
  style, which he did "egoless" [reported]. He credits every collaborator by role in
  launch posts (CSS, CMS, IA, editorial direction) [observed across posts].
- Choose type for the content's real needs, then test in the target medium: ABA code
  fonts were print-tested because some monospaces "look great on screen but fail on the
  printed page" [stated].
- Define the client before designing: at Slate, "Slate is a guide and companion …
  reliable and bullshit-free … curiosity, wit"; the visual direction then visualises "the
  story-making process" [stated].
- Gather references with the client: Megazoid began by collecting inspiration both
  liked, then iterating [stated]. Specimen-site brief: show what the font can do, show
  what it feels like in use, have fun; a playful face gets a playful site [stated].
- He prefers being his own client so he can keep iterating after launch [stated, TGD],
  and prefers hands-on making to managing at "low altitude over the work" [stated,
  Funsizing].

## 3. Taste

- Books, magazines, letterpress, found vernacular type (bus roll signs, Play-Doh
  extruders, Atari catalogues, VHS packaging) [stated].
- Warm neutrals with one confident accent: cream, tan and brown with rust (Happy Cog);
  warm off-white `#f7f6f1` with signal red (AEA); maroon deepened with bright pink,
