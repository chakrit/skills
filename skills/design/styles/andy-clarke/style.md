# Andy Clarke: concept-led art direction

Design a site so that it belongs to one business and could not be mistaken for another.
Distilled from Andy Clarke's (Stuff & Nonsense) freely published work: his blog,
articles, talks, interviews, the free sample of *Standout Designs for the Web*, his
published client work, and other people's accounts of working with him. Written in our
own words; no material from his paid books.

- **Feels like:** a magazine page, a poster or a shop sign made for this one business.
  Bold display type, few colours in unequal amounts, asymmetric grids with deliberate
  empty space, bespoke illustration instead of stock.
- **Fits:** small businesses, personal sites, campaigns, editorial pages: anything with
  a story or an owner with a personality.

## The default to beat

A site generated or templated without thought is assembled from known parts: a centred
photo hero with a headline and a button, a row of three equal feature cards, a stats strip
of four tiles, testimonials, a CTA band, all on one evenly divided grid with the same
rhythm in every section and stock or generated imagery. Each part is reasonable. Together
they carry no idea, so the site says nothing about the business.

Familiar patterns are not banned. A twelve-column grid, a hero or a row of cards is fine
when chosen on purpose for this business. A pattern chosen only because it is familiar is
the failure. Unusual moves without an idea behind them read as random and become a new
cliché.

## Every site has one concept

A concept is one idea, specific to this business, that the design carries: in its
imagery, type, colour, composition and a few interface details. Find it before any layout.

### Where concepts come from

Look in these five places, in one intake conversation with the owner:

| Source                                   | Ask                                                           | Example                                                            |
|------------------------------------------|---------------------------------------------------------------|--------------------------------------------------------------------|
| What the business already owns           | Which logo, sign, card, packaging, place names do you have?   | A council's thirteen village names, stacked, become the hero       |
| What the owner keeps returning to        | What do you love, collect, read, keep mentioning?             | A composer's tape machines become the site's audio players         |
| What customers really buy                | What do people buy from you, one level above the product?     | An investment firm sells confidence, so no buildings are shown     |
| The trade's mechanics, taken literally   | What does your product physically do? Say your line literally | Printhead supplier: famous paintings rebuilt from printed QR codes |
| A field that already made people feel it | Who else made people feel what you need them to feel?         | Youth politics borrows protest and gig-poster language             |

- The concept is what the owner keeps coming back to, not what the designer likes.
- Study competitors only to list their clichés, then avoid them.
- Mundane trades make good briefs: every business has a story worth telling.
- A brief states goals, problems and hopes, never solutions. Treat a "sites we like" list
  as context, not as a target.

### Pinning the concept down

1. **One line.** List true things about the business, keep the single one that is
   theirs alone and hard to argue with, and state it in one line.
2. **Three or four single words** for how the site should feel. Each word must turn into
   concrete choices.
3. **One test question** every later decision must pass, taken from what customers really
   buy: "Does this make them feel they are in safe hands?"
4. **What not to show.** The concept also rules imagery out: no building photos for a firm
   that sells trust.
5. **Reassuring or energetic.** Symmetry, even columns and a shallow type hierarchy read
   as stable and calm. Asymmetry, odd column counts and a steep hierarchy read as energetic
   and opinionated. Choose on purpose for this business; a site may move between the two
   from section to section as its story does.

### Borrowing a reference

- Pick **one** outside reference per design (a poster, a magazine page, a record sleeve,
  a comic, a film title card, a shop sign, a printed menu) whose main device suits the
  concept. Do not blend several in one design.
- Name the reference's two to four distinctive threads (a structure, a type treatment, a
  palette, an image habit) and translate each into one web move.
- Translate qualities, not surfaces: take a source's confidence, economy or noise, not its
  exact shapes. Never reproduce a source's artwork.
- Look first at things the customers already know: local film and concert posters,
  market and shop signage, fair posters, packaging, comics, the shop's own printed menu.
- The page can become an object from the customer's world: a menu card, a magazine
  cover, a shop sign.

## Order of work

1. Intake conversation and content: gather and rewrite the content, then storyboard what a
   visitor discovers, in what order.
2. Concept, line, feeling words, test question, reference.
3. Type first: shortlist faces for how they feel, test them with the business's name,
   numerals and every language the site uses, then choose. Avoid faces chosen only
   because they are everywhere.
4. Palette with roles: one dominant, one supporting, one accent, plus a dark that is not
   pure black and a light that is not pure white. Use them in clearly unequal amounts.
   Sample colours from the business's own things.
5. Compose in greys, phone and desktop side by side, before any colour or decoration.
   Every agent design passes through this composition step before code: designing
   directly in code drifts to boxes.
6. Make the concept assets (see below), shown early and rough so the owner can react.
7. Build, then judge the result fresh and by feeling ("does this feel like us?").

## Where the art direction goes

Concentrate it; do not spread it evenly.

- **The first screen** carries the concept at full strength.
- **One or two signature controls** are dressed in the concept's materials (the menu, the
  order button, the audio player, the form) while still looking clickable.
- **Section headings, numerals and practical facts** carry the type treatment.
- **Other sections** stay calm and consistent. Long pages vary composition by the job of
  each section: a large, slow composition for the main promise; small, quick modules for
  lists; calm even columns for reassurance and the close.
- One thread (palette, type family, grid, illustration style, character) ties every page
  together. Pages may each look different; they must feel connected.
- When everything is loud, nothing is heard. Some pages need only good typography.

## Concept assets are the product

The concept lives in bespoke assets, not in the grid. A site stripped of them falls
straight back to the default. For every site, make and keep these as reusable files:

- the mark or a mark extension (a motif built from the logo's own geometry);
- an illustration set or image treatment in one style (drawn motif, duotone photos,
  cut-outs with a consistent outline);
- display lockups (the name, the line, key numbers);
- the palette with roles.

Prefer imagery that is drawn from the business's own logo, products, place or people over
stock or generic illustration. Owner photos treated in the palette (duotone, cut-out) are
a cheap alternative to illustration.

## Moves

Each move replaces a default. Use one only when it serves the concept.

### Grid and composition

| Move                                                                                                   | Replaces                                           |
|--------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------|----------------------------------------------------|
| Leave whole columns or a quarter of the page empty on an even grid                                     | every track filled, content centred                |
| Add one odd track: a narrow column, a headline column, margin tracks in the grid                       | blocks spanning 1–4 of 12 equal columns            |
| Compound grid: overlay two column counts (3+4, 4+5, 4+6) and use every line                            | one equal grid, for pages with many element widths |
| Column widths grown by a ratio (√2, golden, double square)                                             | guessed splits like 8 and 4                        |
| Staircase starts: headline, standfirst and body on different grid lines                                | one flush left edge                                |
| Images and text on different column counts, so their gutters don't line up                             | image and text sharing gutters                     |
| Modular grid with zones sized by importance, the largest zone used once, some modules empty            | a uniform card grid                                |
| Main column plus a narrow utility rail (hours, phone, services)                                        | three equal cards                                  |
| One large structural shape instead of a hero: a colour slab, a band, a horizon, a giant letter or mark | photo hero with a centred headline                 |
| The key message or proof in the hero space, or image and message fused into one composition            | a full-width banner as filler                      |
| Text whose edge follows the picture's outline                                                          | an image box beside a text rectangle               |
| Rotate a whole group, never single random items; diagonals from empty modules                          | everything orthogonal                              |
| Bleed off the edge and crop until a detail turns abstract; show the whole elsewhere                    | the whole product centred with padding             |
| Overlap: image and text in one cell, cut-outs in front of type, elements breaking out of the grid      | flat side-by-side cells                            |
| Mirror or invert two halves across a seam for two-part content                                         | alternating image-left, image-right rows           |
| Visual order differs from document order when it helps; header and footer need not sit top and bottom  | fixed top-to-bottom template                       |
| Large, borderless, edge-bleeding panels for the moment that matters; small boxed ones for the rest     | panels sized by content amount                     |

### Type

| Move                                                                                            | Replaces                        |
|-------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------|---------------------------------|
| Display type as the image: stacked, width-fitted lines, mixed weights, words boxed or rotated   | one centred H1 and a subtitle   |
| Practical facts as display type: opening hours, the phone number, the next date, the price      | small grey utility text         |
| Giant numerals with a small, lighter unit                                                       | a stats strip of icon tiles     |
| The navigation set as part of the hero lockup                                                   | a separate generic nav bar      |
| A different lockup for each parallel item (badge numeral, script phrase, condensed stack)       | identical icon-title-text cards |
| Captions beside the image, aligned to its bottom, or as a composed block; rules above and below | grey caption under the photo    |
| Magazine anatomy: running head, standfirst, lede, pull quote, drop cap                          | hero, features, CTA slots only  |

### Colour, image and motion

| Move                                                                                                 | Replaces                      |
|------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------|-------------------------------|
| Three hues in unequal amounts; one accent that links a key word to the image                         | an even multi-colour palette  |
| Overprint: flat shapes overlap to make a third colour (needs drawn subjects)                         | drop shadows and glassy cards |
| Extreme crops and growing image sequences to tell the story                                          | medium photos in cards        |
| A character or illustrated world for personality-led businesses, reusable and re-dressable by season | generic illustration          |
| Slow, looping, real-world ambient motion; reduce anything that draws the eye; respect reduced motion | motion as decoration          |
| A small hidden reward (alternate theme, Easter egg), never on the main path                          | nothing to discover           |

## Phones

Most visitors to a small business's site see it on a phone, so the phone version is
designed, not derived.

- Decide which signature element must survive on a phone and build it in a phone-native
  form. Letting it disappear at a breakpoint is the failure case.
- Ways to keep it: a horizontal scrolling strip instead of shrinking a row; a different
  crop or drawing for the phone, never a scaled copy; keep the ratio of scale contrast,
  not the sizes (size against the viewport); keep a small token of a device that cannot
  fit (a band of the palette, one shape behind each block); recompose type objects (a
  tower of names becomes two columns).
- Scale contrast can grow on the phone: a side column of display type becomes a
  full-width display block.
- A phone layout may keep columns, indents and empty space; it need not be one column.
- Set breakpoints where the content breaks, not at device widths. Secondary ornaments may
  go; the concept may not.

## Familiar mechanics stay familiar

Pricing tables, buttons, forms, navigation behaviour and calls to action keep their
expected behaviour and look clickable. Reinvent what faces the brand; leave the mechanics
people rely on.

## Thai script

- Thai has no capitals and few condensed widths: get display contrast from weight, scale,
  tight leading and colour splits instead of condensed capitals.
- Do not set Thai vertically. Vertical type is limited to Latin numerals, dates and short
  Latin words.
- Thai has no spaces between words: text wrapped around shapes is set ragged, never
  justified.
- Giant numerals (prices, years, counts) work in any script.
- Choose display faces that have enough Thai weights for the type moves the concept
  needs.
