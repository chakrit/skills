Hollin Hill Observatory is a white dome on the moor above Hollin with a 40 cm telescope inside, looked after by twenty-six volunteers. Twice a month we open it on a Friday evening, and anyone who books can climb the hill and look through it for nothing.

Most people who come have never used a telescope. A volunteer stands at the eyepiece with you and tells you what you are seeing.

Open nights this winter

Most nights have two sittings, at 18:30 and 20:00, because the dome holds twenty people standing. Booking is free and closes at noon on the day.

  1. Albireo, the gold and blue double

    Friday 4 December · 18:30 and 20:00 · 23 of 40 places left

    Look at the swan's head in Cygnus through our telescope and one star becomes two, gold beside blue, about 430 light years away. Cygnus sinks into the west by nine, so come to the first sitting if you can.

  2. The Pleiades

    Friday 18 December · 17:30, 18:30 and 20:00 · 12 of 60 places left

    We have added a half-hour sitting at 17:30 for children under twelve and whoever brings them, since a cluster is the easiest thing to find in an eyepiece on a first try. Most people count six stars in the Pleiades by eye. Through the telescope there are dozens, blue-white and close together, all about 440 light years away, and a volunteer will name the brightest seven for you.

  3. The Moon at first quarter

    Friday 15 January · 18:30 and 20:00 · 9 of 40 places left

    At 384,000 km the Moon is near enough for the telescope to pick out single crater walls, and along the shadow line they cast long shadows that put the ground in relief.

  4. The Orion Nebula

    Friday 29 January · 18:30 and 20:00 · 38 of 40 places left

    In Orion's sword, about 1,340 light years from us, is a cloud of gas where new stars are forming. Four young stars at its centre, the Trapezium, light it from inside; on a steady night the telescope shows all four, and the gas looks grey-green to the eye.

If the sky is cloudy

At four o'clock on the day we decide whether the night will be spent at the telescope or indoors, and email everyone who booked.

An indoor night keeps every sitting listed for that night, the children's half hour included: a tour of the telescope and a talk in the warm room, finished by half past nine as on a clear night. If the cloud breaks during a sitting, we open the dome's shutter and go to the eyepiece.

Before you come

The dome is unheated, and the hill is usually four degrees colder than the town. Wear a hat.

Your eyes take about twenty minutes to adjust to the dark, so please keep phone screens off inside the dome; we lend red torches at the door. The last 300 metres from the car park on Moor Lane is a gravel track with a handrail, and there is one step up into the dome. Children under twelve come with an adult.

Book a place

Tell us which night, which sitting and how many of you are coming. If a night is full we keep a waiting list, and about one booking in five is cancelled by the day.
