# Simon Collison: a motive, a small kit, and a voice in every label

Design a site the way Simon Collison designs: find the motive, impose a tight kit, let
the type lead, and write every label in one voice until the whole site reads as one
object. Distilled from his own sites and journal, his case studies, his talk slides and
articles, and other people's accounts of his work. Written in our own words.

- **Feels like:** a well-kept miscellany, a newsletter on headed paper, a record-shop
  counter. Few colours with conviction, one ordinary text face set with care, a condensed
  display voice, footnotes and drop caps, copy that makes you smile, an honest flat
  surface.
- **Fits:** cultural businesses, events and conferences, personal and editorial sites:
  anything with a voice.
- **Fits poorly:** checkout flows. There his own rule is reduction and breathing room
  ("flexible but stern").

## The default he would refuse

A site that looks like every site in its trade, with labels nobody wrote, icons that
literally depict their section, texture faked to look old, effects added because the
browser can do them, and white space used because minimal is assumed to be clean. He
would rather divide opinion than be boring.

## 1. Find the motive

Before any layout, write in the brief which motive drives this design. Choose from his
list:

| Motive     | What it asks for                                               |
|------------|----------------------------------------------------------------|
| Nostalgia  | a period object                                                |
| Vernacular | the visual language the audience's culture already speaks     |
| Humour     | wry copy, an unexpected picture, a joke the reader has to find |
| Rhetoric   | a clear argument                                               |

Then name **the object**: one thing that the whole site will be. Look for it in period
print and ephemera (miscellanies, engravings, paperback covers, record sleeves, stickers,
newspapers) and in the culture the customers live in. One object per site; do not blend.

Throw ideas away freely. Several concepts should die before one survives. Provoke
yourself: what if the text was huge? What if it was less Swiss?

## 2. Impose the kit

Complete freedom is the worst start. Write the kit into the brief before designing, and
keep it this small:

- one grid;
- two typefaces;
- two colours, one grey, a near-black, a white;
- what the kit may generate: line patterns, solid blocks, tints.

Everything on the page comes from the kit. A season may branch off with its own colours;
the kit still does most of the work.

## 3. Let the type lead

Choose type before layout, for how it informs the whole feel.

- **The text face may be an ordinary one.** A tired, familiar face (Times) used with full
  craft shows skill through composition rather than through the choice of face. Make it
  "richly and superbly ordinary": careful sizes, real italics, small capitals for dates,
  spaced capitals for navigation. Bold only for drop caps.
- **The display face is condensed** and fitted to the business's own name: set the name
  in the candidates and keep the face that sits best with its letters. A narrow sans
  "doesn't feel too modern" next to a period text face; a wide one may fight the name.
- **An accent face may antiquate**: an italic, a Baskerville-style ampersand, a
  monospace.
- **Name and descriptor.** Set a name or title in condensed capitals and put an italic
  serif descriptor under it that carries the wit. The caps give order; the italic gives
  voice. The descriptor goes under the title, never above it as a label.
- Keep body text large. His v4 was criticised for small type; do not repeat it.

## 4. Write the copy before the layout

Finalise the words first. Every word on the site speaks in the object's voice: section
names, navigation, captions, the footer, the closed-comments notice. "Potted
Autobiography", not "About". "Exhaustive Archives", not "Blog". A colophon that says the
site is "kept alive with tea".

- The voice is wry, warm and specific: real place names, real objects, dates, the odd
  aside. Self-deprecating.
- Buttons and links stay plain enough to act on.
- Read every line against the bans' writing tells before it reaches the page. Wit that
  comes as a setup and an emphasised payoff is a banned pattern.

## 5. Juxtapose

Put one picture beside the type and see if the design clicks.

- **The irrelevant illustration.** One drawn creature per section, chosen to have no
  correlation with the section. The picture gives no clue, so the reader reads the
  caption, and the caption makes the joke. Draw them as single-colour line engravings.
- Or **hand-made marks** beside confident type: a pencil-drawn bird, a rough rule, a
  scribbled logotype, next to a very large, plain line of type.
- Or **vernacular parody**: stickers that riff on the audience's own culture (album
  sleeves for a music crowd).
- Unify any pictures by tinting them in the kit's colours, so they look like one family.

## 6. Furnish the page

Use several of these:

- a big drop cap in the accent colour at the start of each main text, or a § sign as the
  drop cap;
- catalogue numbers on entries, in a list column or a roundel beside the entry, never as
  a numbered label above a section heading;
- an aside column of italic metadata between dotted rules, with dates in small capitals;
- a double rule under a title;
- footnotes with return links;
- a masthead lockup: an italic line, a line of spaced capitals, the name in condensed
  capitals, each whole line with its own treatment;
- a footer lockup around one huge ampersand;
- for a history, **a timeline**: years on one rule, short fragments in narrow columns,
  small objects hung on stems, turning points marked by rotated labels.

## 7. Keep the surface honest

Truth to materials: the screen is a screen.

- No coffee rings or worn textures. An antique voice sits on a clean flat ground:
  off-white with a faint grid, or a calm dark tone.
- No gratuitous effects: no drop shadows, rotations for show, spinning buttons.
- Motion only where something would really move, along the path its cause gives it.
- Restraint is economy that gives immediate meaning. Do not assume a minimal page is
  clean; complexity can be elegant when it is organised. Trust readers to fill in the
  blanks.

## 8. Build the system in the browser

Design pages as parts of one system; the conceit then bends a rule.

- Sketch away from the screen to sharpen the reasoning, then do most of the designing in
  the browser: throw the components around and squint at the screen.
- Put everything on a firm grid.
- For commercial screens (baskets, checkout), reduce: remove what is extraneous, let
  things breathe, and let hairlines and type sizes do the work.

## 9. Phones

- Reflow; do not shrink.
- Keep body text large. Keep the drop caps, the footnotes and the voice; the irrelevant
  pictures stay.
- A flexible layout may "twang around" and look a little odd at some widths. That is
  acceptable.

## 10. Articulate the decisions

Write each major decision in the brief as a move with its reason in visual grammar, never
"because it feels right": line and point, form, formal or informal structure, balance,
pattern, texture, repetition and rhythm, path and movement, colour, white space as the
pause, depth, affordance, juxtaposition, economy, grid.

## What to refuse

- Labels nobody wrote; icons that depict their section literally.
- Fake wear and texture; effects for their own sake.
- More faces or colours than the kit allows.
- Minimal for its own sake; white space as a default rather than a pause.
- A label stamped above each section heading, whether caps, italic or numbered.
- Any word inside a headline set apart by colour, italic, weight or a mark. Give the
  whole line one treatment; a stacked lockup may treat each whole line differently.
- Copy built as a setup with a short emphasised payoff.
- Content set in boxes repeated in a grid. Use ruled lists, catalogue entries, a
  timeline.
