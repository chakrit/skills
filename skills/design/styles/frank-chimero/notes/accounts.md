# Accounts: what other people say about Frank Chimero's design

Stream: `accounts`. Raw records are in `/tmp/designers/frank-chimero/raw/accounts/`, one
text file per source with its URL on line 1 (HTML stripped to text). Pages were read in
full from those files except where noted. Two pieces of work were looked at in the
browser on Wayback to check what reviewers describe: Lost World's Fairs Atlantis (2010)
and "What Screens Want" (2013).

Claim markers: **[reported]** someone else describes it; **[stated, via X]** his own
words as relayed or quoted by someone else; **[observed]** seen in the work.

Coverage note: third-party writing about Chimero is mostly about his *ideas*; outside
descriptions of how his work *looks* are few. The visual moves below therefore lean on a
handful of sources (Print 2010, Fonts In Use, CSS-Tricks, MetaFilter, Goodreads), checked
where possible by looking at the work in the browser (Lost World's Fairs Atlantis and
"What Screens Want", both on Wayback). No outside account of his Abstract or
Studio Frank work was found.

## Moves

### 1. Flat, few-colour illustration with a hand-traced edge

- **What others say.** Print's 2010 New Visual Artists profile calls it "classic Chimero":
  flat backgrounds, two or three pastel colours, geometric vector drawings made human by
  tracing the outlines by hand and rescanning them. [reported, Print 2010]
- **Where it came from.** Print attributes the limited pastel palette to screen-printing
  posters in college. [reported, Print 2010]
- **The default it replaces.** Clean, glossy vector work with unlimited colour and
  perfect edges. The move is to restrict the palette as a print process would and to
  rough up the geometry on purpose.
- **Peers' reaction.** Aaron Draplin singled out the illustration, especially the States
  series, as what stood out most in 2008. [reported, Draplin] Grain Edit's editors
  describe the work as mixing humour, wit and style. [reported, Grain Edit 2009]
- **Seen in the work.** The Atlantis World's Fair page uses the same language at page
  scale: flat sky-blue sea with soft, low-contrast wave texture, a tan sky, one hot
  orange-red accent (the depth badge), dark grey circular "portholes" on a grey shaft.
  [observed, Wayback 2012 capture]
- **When it fits.** Editorial or explanatory pages where a drawn image should carry a
  concept rather than decorate; Print quotes him that he loves "ideas more than
  prettiness". [stated, via Print 2010]

### 2. Scroll as a physical journey (the Atlantis descent)

- **What others say.** Kottke held up Chimero's 1962 Atlantis World's Fair as the
  standout of the Lost World's Fairs set and as proof that web fonts made web design
  experimental and fun again. [reported, Kottke 2010] SOCKS reduced it to "HTML5 + web
  fonts". [reported, SOCKS 2010]
- **Seen in the work.** The page is one long vertical descent into the sea. A depth
  gauge in a speech-bubble badge counts "0K, 1K, 2K LEAGUES" as you scroll; a ruler of
  tick marks runs down the left edge; "BELOW" is set vertically; the title "1962 /
  ATLANTIS / WORLD'S FAIR" is a large white serif with very wide letterspacing on the
  date and on "WORLDS"; facts arrive as small teal "DID YOU KNOW" panels with yellow
  heavy sans heads. [observed, Wayback 2012 capture]
- **The default it replaces.** A scrolling page that only stacks sections. Here the
  scroll position *is* the story's variable (depth), shown by a persistent gauge.
- **When it fits.** One-off narrative or promotional pages with a single spatial idea.
  Others did not discuss reading cost; Chimero later argued against "whizzbangery" in
  marketing sites (see move 5), so treat this as early-career, not his settled view.

### 3. The essay as a paced visual sequence, with colour that changes as you read

- **What others say.** Chris Coyier called "What Screens Want" beautiful and pointed at
  its colours slowly fading as you read, as a way of enacting the essay's point about
  flux. [reported, CSS-Tricks 2013] Hillel Arnold: short, punchy and "pretty to look
  at". [reported, Rockefeller Archive blog] A MetaFilter defender called it a good way to
  turn a talk into a visual essay, and another liked its slow pacing that leads to the
  final point. [reported, MetaFilter 2015]
- **Type, as recorded by others.** Tiempos Text for the body, Lato for the large
  interstitial type, Klim's Calibre in some graphics. [reported, Fonts In Use; the Lato
  and Tiempos credit is his own colophon, quoted there]
- **Seen in the work.** Opens on a dark charcoal slab with a large, three-line serif
  title ending in a long grey rule; the subtitle is small grey italic. Below, a pale
  mint band, then a single centred column of mid-grey serif text on white, a short
  centred rule, and a large centred serif section question in ochre/gold.
  [observed, Wayback 2013 capture]
- **Comparison others make.** One MetaFilter commenter likened the giant interstitial
  type to Douglas Coupland's book design of the 2000s. [reported, MetaFilter 2015]
- **The default it replaces.** A blog post in the site template. Each long essay gets its
  own page, its own colour sequence and big set-piece type between sections.

### 4. Make the book's own object argue its case

- **What readers say.** Goodreads reviewers repeatedly praise the physical design of
  *The Shape of Design*, which he did himself: "nicely understated, well-considered" with
  a gravitas rare in design writing; good to hold; a binding that recalls old, durable
  library books; one reader says the design shows the book's message in action, and
  another that its design signals loftiness and makes no pretence of being practical.
  [reported, Goodreads]
- **Type, as recorded by others.** The book is set in Quadraat; the website and free
  online edition add Quadraat Sans and Trade Gothic. [reported, Fonts In Use]
- **Credit.** His editor, Mandy Brown, notes that the art is entirely his. [reported, A
  Working Library]
- **When it fits.** A long-form piece where the container should feel durable and
  bookish, not like a product page: a humanist serif with a matched sans, restrained
  colour.

### 5. Go with the web's grain: a "traditional" look, little CSS, no statement layouts

- **What others say.** Trent Walton endorsed Chimero's 2014 site for accepting a
  traditional look over a willfully esoteric one, and for treating a lot of markup or
  CSS as a sign of working against the web; Walton calls him one of the best web
  designers he knows. [reported, Walton 2014; the two principles are Chimero's, quoted
  there]
- **Echoes.** Bram Van Damme summarised "The Web's Grain" as moving from a nineties
  nostalgia to a case against sites that try to blow you away with technique.
  [reported, bram.us] Jim Nielsen pulled the RWD podcast passage in which Chimero calls
  showpiece marketing sites the new Flash: looked at more than used.
  [stated, via Nielsen] Chris Ferdinandi took "change the design, not the tools" (the
  house that is easy to build, not the skyscraper crane) as a working method.
  [reported, Go Make Things] Rob Weychert quoted the same line. [reported, Weychert]
- **The default it replaces.** Novel layout for its own sake, and heavy tooling to make
  it possible.
- **When it fits.** Personal sites, writing-led sites, anything that should age well.

### 6. Redesign in public, typography first

- **What others say.** Pat Dryburgh and Daniel Eden both note that his 2020 site
  redesign was documented post by post, and Eden groups him with Ethan Marcotte as
  someone who publicly took a site apart and patiently rebuilt it. [reported, Dryburgh;
  Eden] Weychert recommended his post on evaluating typefaces. [reported, Weychert] The
  2018 site got a one-line "beautiful new site" from Weychert. [reported, Weychert]
- **Note.** Nobody outside wrote a visual critique of any version of his site.
  Descriptions of the 2020 typefaces and grid come from his own posts and belong to the
  `own` stream.

## Process and thinking (as others relay it)

- **Design as response, not solution.** His editor Mandy Brown says the book's framing
  of design as a response to a problem, filtered through the designer's disposition,
  changed how she sees her own and others' work; she uses his example of three chairs
  for one need. [reported, A Working Library]
- **How and Why.** Reviewers and interviewers repeat his split between craft ("how") and
  purpose ("why"); Print 2010 links the "why" to teachers at Missouri State who had
  worked under Communism and taught concept and symbolism. [reported, FINE; Print 2010]
- **Influence as shared light.** Popova highlights his chapter on improvisation and
  limits: building from others' work and adding something new, through Buson's haiku
  of one candle lighting another. [reported, Marginalian]
- **From decoration to systems.** Debbie Millman frames his career as moving from
  illustrating, which he calls decorating other people's ideas, to interface work,
  which means designing systems for unknown content. [reported, Print 2015; his words
  quoted there]
- **A fixed illustration sequence.** Grain Edit's intro calls his process "famed"; the
  steps themselves are his and are left to the `talks` stream. [reported, Grain Edit]
- **Simplify the design, not the toolchain.** See move 5. [reported, Ferdinandi;
  Keith]
- **Print and web feed each other.** The studio Another, which worked with him on *The
  Great Discontent* print magazine, describes the print design as taken from the
  website and then fed back into it, "a true multi-format design system". [reported,
  Fonts In Use, quoting Another]

## Taste (as others see it)

- **Serif text faces with character, with a plain sans beside them:** Quadraat with
  Quadraat Sans and Trade Gothic (2012); Tiempos with Lato (2013); posters in History,
  Heroic Condensed and FF Tisa (2010). [reported, Fonts In Use]
- **Few, soft colours** taken from screen printing; flat fields, no gloss. [reported,
  Print 2010; observed on Atlantis]
- **Influences named by others:** Alan Fletcher as his biggest influence (from a search
  summary of a My Modern Met post, not read in full); Japanese poetry, Marshall McLuhan
  and art references through the book. [reported, Marginalian; Goodreads]
- **Dislikes, as others relay them:** showpiece "whizzbangery", attention-commodifying
  platforms ("pave the wilderness ... build a mall"), complexity without legibility,
  loss of view-source. [stated, via Keith, Nielsen]
- **Dissent:**
  - Goodreads: the book is lucid on design's "shape" but abstract in practice; heavy
    analogies and highbrow references read as posturing; "about 15-20 pages that really
    matter"; "flowery words". Others defend its philosophical bent. [reported, Goodreads]
  - MetaFilter: several readers disliked the "What Screens Want" layout ("initially put
    off by the formatting"); others found it readable, even on a phone and in reader
    view. [reported, MetaFilter]
  - CSS-Tricks: Coyier is less pessimistic about the web than the essay. [reported]
  - Tsai's commenters reject "Everything Easy" as rose-tinted nostalgia, saying flexbox
    and Grid make the web easier, not harder, and that the web now serves applications,
    not pages. [reported, mjtsai.com]
  - Alan Jacobs accepts flux for some reading but argues a serious novel tolerates
    almost none, so the right amount depends on genre. [reported, New Atlantis]
