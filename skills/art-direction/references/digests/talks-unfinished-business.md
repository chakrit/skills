# Unfinished Business, season 3 (2018): "Art directing for the web" — digest

Source: six transcripts of Andy Clarke's podcast, which he recorded while writing his
"hardboiled" book on art direction (later *Art Direction for the Web*). Clarke is the
host; the transcripts label him "AK" or "AC". The researcher's local text copies
(`ub-UBPS3E*.txt`) are not published; the PDFs are at
stuffandnonsense.co.uk/content/transcripts/ (full URLs in `../notes/talks.md`).

How to read this digest:

- **Locations.** "p12" is the transcript page number, which appears in the page footer
  ("© 2018 Unfinished Business Podcast 12") just after that page's text. A timestamp is
  added where it helps.
- **Labels.** "Clarke:" marks his own statements. "Guest (name), Clarke agrees/pushes
  back" marks a guest idea he reacted to. "Guest only" marks a guest idea he did not
  react to, kept because it is a repeatable move relevant to small-business sites.
- **Transcription errors.** The transcripts are machine-made and garbled in places
  ("Dan Mole" for Dan Mall, "patent" for pattern, "integrate" for what is almost
  certainly "denigrate"). Readings that are uncertain are marked "(sic)" or "likely".
- **Small screens.** Clarke says little about them this season. What he does say is
  collected under E4 and E7.

## E2 — Mark Porter (former Guardian creative director, magazine art director)

`ub-UBPS3E2-mark-porter.txt`

### Clarke's own views

- **Background and influences (p5–6).** Clarke says he was not trained as a designer.
  He is "rediscovering" art direction through books and through Neville Brody: he read
  *The Face* in the 1980s for its interviews (Boy George, Paul Weller, Grace Jones). He
  cites Cath Caldwell's book on art direction, which includes Porter.
- **Why now (p5–6).** Younger designers ask him how an editorial approach is relevant
  to the web or to a digital product. He thinks CSS Grid lets the web break out of "that
  traditional kind of two or three column model", so art direction will become a wider
  conversation.
- **Definition (p8).** Porter describes the magazine art director's daily job: read the
  text, find its theme, mood and emotions, then decide whether it needs photography,
  illustration or only type, and commission the images. Clarke: "the best summary of
  art direction I've heard in ages."
- **Two other definitions he cites.** Dan Mall (p9): art direction is "the visual
  resonance of how a piece of work feels", and design is the technical execution (do
  the colours match, is the line length comfortable). Phil Coffman of Springbox
  (p10–11): the designer handles execution and the art director handles the strategy
  behind it.
- **Craft over grand titles (p11).** Clarke does not want to denigrate design ("(sic)
  integrate") by inventing a grander term.
- **Why he does the work (p11–12, ~0:24:46).** Solving problems for users is "not why
  I get out of bed in the morning". He separates something people can use from
  something people "love to use". His conference line: "I don't design toothpaste",
  but his work helps people choose Colgate over Aquafresh. He says atomic design,
  pattern libraries and CMS templates have pushed designers down one route. His
  interest is telling the story, whether it is a prison break or an expensive watch,
  so that people connect with it.
- **Against design as decoration (p13–14).** He quotes Irene Au: designers should
  understand that "visual adornment is meant to support the experience and not be the
  experience". He admits he was "unkind to her" in a talk. His objection is to a view
  of design as prettifying: something made attractive and usable once, ticked off,
  then never considered again.
- **Distinctive but conventional where it counts (p16–17).** In the Guardian iPhone
  app the next article peeks in from the right, and the back button and share icons
  are the platform's standard ones. Clarke's point is that a product should not break
  people's mental models, yet the app still feels "very distinctively Guardian".
- **The web as a "cozy cardigan" (p18, ~0:42:29).** He thinks the web has grown up
  into "very safe and not particularly challenging" work. CMSs and technology set the
  limit of what people think is possible, so distinctiveness and deeper creative
  thinking are largely missing.
- **The Brody model (p20, ~0:48:58).** Brody deconstructed and rebuilt what a magazine
  looks like. Clarke describes the method in two layers:
  - First, design the foundation once: grid systems, base type styles and hierarchy,
    mastheads, contents pages and the other "furniture".
  - Then design each article "almost uniquely" on top of that foundation.
  He hopes this becomes "the next step in web design": consistency and readability get
  settled at each redesign, and per-piece creativity happens on top, guided by "what
  the real story is and how we want somebody to feel".
- **Design systems (p21–23, ~0:54:49).** Clarke traces the line from style guides to
  living style guides, component libraries and design systems (IBM Carbon, Shopify
  Polaris). He thinks these risk "not allowing that level of creative freedom on top of
  the system". In companies he works with, anything outside the system is called a
  "snowflake" and treated as a hack, then folded back in once it has been used two or
  three times.
- **The missing piece (p23).** A system should make work fast and consistent ("you
  don't end up with half a dozen different button styles"). What it lacks is art
  direction: asking what to do so that this particular piece of content works best.
  Even a local-government site or a product site must "read that content, illustrate
  it, photograph it, visualize it, lay it out", based on the style guide's rules "not
  just limited by" them.

### Guest ideas Clarke reacted to

- **Judge after a week, not on first reaction (p3–4).** Porter (Clarke agrees): ignore
  the instant reaction in a presentation room. Leave the client a printed dummy or a
  clickable prototype for a week, then ask. Clarke mentions a recent presentation whose
  first reaction was not what he hoped for.
- **Use the device's conventions (p17).** Porter (Clarke agrees): know when to use the
  platform's own behaviour. Messing with scroll, swipe or pinch only creates "cognitive
  dissonance".
- **Real content from the start (p18–19).** Porter (Clarke does not dispute it): grey
  rectangles and dummy type "is not design". After the basic grid maths, real pictures
  and real headlines go in from the very beginning.
- **Every feature unique, inside a known personality (p20–21).** Porter (Clarke
  agrees): most magazines try to make every feature unique. The front and back
  sections are formatted, while the features in the middle are free "within the visual
  language of the publication". Knowing what is right or wrong for the publication is
  the art director's skill. The range runs from *The Economist* (fully templated)
  through *New York* (strong images, fixed type) to *The Face* (extreme).
- **Match the style to the audience (p21).** Porter (Clarke agrees): deconstructed
  layouts suit a youth audience because they feel rebellious, not a mainstream one.
  Recent independent magazines have swung the other way: white covers, a framed
  picture, and a full-bleed image facing a centred headline.
- **Art direction in proportion to the job (p23–24).** Porter, Clarke agrees ("perfect
  note to end"): how much art direction a site needs follows from what the business and
  audience require. GOV.UK should be clear, plain and accessible. Pitchfork, The
  Intercept and Bloomberg Businessweek can be expressive.

### Examples named

- **The Face (1980s, Neville Brody).** Deconstructed type and layout, with each feature
  designed individually on top of a solid system.
- **The Guardian (Porter, 2005) and its iPad app.** A distinctive brand carried across
  print, web and app.
- **DN / Dagens Næringsliv (p19).** A Norwegian business paper Clarke and Porter worked
  on. Scandinavian business papers are loud tabloids, unlike the restrained serif FT or
  Wall Street Journal, so the brief was a financial product with a loud visual
  language.
- **IBM Carbon, Shopify Polaris.** Design systems Clarke names as a risk to creative
  freedom.
- **ITN redesign; a Dutch TV channel (p22).** Porter approached TV the same way as
  print and digital.
- **GOV.UK vs Pitchfork, The Intercept, Bloomberg Businessweek.** The two ends of the
  "how much art direction" range.

## E3 — Dan Mall (SuperFriendly; author of an early article on web art direction)

`ub-UBPS3E3-dan-mall.txt`

### Clarke's own views

- **Feeling, not technique (p6–7).** The CSS Grid conversation has been about
  technique: "we can reproduce magazine layouts". Clarke says nobody has yet worked out
  what art direction on the web is meant to achieve.
- **A loading spinner as art direction (p7–8, ~0:11:32).** A colleague split the
  company logo into four quadrants, rotated them and brought them back together with a
  bounce. The team debated whether the bounce makes you smile or annoys you if it
  happens too often. Clarke calls this his "light bulb moment": whether the bounce says
  something about the brand's personality, and conveys the right emotion while someone
  waits for a PDF, is art direction.
- **Layout carries the feeling of a story (p9).** For a story about an escaped gunman,
  the question is whether to relay facts or make readers feel on edge. The image
  choices, and whether the layout is "slightly isometric or disjointed", shape that
  feeling. He praises ProPublica for doing this well.
- **Redesign-level art direction is brand work (p10).** It makes sure every touchpoint
  conveys the intended feeling.
- **Brand trickles down to every touchpoint (p11–12, ~0:21:06).** He lists social
  posts, LinkedIn, email newsletters, landing pages, marketing pages, sign-up and
  onboarding. He asks whether consistent feeling across all of them is art direction;
  Mall says yes.
- **Beyond visuals (p13).** A unified brand experience includes tone of voice. Clarke
  recalls the Bernbach-era pairing of copywriter and art director.
- **Feedback that names a feeling (p17–18, ~0:33:11).** Clarke presented concepts he
  thought were very well designed: the layout and text arrangement reflected the
  imagery, using "classic kind of art direction techniques". The stakeholders said
  "It doesn't feel like us." They said they were changing how business is done in
  finance, something revolutionary, and asked whether he could see that in the designs.
  Clarke admits the honest answer was no, and calls this feedback "incredibly
  important".
- **Smashing Magazine (p19).** Clarke did early comic-style concept work for the
  redesign (joking that it was "1987"). He values Smashing's cartoony brand. He
  describes thumbnails that are tilted and straighten on hover. His question is how
  writers, illustrators, developers and editors collaborate when five to ten articles a
  week go through a CMS.
- **Design outside the edges (p21–22, ~0:43:33).** Engineering-minded people value
  design systems for efficiency (his CTO bakes them into React or CSS modules). Clarke:
  "very few people are really talking about well, we need to understand this stuff well
  enough so that we can then design outside of those edges."
- **His principles workshop (p25–27, ~0:52:56).**
  - Process: he brought marketing and product designers together to translate an
    external agency's brand work into social, email, web and product.
  - Principles: "bold, clear, intentional and reassuring".
  - Format: the group decided a single word works better than a word with a sentence,
    because extra words reduce clarity and pull attention to the smaller words.
  - Next step (a product design lead's idea): for a week, everyone collects material
    from anywhere (TV commercials, posters, visual treatments) and pins it up as "this
    means bold to me". The group then looks for common ground. For example,
    "reassuring" turned out to mean warm neutral palettes, close to the opposite of
    bold.
- **The lone visionary vs collaboration (p27–28).** Clarke used to argue that companies
  must trust the art director's instincts, as with Brody's vision for *The Face*. He
  now sees the work as bringing everyone together through systems, principles and
  shared mood boards.
- **What a design system is for (p29).** The real benefit is the conversations it
  causes, not the documentation, although he has just written 300 words on the Oxford
  comma.

### Guest ideas Clarke reacted to

- **Feel vs look; ask for feeling words (p14–16, ~0:27:20).** Mall, Clarke agrees ("I'm
  going to completely steal that example for the first chapter").
  - Mall's example: a sparkly "happy birthday" GIF in Comic Sans is badly designed but
    well art directed, because it feels happy and bright.
  - Mall asks clients for feedback on art direction ("too bright, too bold, too quiet"),
    not design ("move this up 20 pixels"). The designer then knows "a hundred
    different ways" to deliver the feeling.
  - Even his young children are good art directors: they can say whether something
    feels happy or scary.
- **Turn up the feeling (p17).** Mall (Clarke agrees, and gives his own story): a
  client said a design did not feel "electric enough", so Mall made the colours
  hotter. A "military-inspired" comp with greens and stencil fonts failed because the
  client did not read it as military, so the concept was right but the execution was
  not.
- **Looking custom with modular parts (p20).** Mall: magazine features look custom but
  are modular. Design systems should allow a middle ground between using the template
  and starting from scratch. Clarke builds on this in the "outside the edges" point
  above.
- **Identity, not "art direction" (p27–28).** Mall: when talking to clients, call it
  "identity". A young brand needs a combination of signals; a mature brand can own a
  single one (Hertz yellow). Clarke accepts this as a summary of the collaboration
  question.

### Guest only (repeatable moves)

- **Smashing Magazine principles turned into visual decisions (p23–25, ~0:46:06).**
  - The principles: "Smashing is the community", and communities are vibrant, have
    dimension, are quirky and soft, and are "turned up to eleven".
  - **Vibrant:** competitor sites (CSS-Tricks and others) used muted palettes, so
    Smashing got bright reds, oranges and blues offset by dark greys.
  - **Dimension:** everything gets light sources and a 3D feel "while the whole world
    is going flat".
  - **Quirky and soft:** the sharp, utilitarian Proxima Nova was replaced with a round,
    quirky typeface ("meat" (sic), likely Mija) with an unconventional lowercase a.
  - **Eleven:** 11 is used as a "magic number" everywhere: 11-degree rotation on
    avatars (building on Clarke's earlier tilted avatars) and matching corner rounding.
  - **Community made literal:** the author avatar is giant and at the top of the
    article.
  - **Principles as a test:** each section is checked with "is this vibrant? does it
    have dimension?", from the newsletter to the checkout flow.
- **Boring first (p22–23).** Mall cites Josh Clark's "The most exciting design systems
  are boring": systematise the boring parts first (buttons, dropdowns) so effort goes
  into one-off problems.

### Examples named

- **NYT "Snow Fall" (p13).** Art directed section by section: video, animation, and the
  page turning dark and then light again.
- **Craigslist (p18).** Functional but not art directed. Mall adds that the raw look
  may in fact suit a "use at your own risk" brand.
- **Smashing Magazine redesign (Mall, after Clarke's concept work).** See the moves
  above.
- **Marty Neumeier (p11, transcribed "the brand new Myer", likely *The Brand Gap*).**
  Mall cites him: brand is a person's gut feeling about an organisation.
- **Hertz yellow (p27).** A brand that owns a colour.

## E4 — Rachel Andrew (CSS Working Group, Grid by Example)

`ub-UBPS3E4-rachel-andrew.txt` — mostly technical; the design-relevant parts follow.

### Clarke's own views

- **His own site (p5, ~0:08:31).** He rebuilt the Stuff & Nonsense site using only CSS
  Grid, with Flexbox for components and no fallbacks, because his audience is mostly
  web people. He also used custom properties and CSS Shapes, drawing the polygons for
  text wraps in Chrome dev tools (p12).
- **Demos that are just "shiny" (p8, ~0:14:11).** Much Grid content amounts to
  "let's recreate this 1930s constructivist layout". He cannot relate that to an
  e-commerce site or a news site, and asks where useful, inspiring teaching will come
  from.
- **Art direction is not pull quotes (p9–10, ~0:17:46).** He wrote a Smashing article
  whose worked example was based on a Neville Brody layout from *The Face* ("I'm a big
  Neville Brody fan"), and he gets "the web isn't print" objections. He is writing the
  book partly because he does not want people to "confuse art direction with hanging
  [pull] quotes". For him it is "a lot more about the thinking behind why you would do
  something and how it makes people feel rather than the technical execution".
- **Designers should know what code allows (p11).** Working with Porter on DN, Clarke
  had to tell him he could not align image captions to the bottom of an image (this
  was before Flexbox).
- **Multi-column layout on phones (p16, ~0:34:10).** He uses CSS columns for footer
  links and navigation. In landscape orientation the links flow into two or three
  columns, so the footer is not "4,000 pixels tall" like Microsoft's.
- **Wish list (p17–18).** He wants to style column rules with border images, give
  alternating backgrounds to multi-column columns, and draw rules in the middle of
  grid tracks. These are editorial devices: decorative column rules and banded columns.
- **Accessibility (p18–19).** He worries about source-order reordering in magazine-style
  grid demos.
- **Grid as a design tool (p20–22, ~0:43:34).** He went from "pencil sketches to a
  beautifully responsive grid-based layout in about 45 seconds". He uses Grid to sit
  with a client and "get the layout right", to try moving the sidebar, and to A/B test
  content hierarchy. He also suggests it for style guides, even for a "stuffy old
  corporate". He values Grid because it lets us "layout content so that it's kind of
  more meaningful or powerful".

### Guest ideas Clarke reacted to

- **Look before you code (p10–11).** Rachel, Clarke agrees: designers should study
  what is now possible instead of accepting developers' "you can't do this on the web".
  Clarke adds the DN captions story.
- **Learn by inspecting (p11–12).** Rachel, Clarke agrees: use Firefox's grid inspector
  to see how other layouts are built. Clarke: "I learned visually."
- **Keep the reading order intact (p19).** Rachel (Clarke raised the question): after
  any visual reordering, check that the page still works with keyboard navigation.

### Guest only

- **Rebuild magazine layouts (p9, ~0:15:41).** Rachel has workshop attendees rebuild
  magazine layouts to get them away from "12 column grid" and Bootstrap thinking. It
  shows them they can have white space and elements that respond to each other's
  height. She also argues that the "web-like" look comes from old technical limits, not
  from good design.

### Examples named

- **Doug Bowman's Wired redesign (p6).** The first big CSS-layout site, which made the
  technique acceptable.
- **Stripe (p5).** An early large adopter of Grid.
- **Jen Simmons, Layout Land (p10).** Video series for designers learning layout.
- **Neville Brody's *The Face* layout (p9).** Recreated in Clarke's Smashing Grid
  article.

## E5 — David Sleight (design director, ProPublica)

`ub-UBPS3E5-david-sleight.txt`

### Clarke's own views

- **Designers on the editorial team (p4).** Digital work tends to split designers,
  developers, content strategists and UX into silos. He is interested in a design
  director who sits on the editorial team next to the people writing the words.
- **Big-story judgment applies everywhere (p6).** He jokes that corporate blogs are
  where press releases go to die. Deciding what is a big story, and signalling it in
  the design, applies well outside newsrooms.
- **Devils and the DEA (p7).** He remembers ProPublica's El Chapo story mainly for its
  opening art.
- **"Evocative of the theme" (p13, ~0:27:28).** He likes Sleight's phrase for abstract
  illustration. His listeners in corporate or software jobs also have stories to tell
  about users and products, and a dull monthly theme such as "productivity" can be made
  engaging "by elevating the meaning" (p14).
- **Landing pages (p15, ~0:31:51).** He mocks the stock photo ("Ethnically diverse
  people enjoying a spreadsheet", p14). A landing page reached from an email campaign is
  "a perfect opportunity to direct how somebody feels" towards the call to action, and
  "absolutely ripe for good art direction".
- **Pace from comics (p16, ~0:34:07).** He gave a talk based on Scott McCloud's
  *Understanding Comics* (strong recommendation). The idea is to slow readers down or
  speed them up at chosen moments, using panel size and repeated shapes "to create that
  rhythm". He admits this is still flat, on-screen thinking. He then drops the word
  "stories" because it puts some people off ("but, in fact, everybody does" tell them).
- **The art director as the glue (p17).** Across social, email, web and video, art
  direction sits in the middle of the team so the message does not get "lost in the
  gaps".
- **The essence of the story (p19, ~0:42:43).** His summary: someone has to think about
  the essence of the story and how the organisation wants people to feel, and that
  person should be "pivotal" in the team, not just present.
- **Small delights must fit the brand (p20–21, ~0:46:20).** He describes a login
  screen with a bear whose eyes follow the cursor and which pulls a face at a wrong
  password: "surprise and delight". How far to take it depends on the design
  principles and brand values; it may not suit a tax return. Brand personality and
  tone of voice decide it: "that's where the art comes in".
- **Slack (p22).** Has a constructed personality that is "not trying too hard".
- **An activity, not a job title (p23, ~0:52:25).** Before the 1960s an art director
  put art to copy "thrown over the wall"; the role later became crafting ideas. Clarke
  now thinks art direction is an activity many people perform. An interaction designer
  choosing the bear's expression is art directing, and so is someone choosing
  illustrations for a corporate brochure.

### Guest ideas Clarke reacted to

- **Abstract art when the story is uncertain (p12–13).** Sleight, Clarke agrees: when
  a story's direction is unknown, commission metaphorical, abstract illustration
  "evocative of its theme" rather than depicting specific events. It survives late
  rewrites.
- **Feel before say (p14).** Sleight (Clarke builds on it with the landing-page
  point): ask "What do you want somebody to feel?" instead of "What do you want this
  page to say?". That "strange literalism" is what produces the laughing-businessman
  stock photo.
- **Art direction that sets the story's structure (p17–18, ~0:37:42).** Sleight, Clarke
  agrees ("A number one example"). In "An Unbelievable Story of Rape" (with The Marshall
  Project), two reporters' voices were kept separate. The victim's chapters use
  figurative illustration and the police chapters use evidentiary photography. Clarke:
  the presentation "adds a huge amount of weight to the feeling".
- **Not window dressing (p19).** Sleight (Clarke agrees): art direction should have a
  seat at the table.
- **Don't overplay the hand (p20–21).** Sleight, Clarke agrees: a small delight works
  when it "reveals itself over time". Crass marketing is "everything at 10".
- **Art direction is a skill, not a role (p23).** Sleight agrees with Clarke's view;
  Clarke jokes it is the first time a guest has agreed with him.

### Guest only

- **Opener art "a couple clicks over the top" (p8).** For Devils and the DEA,
  illustrator Tim McDonagh (transcribed "McDonough") made "bonkers" opening art. A
  well-matched illustration sets the tone "right off the bat".
- **Words first, then sketches (p10).** Everything starts as words: talking to the
  writer "like group psychologists". Then paper-notebook sketches of the opener,
  screenshots of similar work, and straight to code.
- **Product art direction in time (p15).** On the web, art direction includes time and
  interaction: how things unfold and reveal themselves across a user flow, not only a
  static layout.

### Examples named

- **Devils and the DEA (ProPublica).** "Bonkers" illustrated opener.
- **An Unbelievable Story of Rape (ProPublica and The Marshall Project, Pulitzer).**
  Illustration vs photography split by point of view.
- **Firestone and the Warlord (p12).** A 20,000-word story worked on until 3 a.m.
- **Dropbox (p15).** Adopting editorial, paper-cut-style illustration. Sleight thinks
  the transition is not finished yet, but it is an attempt to art direct a platform.
- **Skype's "wiggly worm" typing indicator (p20); the bear login screen; Slack; the
  Twitter app (p22).** Product art direction examples.

## E6 — Stephen Hay (Catawiki; author of *Responsive Design Workflow*)

`ub-UBPS3E6-stephen-hay.txt`

### Clarke's own views

- **Embellishment and stuff (p4, ~0:05:13).** Against Hay's minimalism, Clarke says: "I
  like a product that has a certain embellishment to it." He keeps a clean desk but
  surrounds himself with King Kong posters and Godzilla action figures, because "stuff
  is stimulating".
- **The book's structure (p7).** It has a section on art directing marketing and
  transactional sites and a separate chapter on digital products. Hay's questions make
  him reconsider the split.
- **Communication, not product (p9, ~0:16:23).** "I've never ever considered myself a
  product designer. I've always wanted to be involved in communication more than
  anything else." The term art direction rarely resonates with product designers.
- **Definition (p11).** Art direction is about "extracting meaning and making somebody
  feel a certain way" when they read editorial or see an ad.
- **Sell the experience (p11–12, ~0:25:33).** He is working on visual identity
  positioning, with Airbnb as a top reference. He notes a "mono-culture" of visual
  identities in tech, where all the illustration looks the same (crediting "Kevin").
  Airbnb and Uber sell the weekend in San Francisco or the festival, not the room or
  the ride. The difficulty is carrying that message down into icon style, label
  wording and tone of voice.
- **His detail habits (p14–15, ~0:31:35).** He calls himself "very fastidious":
  - He sets line weights as a mirror or proportion of the typeface's stroke.
  - He echoes the style and colour of shadows in illustrations.
  - He carries marketing-side animations and transitions into the product.
  He calls these "low-hanging fruit" for a unified feel.
- **Feelings as the glue (p18, ~0:39:45).** Clarke reads Hay's design funnel (below)
  and adapts it: the "moods and metaphors" step "could actually be about how you want
  somebody to feel". That feeling is the glue across formats and channels, so what goes
  on a small screen, in a native app or on a TV "is not going to be superficial".
- **Turning principles into visuals (p19–20, ~0:44:22).** In his principles workshop,
  "bold" licenses provocative messaging. "Reassuring" was hard to visualise; softer
  colours and lower contrast are the obvious answers. He decides reassurance comes more
  from behaviour: fast uploads, previews, and empty states that fill in progressively.
  His example is YouTube on 3G, where the greyed-out placeholder rectangles pulse gently
  to show the app has not crashed.

### Guest ideas Clarke reacted to

- **Art direction is fundamental to UX (p10–11).** Clarke reads out Twitter replies to
  his question "Does art direction have a place in digital products?". Marco Pisani
  says art direction is fundamental to UX and that Airbnb sells "experiences and not
  rooms". Clarke: "This guy is good." Hay agrees too. A reply from David Perel (sic)
  says art direction comes after the UX is designed; Clarke contrasts the two but does
  not endorse it.
- **Onboarding and empty states (p13–14).** Keith Robinson (Atlassian) says art
  direction matters most in sign-up, onboarding, the handoff from marketing to product,
  empty states, feedback, animation, delight and error messages. Clarke takes this up.
  Hay goes further: it should "permeate through everything". Otherwise the house has a
  pretty outside and a "ripped up" inside built by engineers.
- **Hay's design funnel (p15–16, ~0:32:51).** Hay, Clarke strongly agrees.
  - The steps run from values and goals, to moods and metaphors found through
    association, to ideas, to a concept, to the visual language, and only then to
    design.
  - Hay notes that most designers jump straight to the visual language and "grasp onto
    ... little tricks", naming Clarke's typeface-proportion trick. This is a gentle
    pushback, and Clarke accepts it.
  - Every small choice, such as the typeface, should be able to answer why. The art
    director works on what comes before the visual language: "the life blood that runs
    through every limb of this product".
- **Reassurance depends on context (p20–21).** Hay, Clarke agrees: booking a flight to
  Australia needs payment security and trust, while streaming needs confidence that
  playback will not buffer. The tactics differ.

### Guest only

## E7 — Rob Weychert (editorial experience designer, ProPublica)

### Clarke's own views

- **An editorial approach for a product company (p5–6, ~0:10:55).** He opened a
  principles workshop with "my principles for design" (four of them) and said he wanted
  "an editorial approach to how we present the content". Asked what "editorial" means,
  he points to a designer's Uber case study: beautifully thought out and illustrated,
  "not ... a dump of words and pictures on the screen".
- **Lost Mothers grid (p9).** He praises it as "telling a story within a story", a
  story told without being explicit (details below).
- **Against pixel-perfect comps (p10).** Sketch has revived pixel-perfect mockups and
  "a hell of a lot of kind of waterfall effort", even on so-called agile teams.
- **Flexibility vs control (p11).** Citing John Allsopp's "A Dao of Web Design", he
  asks how to balance the web's fluidity with enough control to convey the message.
- **Small screens.** These are his main statements on small screens this season.
  - **Big-screen layout as enhancement (p12, ~0:28:07).** Precise control of layout on
    large screens is "almost like another layer of progressive enhancement": "when you
    get to a screen that is big enough ... then you can have the fancy grid layout."
  - **Small screens keep the art direction (p12, ~0:29:26).** Talks on "art direction
    for the web" often reduce to "let's make this magazine layout in CSS". Clarke: "the
    layout is only one facet of the tools that we would use for art direction even on
    the tiniest screens. We may not have layout in the broad sense ... but we still got
    mood. We still got feeling. We still got great typography." He adds that an
    interactive set of metaphors is barely explored.
- **Print's control vs the web's (p13).** Carson and Brody deconstructed, but print
  gave them control over the result. On the web we give up control of type size,
  layout, and even whether the reader can see the screen. So art direction "needs to be
  redefined".
- **Influences beyond design (p14–15, ~0:34:26).** Clarke studied fine art, not graphic
  design. Mid-century print art directors had broad interests in art: he notes Alexey
  Brodovitch beat Picasso to first prize in a poster competition, and that photography
  shaped their work. They "intuitively knew how to combine imagery with the written
  word". A narrow focus on product design puts "the blinkers on".
  - His modern example is "Brendan Doors" (sic, likely Brendan Dawes).
  - He hopes a layout like the Lost Mothers grid could be "informed by ... Damien Hirst
    painting".
- **Your own boring system (p17, ~0:42:54).** People take Bootstrap, tweak a few
  colours and consider design solved. Material Design is well designed, but its
  personality is Google's. His book proposes "foundation styles": colour, typography
  and interaction patterns that reflect this publication's or brand's goals and
  personality. "It's okay to have a boring design system as long as it's your boring
  design system and not somebody else's." He quotes Josh Clark: "crafting a design
  system is about clearing the way for others to invent and imagine."
- **Art direction treated as an afterthought (p18).** Product designers he has spoken
  to "feel like art direction kind of comes at the end ... the bells and whistles".
  Clarke presents this as a problem.
- **A "drier patch" (p19).** He thinks the last three or four years were dry because of
  tooling and the early focus on making responsive design work; art direction was set
  aside. Now is a good time for it in editorial, product and marketing.

### Guest ideas Clarke reacted to

- **Lost Mothers (p7–9, ~0:16:35).** Rob, Clarke agrees.
  - The story is about maternal deaths in the US: an estimated 700–900 in 2016, of
    whom only about 130 could be identified.
  - David Sleight resisted the first idea, a gallery of faces.
  - The final design is a grid of roughly 800 slots, mostly empty, so the empty space
    shows how little is known. Readers can then drill into individual women, with
    about 15 in-depth vignettes.
- **Visual thinking from everyday sources (p15, ~0:38:26).** Rob, Clarke's question
  prompts it. The Lost Mothers grid was inspired by the dot-grid floor of ProPublica's
  elevator. What matters is "visual thinking acumen", making non-obvious associations
  from the city, nature or a commute, more than formal cultural literacy. This
  partly narrows Clarke's fine-art framing.
- **Convention first, then specificity (p16, ~0:40:58).** Rob, Clarke follows with
  agreement and his "your own boring system" point.
  1. Start with the problem and its most conventional solution.
  2. Ask how the convention falls short for this problem.
  3. Add or subtract so the design answers "the very specific problem to be solved as
     opposed to the category of problem".
  For an article: flow the text into a standard layout, add images, read it, then ask
  "How might I hear it better? What if this happened?". Generic frameworks outsource
  the work to "somebody who doesn't know the specifics". The trouble is not that
  everything looks the same but that nobody works out what makes this thing special.
- **Specificity (p17).** Rob, Clarke agrees: is the design speaking to your audience
  specifically, or to whoever happens to be listening?
- **Big-screen fetish (p12).** Rob (responding to Clarke's progressive-enhancement
  point): people still fetishise big-screen layouts, though most readers use phones.
  Small screens are still "this just tall stacked thin layout", and memorable
  storytelling there is unsolved.

## Recurring themes across the season

1. **Art direction is about how the work makes someone feel and what it means, not the
   technique.** Clarke returns to this in E2 (connecting people to the story), E3 (the
   spinner bounce), E4 ("not ... hanging [pull] quotes"), E6 ("extracting meaning and
   making somebody feel") and E7 (layout is "only one facet"). He repeatedly warns
   against equating art direction with CSS Grid magazine recreations (E3, E4, E7).
2. **The Brody / *The Face* model: a solid system with each piece designed on top.**
   He states this most fully in E2 and refers back to it in E3, E4 (his Brody-based
   Grid demo) and E7. He hopes the web adopts it.
3. **Design systems and frameworks breed sameness unless they are your own and leave
   room.** In E2 one-offs are dismissed as "snowflakes", in E3 he wants to "design
   outside of those edges", and in E7 he argues for "your boring design system", warns
   against Bootstrap and Material, and proposes "foundation styles". He accepts the
   value of consistency each time.
4. **The web has gone safe and samey.** It is a "cozy cardigan" in E2, Grid demos are
   "shiny" in E4, and the last years were a "drier patch" in E7. He blames tooling,
   CMSs and frameworks, not the medium.
5. **Brand feeling should reach every touchpoint and the smallest details.** Examples
   are the spinner bounce and the list of touchpoints in E3, the bear and Slack in E5,
   and line weights matched to type strokes, echoed shadows and icon and label tone in
   E6. Art direction is the glue between channels (E5, E6).
6. **Principles first, then their visual meaning.** He runs single-word principle
   workshops ("bold, clear, intentional, reassuring") and has the team pin up visual
   references for each word (E3, E6, E7). He agrees with Hay's funnel from values to
   moods and metaphors to visual language (E6).
7. **Art direction belongs in marketing and product, not only editorial.** He makes
   this point with the toothpaste line (E2), email-campaign landing pages (E5), "sell
   the weekend, not the room" (E6), and an "editorial approach" for a fintech product
   (E7). He sees art direction as an activity anyone can do, not a title (E5).
8. **Client feedback should name feelings.** He embraces "It doesn't feel like us"
   (E3), Mall's request for feeling words, and Porter's "judge it after a week" (E2).
9. **Influences outside web design.** These include *The Face* and Brody (E2, E4, E7),
   Scott McCloud's *Understanding Comics* for pace and rhythm (E5), mid-century art
   directors with a foot in fine art such as Brodovitch (E7), and his own fine-art
   training (E7). Collected objects (King Kong posters, Godzilla figures) "stimulate"
   him (E6).
10. **Small screens.** He has no dedicated small-screen method this season. His
    position: mood, feeling and typography survive on the smallest screen, and complex
    grid layout is a progressive enhancement for large screens (E7). Values-led design
    keeps small-screen work from being superficial (E6). One concrete trick is footer
    and navigation links flowing into columns in landscape (E4).
