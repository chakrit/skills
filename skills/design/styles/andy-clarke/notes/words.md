## Findings

### Process: what he does first, what he settles before layout

- **The company's story comes before any visual decision.** [stated] "Before I think
  about illustration, layout, or typography, I carry out a content audit and work through
  the structure of a website to understand the story it needs to tell." (S5) The story
  "isn't your 'about us' or 'company history' page, it's a combination of your
  experience, expertise, and values, plus the reasons people choose you"; "My job isn't
  to invent a story, it's to uncover yours." (S4)
- **Listening and questions first.** [stated] "My process starts with listening. I'll ask
  questions, challenge assumptions, and look for why you or your organisation is
  different from everyone else's." (S4) "That's why I ask questions before I start
  designing." (S2) He publishes a 35-page Hardboiled Client Interview Guide on "how I
  prepare for client conversations, the questions I ask, and how I spot unexpected
  details that can inspire distinctive designs" (S9; the guide itself is a purchase bonus
  and was not read).
- **Then the story drives every element.** [stated] Once the story is found, "the
  colours, illustrations, layouts, and typography we choose will reinforce that
  difference" (S4); "Images communicate ideas and illustrate content, layouts direct
  attention, typography expresses your tone of voice, and motion adds emphasis where it's
  needed." (S4)
- **Purpose and feeling before the brief.** [stated] In 2019 he described first working
  out a product's bigger purpose, then "a designer would come in with a creative brief"
  asking "what typography style is going to convey that message? What kind of layout?
  What kind of color scheme? What kind of graphics" and "how do you want people to feel".
  (S17) For Smashing Magazine's redesign he ran week-long roundtable workshops, three or
  four times, to find "what the Smashing message was", producing words such as "bright
  and bold", "turned up to eleven", "quirky" that a second designer then designed to.
  (S17)
- **Order of work on recent projects.** [stated]
  - Mountain Duck: "I began by mapping the Mountain Duck content and simplifying the
    path between the home and download pages. Once I understood that path, I explored
    ways to bring the Mountain Duck character into the experience"; "The project began
    with wireframes and content planning before moving onto illustration and animation."
    (S7)
  - Lockrose: content audit and structure, then "Once that journey felt right, I began
    sketching ideas for a visual language" (S5); wireframing the user journey, visual
    language, Eleventy templates, CMS (S5).
  - Every illustration: "began life as a pencil sketch before I redrew it as scalable
    vector artwork, animated using SVG and CSS" (S5); "Drawing ideas before moving
    pixels" (S6); Moonwalk Audio "I started by making pencil sketches" (S8).
- **The home page illustration sets the visual language for the rest.** [stated] "The
  Moonwalk Audio homepage illustration established a visual language I could use
  throughout the website." (S8)
- **First ideas get thrown away.** [stated] On Lockrose: "my first ideas weren't the ones
  we eventually built. I explored combining photography with bold graphic elements, but
  the team weren't convinced"; the client did not want photographs of people, so he went
  "back to the drawing board. Literally." (S6)
- **A visual language can evolve during the work rather than be fixed first.** [stated]
  On his own book: "I didn't start with a fixed idea; instead, I let a visual language
  evolve." (S10) Note: this is about a book, not a client website.
- **He designs and builds.** [stated] "I build the websites I design using modern web
  technologies. I don't develop websites designed bu [sic] someone else." (S4) On
  Moonwalk Audio, "I knew I'd be developing the website, so I could develop the
  illustrations and interactions alongside the content and page layouts." (S8)
- **Spends saved build time on content and visuals.** [stated] Using his own Eleventy
  starter meant "I could invest more of the project's budget in content, illustrations,
  and refining the visual language. That's always a trade I'd rather make as clients
  rarely remember which static site generator I used." (S6)
- **Layout and "atmosphere" are separate design layers.** [stated] (2012) "we need a new
  word for what's left when we remove layout from a design. I call what's left
  'atmosphere'"; "I use Fireworks to design atmosphere (typography, color and texture)
  and to develop fine design details, an extra layer of polish, after all of the
  sketching and interactive prototypes are done." (S23)
- **Layout, theme and colour as independent layers (one 2019 project).** [stated] For a
  French football magazine he designed "about half a dozen different layout
  combinations. All based on exactly the same CSS", themes named after French footballers
  covering headlines, block quotes, table headers and buttons, and "six different color
  schemes", giving "a color layer, a theme layer and a layout layer" editors can pick
  from. Fixed data pages such as league tables are left alone. (S17)
- **Real-device design testing.** [stated] (2012) "I find testing my designs on real
  smartphones and tablets while I'm working to be incredibly useful"; designers need only
  "a subset of devices" to "develop an affinity for how our designs work on any type of
  device when we hold it our hands" — "design testing" as distinct from "device testing".
  (S23)

### Reasons given for particular moves

- **Compound grids instead of twelve symmetrical columns.** [stated] Bootstrap-style grids
  "led to a generation of homogenous layouts and uninspiring designs"; "Nine out of ten
  [students] sketches twelve symmetrical columns." (S18) A 2+3 compound gives four
  columns, "the outer two are twice the width of those on the inside … 2|1|1|2"; 3+4
  gives "3|1|2|2|1|3"; 4+6 gives "2|1|1|2|2|1|1|2". (S18) The article's prose also says
  "Overlaying four columns with five … 6|1|4|3|3|4|1|6", which does not match its own 4+6
  figure; recorded as written. Reason for compound grids: flexibility for varied content
  and "visual pacing": "Repeating smaller modules speeds up the movement, while larger
  ones slow it down. People naturally spend longer looking at larger spacial zones." (S18)
  He sells 4+5, 4+6 and 3+4 compound, modular and ratio-based templates (S28). In 2026 he
  calls the twelve-column grid a useful tool "rather than templates that should dictate
  how every website looks." (S9)
- **Stacked grids to separate subjects.** [stated] "When pages contain two separate
  subjects or different types of content, stacking grids can be a great way to make that
  difference more obvious"; his example uses a grey background, bolder type and paragraph
  spacing in place of first-line indents for the second story. (S18)
- **Aligning a pull quote to the grid.** [stated] A quote one six-column-grid column wide
  "feels disconnected from the story"; aligning its edge to six-column grid lines makes it
  "feel part of the story." (S18)
- **The measure decides the layout.** [stated] "rather than allowing layout to dictate the
  measure, doesn't it make more sense for the measure to inform layout decisions?"
  Starting value `--measure: 60ch`, "a baseline, then adjust by eye", because x-height,
  width and tracking change the perceived length; variants `55ch` for captions and
  shorter blocks, `80ch` for "introductions, headings, and hero sections". (S13) Grid
  example `minmax(0, var(--measure)) 1fr`: text locked to the measure, the second column
  takes what remains. (S13) Multi-column text uses `column-width: var(--measure)`. (S13)
- **Breakpoints from the measure, not from devices.** [stated] Device breakpoints "should
  be called 'guesspoints'"; "when a column becomes narrower than a readable line length, a
  layout can collapse," using container queries. (S13) A reader comment on the same page
  disputes that a custom property works inside a container query condition [reported,
  S13 comments].
- **Breaking the grid, sparingly.** [stated] After establishing a grid, "breaking it
  occasionally can be a powerful way to draw attention to key content"; pulling images
  into margins with negative margins gives "a more casual, energetic feel"; "Used
  sparingly, these breaks serve as punctuation." (S15)
- **Inline versus full-bleed images.** [stated] Images inside the column suit "charts,
  diagrams, and infographics" but can feel "predictable and lacking in energy"; full-bleed
  images "act like dramatic pauses" but "lose their impact quickly if they're overused or
  feel like filler." (S15)
- **Modular grids for image groups.** [stated] Rather than stacking images, a modular
  grid allows mixed landscape and portrait images and varied scale, and "groups related
  visuals, reinforcing the relationship between them." (S15)
- **Text wrapped around a cut-out (shape-outside).** [stated] It "slows people down
  slightly, creates visual rhythm" and "brings text and image into a closer
  relationship"; he would start from an image's alpha channel rather than circles: "No
  clipping paths. No polygons." (S15)
- **Captions beside images, styled as pull quotes or side notes.** [stated] "Most
  captions look like afterthoughts to me — small, grey text, tucked beneath a picture";
  "No rule says captions must sit below an image." (S15)
- **Whitespace around images.** [stated] "Tighter spacing is useful when grouping images,
  but it also creates tension. In contrast, generous margins give an image more breathing
  room." (S15)
- **Standfirsts.** [stated] Typographic details "pulled from frameworks and pattern
  libraries" get "very little original thought". A standfirst "should stand apart as well
  as stand out", is "two, three, or four lines long at most … between 20–50 words", and
  need not be larger: "use exaggerated leading … and a deeper margin below it". Borders
  under a standfirst "might be two, three, or four times the width of your underlines".
  Other listed treatments: placed above the headline, outdented with a left border so its
  text aligns with the columns below, floated into a wide left margin and right-aligned,
  rotated on its side, set over a full-screen image. Its look comes from the
  publication's style and "sometimes the article's subject". (S21)
- **Scale relative to other elements.** [stated] "Sheer size is rarely enough on its own
  … largeness must be relative to the size of other elements"; a huge wheel image makes
  the Beetle look small, and he wants "a similar contrast in scale on small screens."
  (S19)
- **Pattern, texture, gradients and shadows against flat design.** [stated] Flat design's
  reduction "has had unfortunate repercussions … a regrettable uniformity"; he advocates
  "gradients, patterns, shadows appropriately to tell stories and provide greater
  affordance", while "not advocating a return to the worst excesses of skeuomorphism".
  (S19) The 24ways piece "Z's Still Not Dead" (2019) is summarised on his index as
  bringing flat design "back to life … with … a sense of depth" (S12; article not read).
- **Illustration in place of photography, drawn from a reference.** [stated] Lockrose:
  vintage Graphis issues, "Flat colours. Strong shapes. Clear compositions. They had
  personality without feeling decorative"; shapes built on intersecting triangles that
  "echoed the company's own identity and gave every illustration a consistent underlying
  structure." (S6) "Mid-century modernist magazine covers were the inspiration behind my
  illustrations for the Lockrose website." (S5)
- **Illustration that carries the idea, not decoration.** [stated] Lockrose's process
  sequence goes from pencil sketch to finished artwork across tabs: "The illustrations
  don't simply decorate the website—they reinforce its central idea that successful
  projects develop through clearly defined stages." (S6)
- **Unifying unrelated objects.** [stated] Moonwalk Audio: "black outlines, geometric
  shading, and a limited colour palette" to make disparate toys belong to one world;
  kept imprecise because "making everything too precise would've taken away the
  playfulness." (S8)
- **Interaction instead of conventional players.** [stated] Moonwalk Audio: monochrome
  objects turn full colour on hover and play sound on press, because the company makes
  sound for interactive experiences and he wanted something "that encouraged people to
  explore rather than scroll." (S8)
- **A mascot from the logo.** [stated] Moonwalk Audio's microphone-headed logo motif
  became "a mascot which appears in different poses across the site" (S8); the Mountain
  Duck character "appears throughout the site, guiding visitors while adding warmth and
  humour" (S7).
- **Ambient animation.** [stated] Subtle, slow, looping motion "that add[s] atmosphere
  without stealing the show". Choose what moves by asking "Does this thing have weight?",
  "Is it flexible?", "Would it move in real life?"; feathers swing "three degrees". Use
  "longer durations", ease-in-out or relaxed cubic Béziers, smooth loops; layer several
  subtle animations "like building a sound mix"; "If someone's eyes are drawn to a raised
  eyebrow, it's probably too much"; "reserve the most obvious animations for elements
  that I need to create the biggest impact"; wrap in prefers-reduced-motion. (S16) Lockrose
  examples: rotating turbine blades and a glow around the nuclear station, "without being
  a distraction." (S5) His AI rules: "Motion should be subtle, purposeful, and
  non-disruptive." (S26)
- **Typography with more personality than the client had.** [stated] Lockrose:
  "introduced typography with more personality than the generic typeface used
  previously." (S5) Mountain Duck: "replacing generic framework components with custom
  typography, animations, and illustrations." (S7) No typeface is named for either.
- **Typeface for his own site and book.** [stated] Obviously by OH no Type Co., "the same
  typeface I use on my website, and I chose it because it has plenty of styles and
  weights." (S10)
- **Book grids.** [stated] "a six-column grid for the running text and a modular grid for
  arranging groups of images" (suggested by Alex); square colour chips "every time I
  mention colour"; hand-drawn page designs "so they don't distract from the colour or
  typography examples"; book colours match the Wheelman red, white and blue palette.
  (S10)
- **A 2011 custom grid.** [stated] "I've never been entirely happy with grid frameworks
  such as the 960 Grid System, so I start almost every project by creating a custom
  grid." One project: "84px columns and 24px gutters", giving "an odd number of columns
  at common tablet and desktop widths" and room for 300px advertising. (S24)

### Phones

- [stated] Collapsing to one column loses hierarchy: "rhythm becomes monotonous … pages
  turn from being designed experiences into content feeds." Instead "treat each section
  as a distinct composition, a designed moment." (S14)
- [stated] A desktop modular grid of album covers becomes a horizontal scroller on small
  screens via a container query, "a way to give grouped content its own stage, the way a
  magazine spread might." (S14) Same move in 2019: six Beetles in a horizontal scrolling
  panel so they are not shrunk and people do not scroll past all six before the content.
  (S19)
- [stated] Below a container width of `48rem`, two columns become a sideways scroller,
  each column `85%` wide so part of the next shows, "hints that there's more to see."
  (S14)
- [stated] A shape-outside layout that "feels cramped" on small screens is rebuilt as a
  sideways image-text-image sequence with scroll snapping, which "slows someone's reading
  speed by making each swipe an intentional action." (S14)
- [stated] Landscape orientation is "an opportunity to recompose a layout, not just
  reflow it"; orientation media queries add columns or switch on the text-wrap effect
  only in landscape. (S14) In 2019 he swapped portrait and landscape images by
  orientation: "a fabulous, but seldom used media query." (S19)
- [stated] "Small screens don't make design more difficult; they make it more
  deliberate"; the aim is "not to mimic miniature magazines on mobile, but to capture
  their energy, rhythm, and sense of discovery." (S14)
- [stated] The small-screens article was written in answer to a reader who called his
  guides "mostly impractical in real life" for responsive work (S14).

### What he refuses or dislikes, and why

- **Websites that look alike.** [stated] "Too many websites look alike … changing the
  logo, colours, and photographs would be enough to make a website for one company look
  like it belongs to another." Familiar patterns are not "inherently wrong" but designers
  "have become too comfortable relying on them." "I don't believe every website needs to
  be unusual or experimental." (S9)
- **Framework defaults.** [stated] "I get so disappointed when I see stuff which is not a
  gajillion miles away from framework default in terms of layout or button styles or type
  hierarchy … that's like completely missing the point of the design." (S17) Mountain
  Duck: "Nothing visitors see was inherited from a framework." (S7) 2016: the "pour your
  content into a framework template" school "is't [sic] good enough." (S22)
- **Templates and trends.** [stated] "The websites and products I create aren't built
  around templates or trends." (S4)
- **Difference for its own sake.** [stated] Different layouts "not just for difference
  sake, but to better communicate the meaning of our content and to tell better stories."
  (S22) "good design isn't about making businesses look different. It's about helping
  people understand what already makes them different." (S6)
- **Personality at the cost of clarity.** [stated] "personality should never come at the
  expense of clarity. Every illustration, animation, and interaction needs to support a
  user's journey and not distract from it." (S7)
- **Showing clients static visuals for sign-off.** [stated] (2012) Static visuals are
  "like bringing a knife to a gunfight" and "set the wrong expectations"; "Work in
  Photoshop and Fireworks, by all means (I do) … Just don't … [use] them as sign-off
  artefacts." He blames fixed 960px sites on clients signing off 960px visuals. (S23)
- **Prescriptive desktop-only wireframes signed off before design.** [stated] "I've a
  problem with any part of the process that limits our potential for great design. This
  includes wireframes — typically desktop-only wireframes — that are produced and often
  signed off by a client long before they arrive in the design studio." (S23)
- **Unstructured client feedback and the "big reveal".** [stated] "Don't email pictures
  of websites to your clients and then ask for their 'thoughts.' Are you mad?!"; "Don't
  wait until after weeks of work before having a 'big reveal.'" (S23)
- **AI as a producer of finished artwork.** [stated] His 2026 talk uses AI "as a reference
  rather than a replacement … Rather than asking AI to produce finished artwork, Andy uses
  it for visual research." (S27) On AI coding tools he says "I feel deeply conflicted"
  but uses them; machines "use too many class attributes and overly specific selectors"
  and "wrap everything in <div> elements." (S26)

### How he works with clients

- [stated] Direct contact: "you'll work directly with me. There are no layers between us";
  "I'll sometimes ask awkward questions and challenge your assumptions, but I'll always
  explain the thinking behind every design decision." (S2, S3)
- [stated] "I don't specialise in a specific industry. I specialise in uncovering what
  makes organisations distinctive." (S3) Favourite projects often come from independent
  developers who need a designer. (S2)
- [stated] Week-long design sprints at a fixed £4,400 per week; most websites take two to
  four weeks. He also writes or edits copy. (S4) Lockrose took one month, Mountain Duck
  two months (S5, S7).
- [stated] (2012) Keep clients involved at every step: work alongside them, shared
  folders, a Skype window "continually open"; structured face-to-face feedback workshops
  or crits limited in time and scope, with a written record; "only the people who show up
  to a workshop may have an opinion about the design"; "you are the designer … never
  underestimate your role"; clients told the reasoning "are far less likely to request
  unnecessary changes." He delivered "responsive templates" rather than static visuals.
  (S23)
- [stated] Technical clients: "technical businesses tend to believe that their
  credentials, facts, and service lists are enough. Trust me. They're not." (S6)
  "One of the biggest mistakes software companies make is assuming that professionalism
  and personality don't belong together." (S7)
- [reported] Client testimonials on his site: "If you want something bold and different
  I highly would recommend Stuff & Nonsense" (Peter O'Flynn, Codeboxx); "super light and
  very mobile friendly" (Mike Worth); "Everyone mentions how his design perfectly tells
  my story" (Ryan Knaggs); "our end product has far exceeded our expectations" (Lyndsey
  Evans, Lockrose). (S1, S2, S5) These are chosen by Clarke for his own site.

### What he believes about design in general

- [stated] "Every business has a story worth telling"; "every design decision should help
  tell it." (S1, S2) "Every decision that we make in terms of design is an opportunity to
  tell a story … even … the style and the wording of microcopy." (S17)

### Where he changed his mind

- **Fluid grids (2011).** [stated] "not long after I started working responsively I
  realized that the formula shouldn't (always) be a one-fix, set-and-forget calculation";
  he moved to a separate set of percentages per breakpoint. (S24)
- **Device breakpoints to measure-based ones (2011 → 2025).** [stated] In 2011 he chose
  "three 320 and Up breakpoints above 320px" (S24). In 2025: device breakpoints are "a
  hard habit to break and one which, to be honest, I haven't always managed myself";
  he now lets the measure decide. (S13)
- **max-width to logical properties (by 2025).** [stated] "Although it took time to
  retrain my muscle memory, I now use CSS logical properties." (S13)
- **Static visuals (2009 → 2012).** [reported] Meagan Fisher (2009) credits Clarke's
  "Time to stop showing clients static design visuals" and "Walls Come Tumbling Down" with
  "a fantastic case for creating your mockups in markup." (S25) [stated] In 2011 he wrote
  "designing in a browser makes the best sense" (S24); in 2012 he still used Fireworks
  for atmosphere and detail but not for sign-off (S23).
- **Turned to print after boredom with the web (before 2019).** [stated] "A few years ago,
  I became incredibly bored with what was passing for creative design on the web at the
  time, and so I started to look elsewhere." (S20)
- **Examples too editorial (2018 → 2026).** [stated] Feedback that Art Direction for the
  Web's examples "felt too editorial" was "fair criticism"; Standout Designs for the Web
  applies the same thinking to "an online shop, a product page, or even something as
  ordinary as a form." (S9)
- **Short "shots" (2019 → 2026).** [stated] In 2019 he "threw the whole three shots idea
  out of the window" (S17); in 2026 the new book is the first of "Hardboiled Web Design
  Shots", around 180 pages (S9). This concerns book format, not design.
- **Print-first to digital editions.** [stated] "When I wrote my first books, I was only
  concerned about how the printed copies would look. It wasn't until Art Direction for
  the Web that I even thought about the digital versions." (S10)
- **AI tools (2026).** [stated] Moved from asking ChatGPT and DeepSeek to debug, to Codex
  with an AGENTS.md, while "deeply conflicted." (S26)
