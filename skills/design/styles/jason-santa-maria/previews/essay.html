No. 11 ◆ Winter 2026 ◆ Indigo Essay

The Vat Keeps Its Own Time

Ilse Brannock

Cloth comes out of an indigo vat green, and for about a minute it is the colour of pond weed in June.

Ilse Brannock

dyes wool and linen on a hill farm in the Welsh Marches.

Eleven dips ◆ 14 min

Then the air gets to it. You lift a length of linen out of the vat and it hangs from your hands the colour of something alive, a wet yellow-green that has no business being near a blue. Oxygen finds the dye in the fibres and you watch the green go teal, then slate, then the first pale sky of a winter morning. Nothing in the workshop moves faster than that change, and nothing else in my working life asks me to stand still and simply look.

I have been dyeing with indigo for nineteen years. For the first five I fought the vat. I fed it when I thought it was hungry and warmed it when I thought it was cold, and I lost three vats to impatience before an older dyer in Ludlow told me the thing I should have known from the start: a vat is a slow fermentation, and it keeps its own time.

1A fermentation vat reduces indigo with bacteria and a food source (madder root, bran, fruit) in an alkaline bath. It takes days to wake and can live for years if kept.

This essay is about learning to work at that speed. It is also about the eleven dips it takes to reach the blue I want, and why I stopped trying to get there in fewer.

Reading the flower

Every morning the vat shows you its face. When it is well, a skin of coppery foam gathers in the middle of the surface, bubbles packed together and shining like the inside of a mussel shell. Dyers call this the flower. A good flower is tight and bronze, with a purple edge; a poor one is grey and loose, or not there at all.

Under the flower the liquid should be a clear yellow-green, the same green the cloth will show when it first comes out. If the bath looks blue, the indigo has already oxidised and will sit on the surface of the fibre instead of going into it. A blue vat needs feeding and a day of rest, not more cloth.

2I skim the flower off before each session and lay it back on the surface after, so the vat stays covered from the air overnight.

I read the vat with my nose too. A healthy one smells sweet and a little sour, like a bakery bin. When it smells of drains I have overfed it, and when it smells of nothing it has gone to sleep. My notebook for this vat has eleven years of entries and most of them are about smell.

Eleven dips

A single long soak does not make a deep blue. The cloth takes what it can in the first few minutes and after that it only gets heavy. Each dip is a short visit: in for three minutes, out to the air for twenty, rinsed, and back in. The colour builds from many of them, laid one over the next, the way a wall gets its colour from coats of limewash.

Over the years I have settled on eleven. The first four take a length of white linen to a clear mid-blue that most people would happily wear. The next four slow down, each adding less than the one before. The last three are for a depth that only shows when you hold the cloth next to the eighth dip and see the difference.

3Twenty minutes in the air is a minimum. On cold days in the barn I leave it half an hour, because oxidation slows down with the temperature.

The strips in the drawing above hang in my workshop as a gauge. When a customer asks for a particular blue, I show them the row and they point. Almost everyone points at six or seven. Almost no one points at eleven until they have handled it, and then they understand why it costs what it does.

Fewer, longer dips are possible with a stronger vat, and plenty of good dyers work that way. I tried it for a season. The blues were darker sooner and they rubbed off on the next chair you sat in. Indigo laid down slowly stays where it is put.

What the cloth remembers

A length dyed in eleven dips carries the whole session in it. Hold it to a window and the weave shows faint bands where one dip met the air a little unevenly. I used to rinse and re-dip to hide them. Now I leave them, because they are a record of the day: the cold that slowed the third oxidation, the minute I was called to the door during the seventh.

The vat I use now was started in March 2015 with a handful of madder root and a bucket of last year’s bath. It has outlived two barn roofs and one marriage. When I go away I ask a neighbour to stir it once a day and look at the flower, and she sends me a photograph of the surface every evening, without comment, which is the right way to report on a vat.

4Madder root feeds the bacteria and gives a little warmth to the blue. Some dyers use dates or henna instead.

It would be quicker to buy reduced indigo and make a chemical vat in an afternoon. People do, and their cloth is blue. I keep the slow vat because working at its pace has changed how I do everything else in the workshop. I weigh more carefully. I write things down. I stand at the rail for the minute the linen is green and I do not answer the phone.
