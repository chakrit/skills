Sawyer

A gothic in four widths, drawn from the lumber sign on a brick warehouse at the north end of Front Avenue.

Designed by Ruth Okafor & Dale Ennis · 4 widths · 9 weights · 72 styles

Dale walked past the sign every morning for eleven years on his way to the shop on Interstate. The warehouse sold fir and cedar until 1974 and has been a parts depot, a band rehearsal space and nothing in particular since. Nobody we asked knows who cut the letters. They were sawn from inch-thick boards, painted the colour of the moss that now grows over them, and bolted on with carriage screws you can still count from the street.

When the owners told us the building was being sold, we spent two Saturdays on a ladder with a tape and a roll of butcher paper. Rubbings of six letters became the first drawings. The rest of the alphabet we had to work out from those six: how a saw handles a curve, where a sign painter would cheat a joint, what an S looks like when someone cuts it with a coping saw in a cold shed.

Sawyer is the result of two years of that. The Extra Condensed is closest to the sign and the one we reach for at poster sizes. The narrower widths keep the sawn corners; the wider ones soften them, so the Regular can set a menu or a sign-up form without shouting. Every width runs from a Hairline you could cut with a knife to a Black that looks bolted to the wall, and the variable fonts cover everything between.

The family has small caps, old-style and tabular figures, arrows for wayfinding and the accents for every Latin-script language we could find a reader for. There is an R with a straight leg for anyone who finds ours too lively. We would rather you didn’t use it.

“Two Saturdays on a ladder with a tape and a roll of butcher paper.”
