## 1. Moves

Each move: what it is, where he used it, the default it replaces, his stated reason,
and when it fits.

### Move 1. Replace the 12-column grid with a compound grid

- **What.** Overlay two grids of different column counts at the same width and gutter.
  3+4 gives six columns in the rhythm 3|1|2|2|1|3; 4+5 gives eight columns,
  6|1|4|3|3|4|1|6 (CSS `grid-template-columns: 6fr 1fr 4fr 3fr 3fr 4fr 1fr 6fr`). Take
  widths from either parent grid, or combine them into widths that belong to neither.
- **Where.** His 2021 site (every page, including the hero illustration's internal
  proportions); his 2018 site used 4/6; Zombie Hunt; RSPCA and Crisis redesigns;
  the Standout Designs book; the Layout Love templates.
  (`using-a-4-5-compound-grid`, `how-a-3-4-compound-grid-can-improve-on-12-columns`,
  `designing-zombie-hunt-part-three-designing-a-layout-grid`, `his-name-is-fast-eddie`)
- **Default replaced.** A Bootstrap-style 12 or 16 even columns used only to align
  edges.
- **His reason [said].** Even columns are a monotonous drum beat: "Think of each column
  as a beat". The cliché is caused less by twelve columns than by their even ratio and
  symmetry, which shape how designers think. A compound grid "will fundamentally alter
  how you think about layout". He admits a 12-column grid *can* produce the same
  results, but says starting from it would not have broken his habits.
- **Personality per parent grid [said].** A 3-column grid feels editorial and
  informal; a 4-column grid packs text into tighter, more serious blocks; 5 columns
  are rare online and feel unfamiliar; mixing 4 and 5 on one page lets one section
  feel tense (5, asymmetric) and the next reassuring (4, balanced). In the RSPCA
  article he used an asymmetric 5-column introduction to add tension to a sad puppy's
  story, then a balanced 4-column section for the happy ending.
- **Fits.** Any site that needs several page types to look different yet related (a
  small business with home, menu, story, and contact pages). Doesn't fit when the
  content is one column of running text and nothing else.

### Move 2. Use the skinny columns as precise offsets and whitespace

- **What.** The narrow columns a compound grid produces (the 1s in 6|1|4|3...) are
  used not for content but to offset a headline, widen an image by exactly one sliver,
  indent a pull quote or inline image, or leave a gap that separates main content from
  supplementary content.
- **Where.** `how-a-3-4-compound-grid...` (headline offset, image widened by a skinny
  column, pull-quote indent); the 2021 home page, where "one of my thinnest columns"
  separates the hero art from a single column of copy (`using-a-4-5-compound-grid`).
- **Default replaced.** Arbitrary padding values and equal gutters everywhere.
- **His reason [said].** It gives whitespace a reason and a consistent measure, and it
  connects images and quotes to the text without interrupting reading.
- **Fits.** Everywhere a compound grid is used; this is the detail that makes the grid
  visible without drawing it.

### Move 3. Modular grids with spatial zones, and empty modules on purpose

- **What.** Divide the page into rows as well as columns. Fill one module, or merge
  several into a "spatial zone". Size of zone equals rank in the hierarchy. Leave some
  modules empty.
- **Where.** RSPCA stories index (the most-read story takes six of eight columns in the
  top field; second-tier stories take four; the big one moves left, right, or centre
  down the page), Crisis Christmas page (donation form in four of twelve modules next
  to a donut chart), Standout Designs p.114 (a tool shop where combined modules give a
  pair of pliers or a chisel a tall zone), Academy of Scoring Arts video library.
  (`modular-grids-for-visually-appealing-layouts`, `rspca-...`, `crisis-...`, sample PDF)
- **Default replaced.** A uniform 3×3 card grid that "creates the appearance of
  structure" but no hierarchy and no suggestion of what to read first **[said, RSPCA]**.
- **His reasons [said].** Modules bring order to lots of varied content *and* make a
  bold layout out of very little content. More, smaller modules speed reading and feel
  busier; fewer, larger ones are places to linger, so module count sets the *pace* of a
  page. An empty module "can be just as important as one filled". Use the largest zone
  once per page.
- **Fits.** Product lists, menus, galleries, story or news indexes, portfolios: the
  exact places where the AI default puts identical cards.

### Move 4. Ratio-derived columns instead of "how many of 12?"

- **What.** Pick a ratio from Wolfgang von Wersin's orthogons (biauron 1:1.236, diagon
  1:1.414, golden 1:1.618, sixton 1:1.732, doppelquadrat 1:2) and grow each column from
  the previous one by that ratio, then reorder the columns freely.
- **Where.** `take-the-guesswork-out-of-layout-by-using-ratios`, Layout Love ratio sets.
- **Default replaced.** Deciding whether a sidebar gets 3 or 4 of 12 columns by eye.
- **His reason [said].** Framework grids give no help with proportional relationships.
  Low ratios give subtle differences suited to academic or editorial content; high
  ratios add drama "for designs with a strong purpose". 1:1 feels harmonious and
  unchallenging, which is why it is everywhere.
- **Note [seen].** His published fr values contain an arithmetic slip (1.236 squared is
  1.528, not the 1.262 he prints), so copy the method, not his numbers.

### Move 5. Make the grid carry meaning: symmetry versus asymmetry

- **What.** Choose symmetry or asymmetry and the steepness of the type hierarchy for
  what they *say* about the client, not by habit.
- **Where.** `election-2024-diving-into-party-website-designs`.
- **His reason [said].** Symmetry reads as predictable, reassuring, stable; asymmetry as
  challenging, opinionated, radical, energetic. Big bold type with a steep hierarchy is
  "unapologetic"; small type with a shallow hierarchy is less challenging; jumbled type
  suggests muddled thinking.
- **Fits.** Use as a decision question per client: is this shop reassuring (a clinic,
  an accountant) or energetic (a street food stall, a gym)?

### Move 6. Dethrone the full-width hero banner

- **What.** Treat the full-width banner image as a filler to justify or remove. Replace
  it with the client's key message or proof in that space, narrow the image, or make
  the image and the message a single composition.
- **Where.** All four election redesigns (Tories' "plan" message moved from below the
  fold into the banner; Labour's five pledges moved from the page bottom to the top and
  the banner narrowed); RSPCA, Crisis, Standout Designs ("full-width hero banners have
  taken over the web like an invasive species" **[said, sample p.6]**); his own 2021
  home page, where "this composition avoids the conventional full-width header"
  **[said]**.
- **Default replaced.** The centred hero photo with a headline and a button.
- **His reason [said].** "Full-width banners aren't inherently bad, but using them as
  filler or because everyone else has them is." He admits his own current site has one.
- **[seen]** His live 2026 home page does lead with a full-width illustrated band, but
  it is a bespoke cartoon scene, not a stock photo, which is where he draws the line.

### Move 7. Turn the page into a printed object from the client's world

- **What.** Find a printed or broadcast artefact the client's audience already knows
  and make the page *be* that artefact: a magazine cover, a tabloid front, a teen
  magazine, a pulp cover, a cartoon title card, a 1930s serial, a 1960s magazine page.
- **Where [seen].**
  - Wheelman after-redesign home page is a magazine cover: masthead, cover lines set as
    tilted white tickets, one large cover portrait, "Subscribe" panel, a diagonal band
    leading into the next section (Standout sample p.11).
  - RSPCA stories for children become a teen-magazine spread: tilted photo frames, a
    round sticker headline overlapping the photo, rotated icon stickers, slightly
    skewed label bars, a gradient mixed from two brand colours, and a repeated
    "ANIMAL STORIES" title band with one instance highlighted (`img-rspca/ex3.png`).
  - Conservatives redesign: newspaper-collage background, ransom-note mix of type with
    single words in boxes, torn paper edges (`post-election-...-1400.png`).
  - Count Binface: video thumbnails and player styled after 1930s Flash Gordon
    serials, Comicraft lettering.
  - His own 2004 site: a yellowed 1970 magazine page; 2006: blog as a magazine issue.
  - Toon Titles: Yogi Bear title cards rebuilt in CSS and SVG, shown inside TV sets.
- **Default replaced.** A neutral web page that could belong to anyone.
- **His reason [said].** Look "literally everywhere other than the web" (memo, 2019);
  he chose the teen-magazine idea because the RSPCA's stories section targets kids and
  their magazines are "optimistic, even joyful". "Keeping consistent doesn't mean
  things must look the same, but they must feel connected." **[said, RSPCA]**
- **Fits.** Strongest when the source is genuinely part of the audience's life. For a
  Thai SME the equivalents would be the printed menu, the market-stall sign, the
  shop's own packaging, Thai film posters, comics, or temple-fair posters.

### Move 8. Display type as the composition

- **What.** Headlines set huge, stacked, condensed, in capitals, sometimes in two
  colours, often occupying a full column or half the screen; individual words boxed,
  rotated, or placed in speech-bubble clouds; dates and numbers exaggerated; standfirsts
  rotated on their side; captions made "larger, louder".
- **Where [seen].** RSPCA article ("PUPPY REGGIE GETS A NEW HOME" filling the right
  half beside an extreme close-up, `img-rspca/ex4.png`); 2021 home ("OUR DESIGNS
  DELIVER"); Wheelman pages ("HEIST" in a cloud, word-tile headlines, "PUT A ROOF OVER
  PHILYS' HEAD" with the name in red); live home service lockups that pair a script
  line ("Design a new") with condensed caps ("WEBSITE or PRODUCT"), like film-title
  lettering. On the 2025 site the four service tiles were each a different
  lockup: a badge numeral, a script phrase, condensed caps with an icon, and a tall
  stack (`wb-2025-1400.png`). The default would be four identical icon-title-text
  cards. Also Toon Titles and Toon Text.
- **[said]** Standfirst article lists fifteen treatments, including placing the
  standfirst *above* the headline, out-denting it with a thick left border, floating it
  into a wide margin, exaggerated leading instead of bigger size, deconstructing and
  rotating each line, and turning it on its side. Standout sample p.150: "Small dates
  can make a big impact"; list markers, ordered-list numerals, form labels, and
  captions all deserve design. But: "when everything is loud, nothing gets heard."
- **[said]** He picks typefaces partly for their range of weights and widths (chose
  Barlow Condensed over Bebas Neue because Bebas has one weight; uses condensed
  versions on small screens and wider versions when space allows; Bankside Sans for
  its extreme widths). He favours condensed wood-type grotesques (Dharma Gothic,
  Compacta, Barlow Condensed, Block Berthold) for display.
- **Fits.** Nearly always for at least one element per page. Thai script has no
  capitals and no true condensed widths in most families, so this move needs adapting
  (weight, scale, tight leading, colour splits, Latin numerals) rather than copying.

### Move 9. Numerals and data as the hero

- **What.** Pull the numbers out of body text and set them as oversized display type,
  with designed symbols (smaller "k", lighter "+" and "%", raised or baseline), tighter
  tracking at large sizes, heavier display cuts when reversed out; replace stock charts
  with brand-specific pictograms (houses of different sizes for rough sleepers by
  nation; one box enlarged for London's 61% rise).
- **Where.** Crisis redesign; Greens ("super-sized numerals"); Labour ("1.5m new homes"
  as giant figures); Wheelman infographic page. (`crisis-...`, `election-...`)
- **His reason [said].** Content should be illustrated "in ways that resonate with a
  brand's identity" rather than with off-the-shelf charts; numbers should "demand to be
  noticed". He let ChatGPT list the numerical data in a page before designing it.
- **Fits.** Any SME with a proof point: years in business, dishes served, rating,
  price. Usually the AI default buries these in a "stats strip" of four equal tiles.

### Move 10. Image as drama: extreme crops, scale sequences, cut-outs breaking frames

- **What.** Crop to the eyes; make a sequence of photos progressively larger and
  rotated; let cut-out figures stand in front of and overlap a panel edge; desaturate
  and vignette a background photo and arrange content blocks around its focal point.
- **Where [seen].** RSPCA (zoom into Reggie's eyes "amps up the drama", ball-chasing
  sequence growing and rotating); Crisis (same eye crop, monochrome vignette);
  Wheelman (seven cut-out "old lags" standing across the bottom edge of an article
  panel, Standout sample p.124); 2004 home (cut-out scooter riders breaking the top
  edge); 2021 home (the gorilla stands in front of the giant headline and hides parts
  of its letters, so figure and type form one layered picture); 2018 home (body copy
  wraps around the gorilla's outline).
- **[said, Zombie Hunt part four]** On small screens he prefers re-cropping (picture
  element or clip-path) to shrinking, to keep the image's impact.
- **Fits.** When the client has even a few real photos. Crop choice tells the story.

### Move 11. One personality thread, many looks

- **What.** Each page or section gets its own composition, but a thread (palette, type
  family, grid, illustration style, recurring mascot) stitches them together.
- **Where.** Standout sample p.163: "Consistency doesn't mean making everything look
  the same"; nine Wheelman pages each with a different layout, all navy, red, white,
  condensed caps. His 2021 portfolio pages all look different but share the 5-column
  half of his compound grid **[said]**. Book: square colour chips every time colour is
  mentioned; hand-drawn page thumbnails so examples don't distract **[said]**.
- **Default replaced.** One template repeated with different content.
- **Fits.** Always; it is the antidote to both sameness and chaos.

### Move 12. A bespoke character or illustrated world

- **What.** Commission or draw a character or scene specific to the client, give it a
  name and poses, and reuse it across the site; prefer this to generic tech-style
  illustration.
- **Where.** Every redesign of his own site (mods, apes, chimps, Errol, Fast Eddie,
  cowboys); Moonwalk Audio (the client's own toys and cardboard scenery drawn into one
  explorable world; monochrome objects turn full colour on hover and play sounds);
  Mountain Duck (mascot guides visitors); Lockrose (an energy landscape with turbine
  animation; process shown as one scene evolving from pencil sketch to finished art);
  Count Binface (rising illustration of the Count).
- **[seen]** The character can be re-dressed over time: the 2026 cowboy hero
  appears in snow in a January capture and in a flowering desert now, with play,
  snow, and flower buttons in the corner. Fast Eddie was recoloured for the light,
  dark, and Easter-egg themes **[said]**.
- **His reason [said].** "If you stay clear of the generic style so popular with tech
  companies, illustrations can add real personality." Software companies wrongly assume
  "professionalism and personality don't belong together."
- **Fits.** When budget allows one illustration system. For SMEs, the shop's own
  objects (as with Moonwalk's toys) are a cheap, specific starting point.

### Move 13. Self-imposed constraints when redesigning

- **What.** In his unsolicited redesigns he keeps the client's grid, typeface, colours,
  and existing content, and still produces something distinctive.
- **Where.** Labour (kept the 12-column grid, Poppins, Labour red, existing icons and
  images); RSPCA (kept brand colours and typefaces); Academy of Scoring Arts (a plain
  12-column grid).
- **[said, Academy]** "Even a conventional grid can feel distinctive when it's used
  with intent." **[said, Standout]** "There's nothing inherently boring about a
  twelve-column grid, a hero banner, a row of cards"; the problem is choosing them
  "simply because they're familiar".
- **Why it matters for us.** Proof that the distinctiveness comes from hierarchy,
  scale, crop, type, and composition decisions, not from exotic components.

### Move 14. Restrained palettes, tuned by eye

- **What.** One or two hues plus a dark that is not pure black and a light that is not
  pure white; extra tints derived from one base colour; desaturate theory-derived
  colours until they "feel right"; colours sampled from a film, poster, or client
  artefact.
- **Where.** Zombie Hunt (replaced vibrating complementary red and green with an
  analogous red, desaturated split-complementary and triadic picks, made a yellow by
  screen-blending two colours, recoloured stock illustrations to match); Greens (five
  greens became one); Elemez (palette from screenshots of Scorsese's *Hugo*); his 2021
  site (red, black, white light theme; two blues dark theme; illustrations recoloured
  per theme); 2004 tint technique; Apple TV artwork (gradients sampled from original
  Japanese Godzilla posters).
- **[said]** Pure black on pure white is too high-contrast; check contrast from the
  start, not at the end.

### Move 15. Small screens: keep the art direction, change the arrangement

- **What.** Keep the same grid family on small screens with fewer columns; re-crop
  rather than shrink images; reorder so critical content is near the top without losing
  the hero's impact (horizontal scrolling panels as one option); keep nav visible when
  it is short; protect the visual hierarchy so a big banner doesn't become smaller than
  inline images.
- **Where.** `designing-zombie-hunt-part-four-catering-for-small-screens`,
  `paying-attention-to-content-hierarchy-across-screen-sizes` (2012), `one-step-beyond`
  (breakpoints set by the width of each dancing character, not by devices).
- **[said]** Mobile-first "has never particularly suited my approach"; he makes things
  fluid instead: type sized for every screen and rarely enlarged, viewport units for
  whitespace, layout added only where needed. Small screens are "one of my favourite
  parts of the job".
- **[seen, `live-home-390.png`]** On his live site at 390 px the cowboy hero crops to
  the central group rather than shrinking the whole posse, and the four service
  lockups become a horizontal scroller. On the 2022 site at 390 px
  (`wb-2022-390.png`) the hero (gorilla plus headline) is one SVG and scales as a
  unit, keeping its composition, while the "25 YEARS' WEB DESIGN EXPERIENCE. NO JOKE"
  column becomes a full-width headline in huge condensed caps. The scale contrast
  survives on the phone and even grows.

### Move 16. Art-directed portfolio panels, not screenshots

- **What.** Represent each project with a designed panel in the project's own colours
  and character instead of a browser screenshot.
- **Where.** 2009 redesign (`method-in-my-modness`); 2021 portfolio stories each with
  the client's palette (`his-name-is-fast-eddie`).
- **Fits.** Menus, case studies, product showcases for SMEs.

### Move 17. Hidden Easter eggs and jokes

- **What.** Extra characters at very wide viewports, an orange Easter-egg theme, a
  retro Cartoon Network homage hidden in a client site, a tongue-in-cheek "help page
  for the help page" footer, a cheeky ketchup-bottle modal that turns green on a valid
  email.
- **[said]** "I love to hide Easter eggs in my client work."
- **Fits.** When the brand has humour; never at the cost of the main path.

## 2. Process and thinking

- **Art direction defined [said, art-directing-the-web 2016].** Not responsive images
  and not per-post layouts, which are results; it is distilling the essential meaning
  of a piece of content and conveying it through design. He frames the industry as
  designing "which one of the two possible websites" and calls the alternative the
  "pour your content into a framework template" school.
- **Start with the story, not the style [said, about page, Lockrose, Binface,
  Standout].** Every client has a story; he begins with conversation and a content
  audit, then looks for "something distinctive about each client, whether that's their
  business, personality, products, subject, or sometimes a seemingly insignificant
  detail" and uses it as the starting point (Standout sample p.164). "My job isn't to
  impose a style." He ships a 35-page Hardboiled Client Interview Guide with the new
  book (not read; paid).
- **Concrete artefacts from the client [said, Moonwalk].** He asked Moonwalk Audio for
  objects (vintage robots, a plastic dragon, pencils, cardboard scenery) and built the
  concept from them.
- **Briefs [said, 2018].** A brief should state goals, challenges, opportunities, even
  dreams; it must not contain solutions. A "sites we like" section is like haircut
  photos on a barber's wall: never acknowledge it; the client is hiring you to solve
  their problem, not copy someone else's solution. Phone the person who wrote a
  prescriptive brief; ask "why". Design for business goals first. A brief should have
  one voice.
- **Sequence on his own redesigns [said, 2018 and 2021].** Write and rewrite the copy
  for weeks first; then sketch on paper; pick the grid by experimenting; design
  "desktop" layouts in black and grey with placeholder art and cursive typefaces while
  exploring composition; then design in the browser; add colour and final
  illustration later. "I often work in black and white … while experimenting with ideas
  for layout compositions" (`using-a-4-5-compound-grid`).
- **Atmosphere versus layout [said, 2012 Smashing Book 3 extract].** Colour, type, and
  texture form a design's "atmosphere", which survives every screen size; layout is a
  separate decision. Clients who say "I don't like the design" often mean the layout.
- **Show work in the browser [said, 2008 and ISO 2012].** Static mock-ups set false
  expectations; working pages crush approval time. At ISO he worked in the client's
  meeting room for half of each week, in one-week sprints per site area, with the grid
  derived from the proportions of the ISO logo.
- **Unsolicited redesigns as marketing and practice [seen].** Election, RSPCA, Crisis:
  each starts with an audit of what is generic on the current site and on competitors'
  sites, states his self-imposed constraints, shows original and transformation side
  by side, and ends with the disclaimer that he lacks inside knowledge. Count Binface
  then hired him to build the imagined design (on Squarespace, in under a week, using
  code blocks with SVG and injected CSS) **[said]**.
- **Creative direction and the CMS [said, RSPCA].** Not every page needs art
  direction; a beef-welfare page needs only better typography. He recommends CMS
  templates with swappable regions for art-directed introductions or image sequences,
  citing ProPublica.
- **Patterns are tools, not templates [said, Standout].** Familiar patterns aid
  usability and save time; stand-out design "isn't about novelty for its own sake"; the
  goal is a site "impossible to confuse with anything else". He invented Wheelman (a
  service company for getaway drivers) to show this for shops, charities, forms, and
  services, not only editorial pages, after readers said Art Direction for the Web was
  too editorial.
- **Medium-specific editing [said, 2026].** On a fixed spread he can introduce an idea
  left and demonstrate it right, make an image enormous to stop the reader, or make a
  sequence small to quicken the pace. Reflowable formats need the cut words back. Pace
  and scale are content.
- **Solve a different problem [said, 2007].** Look at how other fields solved
  different problems (DVD menus for navigation, car climate controls for checkout
  steps), quoting Jack Foster's *How To Get Ideas*.

## 3. Taste

- **Influences he names [said].** Magazine art directors above all: Alexey
  Brodovitch, Bea Feitler and Ruth Ansel (Harper's Bazaar), Otto Storch, Neville
  Brody, Francesco Franchi (his favourite living designer). Also Paul Rand, Saul Bass,
  Paula Scher, Herb Lubalin, Alex Steinweiss (album covers), Robert Massin, Wim
  Crouwel, Richard Hollis, Giovanni Pintori (Olivetti), David King, David Carson, Max
  Huber, Lester Beall, Bradbury Thompson, Alvin Lustig, Ladislav Sutnar, Herbert Matter,
  Armin Hofmann, Tibor Kalman, Milton Glaser. Web peers: Trent Walton, Jason Santa
  Maria, Jen Simmons, Brad Frost, Dan Mall.
- **Pop-culture well [said and seen].** Mod and 2-Tone culture, The Jam, Madness,
  Motörhead, Quadrophenia, British TV adverts (PG Tips), hardboiled detective pulps,
  heist films and London gangland, Hanna-Barbera cartoons, Batman: The Animated Series,
  DuckTales comics, Westerns, Planet of the Apes, Godzilla, Bond posters, Star Trek
  posters, 1990s web pages (Cheestrings, Goosebumps, Cartoon Network 1998).
- **Likes [said].** Energy, joy, "fun", personality, cheekiness; editorial structure;
  condensed wood-type grotesques with many weights; a limited palette; drop and initial
  caps; first-line emphasis; text wrapped round shapes; clip-path shapes instead of
  boxes; bottom-aligned captions; texture and depth (he says flat colour and sharp
  edges have made the web two-dimensional); SVG for illustration and even headline
  text; detail such as thick versus thin rules to separate levels.
- **Dislikes [said].** Generic 12-column framework sites; full-width banners as
  filler; cookie-cutter content cards; single columns of long lines with exaggerated
  paragraph spacing ("scrolling skyscraper"); Roboto ("one of the intentionally least
  characterful fonts"); GOV.UK-style layouts for organisations that need storytelling;
  generic coloured icon rows; random colours; off-the-shelf charts; "generic style so
  popular with tech companies"; lazy style guides; design that "fades into the
  background" as a goal; designers who were never taught type and layout. He calls
  himself "disillusioned with the current state of web design" (2024).
- **Recurring motifs [seen].** Gorillas and apes; mods and scooters; the winged car
  badge logo; circle devices; stickers and badges; rotated labels and tickets; numbered
  "issues"; navy, red, white palettes; British humour in copy and art.
- **Personality in the work [said].** "I often tell designers to put something of
  themselves into their work" (cover artwork post). Also willing to be blunt: his
  public criticism of party, charity, and brand sites is direct.
- **Nuances worth keeping [said].** He does not ask every site to be experimental;
  familiar patterns are fine when chosen; restraint is a choice too (Academy of
  Scoring Arts: no shading, two accents, subtle interactions). He refused to cap line
  length on his 2012 site, arguing the reader controls the measure, yet in 2024 he
  shortens long lines on the Greens and charity redesigns, so treat the 2012 position
  as superseded.
