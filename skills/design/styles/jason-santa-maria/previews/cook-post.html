Quince, slowly

A quince goes into the pot yellow and hard, and four hours later it comes out the colour of a garnet.

Margit HalloranNo. 914 November 2026

Oven
150 °C, lid on
Time
About 4 hours
Serves
4, with syrup to spare

The quinces at the Saturday market come from Pál’s tree, which he says is older than his house. They are lumpy and knuckled, covered in a grey down that comes off on your hands, and so hard that the first one I ever bought bent my best paring knife. Raw, a quince is barely food. What it has instead is a smell, honey and rose and a little pear, strong enough that two of them on a windowsill scent the whole kitchen for a week.

Cooking one is mostly waiting. Peel it, quarter it, cut out the core, and keep the peels and the cores, because they hold the pectin and most of the colour. Everything goes into a heavy pot with sugar, water and half a lemon, lid on, into a low oven. Then nothing much happens for an hour.

Somewhere in the second hour the pale flesh begins to blush. By the third it is the colour of a wet brick, and by the fourth it has turned deep and translucent, a red you can see the light through. The syrup thickens around it. I have tried to hurry this with a hotter oven and got brown fruit that tasted fine and looked like stewed apple.

We eat them warm with thick yoghurt, cold on toast, or with a hard sheep’s cheese after dinner. The syrup keeps for a month in the fridge and goes into anything that wants sweetening. If you can only find one quince, cook it anyway; I have done it for one and not regretted the oven. Pál’s tree, by the way, is the same one in No. 4.

Baked quince in its own syrup

  • 4 quinces, about 1.2 kg
  • 300 g sugar
  • 1 l water
  • ½ lemon, juiced, and its shell
  • 1 vanilla pod, if you have one
  1. Heat the oven to 150 °C. Rub the down off the quinces under the tap, then peel, quarter and core them. Put the peels and cores in a square of muslin and tie it shut.
  2. Warm the sugar and water in a heavy ovenproof pot until the sugar dissolves. Add the lemon juice and shell, the vanilla, the bundle of peels and the quarters. Cover the fruit with a circle of baking paper, then the lid.
  3. Bake for three and a half to four hours, turning the quarters once an hour, until they are deep red and a knife slides in without pushing.
  4. Lift out the bundle and squeeze it into the pot. Serve warm, or keep the quarters under their syrup in a jar in the fridge.
