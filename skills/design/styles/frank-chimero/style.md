# Frank Chimero: write the design, then set it plainly

Design a page by first describing, in plain sentences, what the thing is and what it
wants to be; then build it from plain materials and let attention make them artful.
Distilled from Frank Chimero's own sites and essays, the free online edition of *The
Shape of Design*, his talks as published on his site, his live and archived work, and
other people's accounts. Written in our own words.

It suits small sites with something to say: studios and one-person practices, writers,
a single product, a talk or essay turned into a page, anything whose whole contents fit
on one screen as a list. The result should feel warm, plain and clear: a well-set
manuscript, a sentence over a sky.

He works at two altitudes. He wants "a lot of clarity at 10,000 feet, and a lot of
clarity at two feet. Everything in between becomes the opportunity."

## 1. Write the design first

Answer these in the brief, in sentences, before any layout.

1. **What is it?** Name the thing plainly. Then: make it look like what it is.
2. **What does it want to be?** Every problem has a grain, like wood. Say which way this
   one wants to be cut: a list, a letter, a story told in order, a single offer.
3. **What is the one thing this page must do?** One.
4. **What are the one or two uniquely true things about this business?**
5. **How should a visitor feel?** Tone is not style: style is the tool you pick
   afterwards to set the tone.
6. **What is the direct asset?** A great photograph, a memorable drawing, or one
   pitch-perfect sentence does most of the work. Choose which.
7. **What odd, concrete object belongs to it?** Open on it and return to it, changed, at
   the end.
8. **What unlike thing sits well beside it?** His tone is "sprawling": two things that
   seem unrelated, brought together. One outside reference, chosen over a stock image of
   the trade.

Then set the limits: what content, what structure, what tone. Start with bad ideas to
get moving.

## 2. At 10,000 feet: choose the shape

Pick the one shape the answers point to.

- **The sentence.** One sentence is the whole page: set large in a light serif over a
  photograph, with a way to get in touch and a small status line ("Booking September").
  For a studio or a practice.
- **The index.** The list is the page. Each row a name and a short gloss; years or dates
  on the right; hairlines between rows; the home page may be nothing but the index. For
  a body of work.
- **The split.** On wide screens a sticky index fills one half in a dark ground, and a
  light reading half scrolls on its own with a centred column of about 36rem. The index
  is the navigation and the home page. For a small site whose contents fit on one
  screen and whose main act is reading.
- **The sequence.** A long page as full-width bands, one band per beat of the argument,
  around one reading column. For a talk.

Then name **one structural departure** that carries the feeling from question 5: sort
by mood as well as topic, read sideways, print it as a cheap newspaper.

## 3. At two feet: choose the materials

### Type

Choose one of two systems.

- **One face, one size.** Set everything, headings included, in one face at one size.
  Build hierarchy from space, capitals, italics, colour and position: a title is bold or
  centred with room around it; dates and glosses go grey. A plain monospace makes this a
  manuscript or a letter. Text 14–20px, line-height about 1.5, old-style figures on.
  This survives any reader's font size.
- **Two voices and no middle.** The speaking voice is a light serif at large size: the
  sentence, the names in an index. The furniture is a small plain sans or monospace at
  text size: navigation, captions, dates, buttons. Skip the middle sizes entirely. A
  readable serif carries the body text when there is any.

Either way:

- Look for **new plain materials.** Helvetica, Times and link blue carry other people's
  baggage. Pick an unshowy face that is not the default (he used Klim's Untitled Sans and
  Serif, Newsreader Light, Courier, Quadraat, Tiempos), and refine it with attention.
- Make a system fallback stack sized to match, so the page still holds with no web fonts.
- Keep the measure near 30–36em.
- Tracked capitals belong to utility text (a vertical title rail, a caption), never as a
  label above a heading.

### Colour

- Lay **greys and one warm accent**: a dark green-grey beside a pale grey, a #333 beside
  paper, with burnt orange, gold, sage or a deep red used for links, one rule and the
  title. Keep the accent scarce.
- Allow **one full saturated field**: a coral page, a sky, a peach to mint gradient
  behind a break. Flat fills and simple gradients; they stretch to any size, which is
  what the web wants.
- Let colour **change slowly with time or progress**: a ground that darkens as you
  descend, a hue that drifts over minutes. Never as an entrance effect.

### Images and ornament

- The direct asset leads.
- Set images as **plates**: full column width, a small radius, often two side by side in
  one dark plate, with a small centred caption in the utility face.
- Drawings: line etching, or flat shapes in two or three soft colours with an edge that
  looks traced by hand. Collage of overlapping shapes and archive fragments.
- Ornament is a **short centred rule** (about 10rem), a hairline between rows, a small
  dot. No boxes or panels.

## 4. Everything in between: pace

This is where the opportunity is. Between the shape and the materials, decide how the
page keeps time.

- **Start on the narrow screen.** Choose the order, stack it, and settle the hierarchy
  there with colour, face, size and how links look. Wider screens inherit it and only
  then set things side by side.
- **Compose lock-ups before boxes.** Arrange each group (a picture with its caption and
  date; a name with its gloss and year) as a unit, then decide how many fit across at
  each width. Design how pieces build up, not how they break down.
- **Change the rhythm where time passes.** Section boundaries, a long scroll, line
  height. A new band starts when the argument turns, and its ground changes with it.
- **One thing per screen** in a sequence: one picture, or one sentence.
- **Breaks.** Between movements, a tall section with a few words far apart in a thin
  face, so scrolling reads them one at a time; or one key sentence centred between two
  short rules. Write these as full plain sentences that join one beat to the next.
- **Name the parts** of a long piece by a parallel set (prepositions, verbs), each
  opening on a quiet title card: the name, one short rule under it, and an epigraph with
  its source.
- **Argue by variation.** To make a point, show the same subject several times with one
  thing changed, in a strip, and let the reader see it.
- **A gauge, when progress means something.** If scrolling really is a journey (depth),
  show a small persistent counter and give the bottom a payoff. Keep it a gauge, never a
  show.
- **Motion explains.** Animate only to show where something came from or went, or a
  slow change of atmosphere.
- **Leave a void** that lets the reader fill it.

## 5. Go with the grain

- The web wants vertical stacking, fluid widths, scrolling, a lot of text, flat colour
  and simple gradients, horizontal bands, text as interface, and big type over a tinted
  picture. These shared patterns come from a shared material; use them.
- Image beside text is the medium's one unsolvable tension: the image grows taller as
  it widens while the text grows shorter. Manage it.
- **Change the design, not the toolchain.** Design the house that is easy to build.
  Plain CSS, little of it. Keep the source legible enough that someone can learn from
  it.
- **Show the navigation as words.** A small site has a handful of places; put them all
  on screen, each with its gloss, on phones too. No hamburger.
- **Clarity over simplicity.** Show the seams: labels, sources, what connects to what.

## 6. Phones

- The narrow screen was designed first, so the phone page is the hierarchy itself.
- The split becomes a stack: index first, then the reading.
- Navigation stays visible as a short run of words.

## 7. What to refuse

- A hero with a button over three feature boxes and a logo wall; any content in boxes.
- Scroll-jacking, entrance animations and other "bicycle bear" tricks: impressive work
  against the medium.
- A default face chosen because it was there; a type scale with stepped headings when
  space and position would do.
- Several accents; a palette that never rests on grey.
- A small tracked label stamped above section headings.
- Any word inside a headline or display statement set apart by italic, colour, weight or
  a mark. Treat the whole line one way.
- Copy built as a contrast ("it isn't X, it's Y") or as a setup with a short emphasised
  payoff; a lone connective word ("But.") as display. Write the plain sentence.
- Triads in every block of copy because his frames often have three parts. Name as many
  parts as the thing has.
- Stock imagery of the trade.
