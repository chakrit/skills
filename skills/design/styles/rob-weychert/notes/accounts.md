## Findings

### Process: what comes first, what is shown, how a design changes

- At ProPublica, design work "starts as words": email, talk and one-on-one meetings with
  reporters and editors to find the tone and opportunities in a story; when there is
  time, paper notebook sketches of the opener with reference screenshots; then "we
  generally go from sketching to code pretty quickly", with fewer mockups than Sleight
  would like because of story volume. **Reported** by David Sleight about the team, S2
  (≈0:20:16).
- Sleight told Clarke that Weychert in particular "move[s] very quickly from sketches
  into code". **Reported** by Andy Clarke relaying Sleight, S4 (0:21:09).
- Weychert answers that this varies by project, that he had lately been "diving into
  code way too early" and was trying to sketch and iterate more first. For Lost Mothers
  he showed the reporter and editors initial concepts "almost like a client
  relationship", talked through benefits and drawbacks, iterated in "very basic wire
  framey mockups", and the concept came together in about a week before a prototype was
  built up. **Stated**, S4 (0:21:39).
- He rarely makes high-fidelity mockups now and is looking for a middle ground between
  wireframe and finished code; he has trouble setting type in Sketch, "and type is so
  core to what I do". **Stated**, S4 (0:22:53, 0:24:01).
- Designers at ProPublica code their own layouts. Sleight: Weychert is "a brilliant ...
  CSS web standards coder. So when he does a layout, he is also the one to implement
  it." **Reported**, S2 (0:21:45).
- Sleight keeps a designer in two of three tracks at a time: daily production, big
  custom-art-directed feature stories, and the site's own design and UX; for "someone
  like Rob" that means half to two thirds of his time on the site, then a stint on a big
  story. **Reported**, S2 (≈0:06:11). Weychert describes his own work as falling between
  "platform level" site UX and "bespoke custom design of specific stories". **Stated**,
  S4 (0:15:09).
- How he approaches a problem: start with the problem, apply the most conventional
  solution, then ask how that convention is insufficient for "the very specific problem
  to be solved as opposed to the category of problem"; for an article, flow the text
  into a standard layout first, then add or subtract. **Stated**, S4 (0:40:58).

### Reasons given for particular moves

- **Lost Mothers gallery: a grid of mostly empty slots.** The reporting team wanted a
  gallery of the women who died; Sleight was concerned nobody would look through a page
  of unfamiliar faces, and Weychert says he pushed back less. The information was uneven
  (about 130 identified out of an estimated 700 to 900; photos for some, not others).
  The design shows some 800 slots so the reader sees "how little information we were
  actually able to find"; individual stories open from the grid, about 15 as longer
  vignettes. "The vast majority of it is empty and that sort of the point." **Stated**,
  S4 (0:16:35 to 0:20:41). Clarke describes it as "telling a story within a story": the
  grid gives scope and shows at once how few were identified. **Reported**, S4 (0:19:57).
  Sleight's year-in-review credits the interactive gallery to Weychert and says it
  "highlighted some of the personal stories that are often lost in the statistics".
  **Reported**, S7.
- **Source of the Lost Mothers grid:** partly the dot-grid floor of the elevator in
  ProPublica's building, which he saw every day. **Stated**, S4 (0:38:26).
- **Typographic scale and Sass grid helper for the 2017 ProPublica site.** Sleight gives
  "all credit" for the new site's readability to Weychert, who built "a grid helper tool
  in SASS and a typographic scale"; the stated benefit is that new features no longer
  need "lots of ad-hoc decisions" and can rest on "this vetted thing where there's some
  harmony between the pieces". **Reported**, S5. Sleight also credits Weychert with much
  of the front-end build of the 2017 site, done with Ethan Marcotte, and says the site
  was made fully responsive because ProPublica's readership had become mobile-majority.
  **Reported**, S5.
- **Column Setter (the grid tool).** Two things mattered to him: it would not touch the
  HTML (no "six col" classes), and it would not prescribe a grid; the user defines the
  grid's parameters within constraints. **Stated**, S4 (0:45:14).
- **"An Unbelievable Story of Rape" (ProPublica with The Marshall Project).** Sleight
  names "Me and Rob" as ProPublica's designers on it. The piece keeps two reporters'
  voices as alternating narratives, art-directed differently: the victim's side in
  figurative illustration, the police side in evidentiary photography. Sleight presents
  the idea as his own ("I sort of said ..."); he does not say what Weychert's part was.
  **Reported**, S2 (0:37:42).

### Refusals and dislikes

- Generic frameworks, design systems and pattern libraries amount to "outsourcing" the
  work to someone who does not know the specific problem; his objection is less that
  everything looks the same than that people skip finding "what makes the thing ...
  unique". **Stated**, S4 (0:40:58).
- Committee thinking in big companies, where stakeholders' needs are met and users'
  are not. **Stated**, S4 (0:13:46).
- He tends to strip things down and avoids work that is "superficially forward
  thinking", that is bells and whistles; he worries that this makes him miss chances.
  **Stated**, S4 (0:30:38, 0:31:22).
- He saw web design as having become "mannered", taken over by Bootstrap layouts.
  **Stated**, S4 (0:37:07).

### Where his ideas come from

- After school he wanted to work for a magazine and was "really inspired by David
  Carson". **Stated**, S4 (0:09:57).
- Names John Allsopp's 2000 A List Apart article ("A Dao of Web Design") and Ethan
  Marcotte's responsive web design article as the turning points on flexibility versus
  typographic control. **Stated**, S4 (0:07:03, 0:25:59).
- Ideas from outside design: the city, the commute, nature; he values "visual
  thinking", making associations most people would not make, over any particular
  cultural literacy. **Stated**, S4 (0:38:26).

### Working with clients and collaborators; phones

- Sleight, on hiring him: "Rob brings a level of nuance, intelligence and insight to
  editorial design that few can claim." **Reported**, S6. The same announcement lists
  his independent clients 2010 to 2015 as Macmillan, MailChimp, Pew Charitable Trusts
  and Wiley, his role as print producer for A Book Apart, and calls him "an outspoken
  advocate for user-centered design and the open web". **Reported**, S6.
- Earlier clients named in the An Event Apart bio: Advertising Age, the New York Stock
  Exchange, Comhaltas Ceoltóirí Éireann, Comcast, Sub Pop Records. The bio says he "is
  known for a neurotically meticulous attention to detail", that his print background
  helped him adapt "classic graphic design principles" to standards-based web design,
  and that he is known for humour in his work (Virtual Stan). **Reported** (authorship
  of the bio not stated), S11.
- At Happy Cog in 2007 he "contributed additional art direction" to a site whose design
  Zeldman credits to Jason Santa Maria. **Reported**, S9.
- In 2016 he "had a little fun animating" ProPublica's reporting on Amazon's pricing
  algorithm. **Reported**, S8.
- Zeldman on the 2005 relaunch of robweychert.com: "Let columns multipy" (sic) and
  "Let haikus based on dictionary.com's Word of the Day flourish". **Reported**, S10.
- A 2007 review of *Web Standards Creativity* says the book's typography chapter "has
  some useful math help with sizing both fonts and layouts". **Reported**, S12.
  Weychert's chapter in that book is "Bridging the Type Divide: Classic Typography with
  CSS". **Stated**, S13. The review does not name the chapter's author; the link rests
  on the chapter title.
- Phones: most readers see work "on a tiny little screen", yet designers, himself
  included, still "fetishize" the big-screen layout; the small-screen layout is still
  "this just tall stacked thin layout" and he expects more is possible. **Stated**, S4
  (0:28:32). He designs with RSS readers and read-later apps (Instapaper, Pocket) in
  mind. **Stated**, S4 (0:26:16).

### Beliefs about design in general

- Art direction on the web has to give up the print sense of control and
  self-expression and accommodate "all different kinds of people who might not be able
  to even see what we're designing for them". **Stated**, S3 and S4 (0:33:10).
- Editorial design improves when several people bring their expertise to how a story is
  told, including an editor for a designer's own case study. **Stated**, S4 (0:13:46).
- "I'm a big believer in simplicity". **Stated**, S4 (0:30:38).
- The main task he sees is strengthening the open web against people relying on
  Facebook pages instead of websites. **Stated**, S4 (0:49:40).

### Changes of mind, with dates

- 2018: he had drifted into coding too early and was trying to return to sketching and
  rapid iteration first. **Stated**, S4 (0:21:39).
- 2018: he moved away from "pixel perfect Photoshop mockups like we always used to do"
  and had not settled on a replacement. **Stated**, S4 (0:22:53).
- Around 2010 to 2015, freelancing, he says he lost enthusiasm and fell behind as
  toolchains grew; ProPublica from late 2015 "took a 180" on that (git, Jekyll, Sass).
  **Stated**, S4 (0:07:03).
