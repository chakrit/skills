# Simon Collison: a motive, a small kit, and a voice in every label

Design a site the way Simon Collison designs: find the motive, impose a tight kit, let
the type lead, and write every label in one voice until the whole site reads as one
object from the business's world. Distilled from his own sites and journal, his case
studies, his talk slides and articles, and other people's accounts of his work. Written in
our own words.

- **Feels like:** a well-kept miscellany, a gig programme, a club newsletter on headed
  paper, a record-shop counter. Few colours with conviction, one ordinary text face set
  with care, a condensed display voice, footnotes and drop caps, copy that makes you
  smile, an honest flat surface.
- **Fits:** small cultural and independent businesses, events and conferences, clubs and
  societies, shops with a culture of their own (records, books, seeds, tea), personal and
  editorial sites: anything with a voice and an audience that will read.
- **Fits poorly:** checkout flows and dashboards. There his own rule is reduction and
  breathing room ("flexible but stern"), and the conceit shrinks to a few labels.

## The default he would refuse

A site that looks like every site in its trade, with labels nobody wrote ("Our
Services", "Get in Touch"), icons and illustrations that literally depict their section,
texture faked to look old, effects added because the browser can do them, and white space
used because minimal is assumed to be clean. It pleases everybody and is remembered by
nobody. He would rather divide opinion than be boring.

## 1. Find the motive

Before any layout, write in the brief which motive drives this design. Choose from his
list, one or two at most:

| Motive     | What it asks for                                               |
|------------|----------------------------------------------------------------|
| Nostalgia  | a period object the audience half remembers                   |
| Vernacular | the visual language the audience's culture already speaks     |
| Humour     | wry copy, an unexpected picture, a joke the reader has to find |
| Rhetoric   | a clear argument the layout makes in its order and its scale   |

Then name **the object**: one thing from the business's world that the whole site will
be. A Victorian seed catalogue, a parish magazine, a gig programme, a record-shop
returns slip, a club's fixture card, a field notebook. Look for it in period print and
ephemera (miscellanies, engravings, paperback covers, record sleeves, stickers,
newspapers) and in the culture the customers live in. One object per site; do not blend.

Throw ideas away freely. Several concepts should die before one survives. Provoke
yourself: what if the text was huge? What if it was less Swiss? What if it were printed?

## 2. Impose the kit

Complete freedom is the worst start. Write the kit into the brief before designing, and
keep it this small:

- one grid;
- two typefaces;
- two colours, one grey, a near-black, a white (or their dark-ground equivalents);
- what the kit may generate: line patterns, solid blocks, tints, rules.

Everything on the page comes from the kit. When the page feels thin, generate more from
the kit (a pattern of lines, a reversed block, a tint) before adding anything to it. A
season or a sub-brand may branch off with its own colours; the kit still does most of the
work.

## 3. Let the type lead

Choose type before layout, for how it informs the whole feel.

- **The text face may be an ordinary one.** A tired, familiar face (Times, Georgia, a
  plain grotesque) used with full craft shows skill through composition rather than
  through the choice of face. Make it "richly and superbly ordinary": careful sizes, real
  italics, small capitals for dates, spaced capitals for navigation, old-style figures,
  real quotes. Bold only where it earns it, such as a drop cap.
- **The display face is condensed** and fitted to the business's own name: set the name
  in the candidates and keep the face that sits best with its letters. A narrow sans
  "doesn't feel too modern" next to a period text face; a wide one may fight the name.
- **One accent face may antiquate**: an italic for a motto, a Baskerville-style
  ampersand, a monospace for a ledger figure. Use it for whole lines, never for one word
  in a headline.
- **Name and descriptor.** Set a name or title in condensed capitals and put an italic
  serif descriptor under it that carries the wit ("ADA PRIOR / *keeps bees, argues with
  them*"). The caps give order; the italic gives voice. The descriptor goes under the
  title, never above it as a label.
- Keep body text large. His v4 was criticised for small type; do not repeat it.

## 4. Write the copy before the layout

Finalise the words first. Every word on the site speaks in the object's voice: section
names, navigation, captions, buttons, form hints, the footer, the closed-comments notice.
"Potted Autobiography", not "About". "Exhaustive Archives", not "Blog". A colophon that
says the site is "kept alive with tea".

- The voice is wry, warm and specific: real place names, real objects, dates, the odd
  aside. Self-deprecating rather than salesy.
- Buttons and links stay plain enough to act on. The voice decorates the label around an
  action; the action itself reads as an action.
- Read every line against the bans' writing tells before it reaches the page. Wit that
  comes as a setup and an emphasised payoff is a banned pattern; put the joke in the
  plain statement of a fact.

## 5. Juxtapose

Put one picture beside the type and see if the design clicks.

- **The irrelevant illustration.** One drawn creature or object per section, chosen to
  have no correlation with the section. The picture gives no clue, so the reader reads
  the caption, and the caption makes the joke. Draw them as single-colour line
  engravings in inline SVG, in the kit's ink colour.
- Or **hand-made marks** beside confident type: a pencil-drawn bird, a rough rule, a
  scribbled logotype, next to a very large, plain line of type.
- Or **vernacular parody**: badges, stickers or labels that riff on the audience's own
  culture (album sleeves for a music crowd, show rosettes for growers).
- Unify any pictures by tinting them in the kit's colours, so they look like one family.

## 6. Furnish the page

The editorial furniture is the structure. Use several of these; each must come from the
object:

- a big drop cap in the accent colour at the start of each main text, or a § sign as the
  drop cap;
- catalogue numbers on entries (№ 214), in a list column or a roundel beside the entry,
  never as a numbered label above a section heading;
- an aside column of italic metadata between dotted rules, with dates in small capitals;
- a double rule under a title, hairlines that start and stop exactly where the content
  does;
- footnotes with return links, for asides, sources and jokes;
- a masthead lockup: an italic line, a line of spaced capitals, the name in condensed
  capitals, each whole line with its own treatment;
- a footer lockup around one huge ampersand;
- for a history, **a timeline**: years on one rule, short fragments in narrow columns,
  small objects hung on stems, turning points marked by rotated labels.

## 7. Keep the surface honest

Truth to materials: the screen is a screen.

- No fake paper, coffee rings, scuffs or worn textures. An antique voice sits on a clean
  flat ground: off-white with a faint grid, a plain cream, or a calm dark tone.
- No gratuitous effects: no drop shadows, rotations for show, spinning or bouncing
  buttons.
- Motion only where something would really move, along the path its cause gives it.
  Respect reduced motion.
- Restraint is economy that gives immediate meaning. Do not assume a minimal page is
  clean; complexity can be elegant when it is organised. Trust readers to fill in the
  blanks.

## 8. Build the system in the browser

Design pages as parts of one system: the masthead, the furniture, the type and the kit
hold on every page; the conceit then bends a rule where it pays.

- Sketch away from the screen to sharpen the reasoning, then do most of the designing in
  the browser: throw the components around and squint at the screen.
- Put everything on a firm grid. Ornament without a grid under it falls apart; with one,
  it reads as care.
- For commercial screens (forms, baskets, checkout), reduce: remove what is extraneous,
  let things breathe, and let hairlines and type sizes do the work.

## 9. Phones

- Reflow; do not shrink. Columns stack, the aside column moves under its text, a timeline
  turns vertical.
- Keep body text at least as large as on desktop. Keep the drop caps, the footnotes and
  the voice; the irrelevant pictures stay, smaller.
- A flexible layout may "twang around" and look a little odd at some widths. That is
  acceptable; broken reading is not.

## 10. Articulate the decisions

Write each major decision in the brief as a move with its reason in visual grammar, never
"because it feels right": line and point, form, formal or informal structure, balance,
pattern, texture, repetition and rhythm, path and movement, colour, white space as the
pause, depth, affordance, juxtaposition, economy, grid.

## The audit

Run it on the rendered page. Be blunt, never needlessly negative, and give a fix with
every finding.

- What is the motive and the object, in one line each? Does every label, caption and
  footer speak in the object's voice?
- Can you list the kit in one breath? Is anything on the page from outside it?
- Name the text face and the display face. Is the text face set with real craft (italics,
  small caps, figures, quotes, spacing), and is the body large enough on a phone?
- Which picture is the juxtaposition? Does its caption, not the picture, carry the
  meaning?
- Which pieces of furniture are used, and does each come from the object?
- Is there any fake texture, shadow or effect for its own sake?
- Squint: is the weight balanced, and is everything on the grid?
- Would some people dislike it? If nobody would, it may be boring.
- Could this belong to another business in the same trade?

## What to refuse

- Labels nobody wrote; icons and illustrations that depict their section literally.
- Fake wear and texture; effects for their own sake.
- More faces or colours than the kit allows.
- Minimal for its own sake; white space as a default rather than a pause.
- A label stamped above each section heading, whether caps, italic or numbered.
- Any word inside a headline set apart by colour, italic, weight or a mark. Give the
  whole line one treatment; a stacked lockup may treat each whole line differently.
- Copy built as a setup with a short emphasised payoff.
- Content set in boxes repeated in a grid. Use ruled lists, catalogue entries, a
  timeline, a programme.
