## 1. Moves

### 1.1 Start from what the content is, then borrow the form of a physical thing like it

- **What.** Before any layout, he asks what kind of object the content would be in the
  physical world and borrows that object's cues. For A List Apart: things that are read
  "religiously" rather than skimmed (McSweeney's, old books), so the site took banners,
  stamps, a laurel wreath, numbered issues, book-like hierarchy, an academic feel. [R:
  AEA 2007 liveblog] Seen 2026-11-23 (Wayback, 2005-09): a cream bookplate-like logo
  panel with a faint laurel, "A LIST apart" in serif caps and italic between rules, a
  black serrated "No. 201" seal on its corner, Georgia caps nav with diamond separators,
  a letterspaced date line, centred serif titles with caps bylines, dashed rules, and
  side promos drawn as stamped labels. [O, seen]
- For Comhaltas (Irish traditional-music body) the team went to Ireland, sat in pub
  sessions, then mined Celtic knotwork, leaf/organic textures and the culture's colours,
  while explicitly rejecting the stock "Irish" clichés (leprechauns, shamrocks). [R: AEA
  2007] Seen 2026-11-23 (Wayback, 2007-06): the knotwork survives only as a quiet
  interlace pattern in a saffron band under the masthead; the rest is a rust-red sidebar
  in small caps, a blue swirl mark with a rust serif small-caps wordmark, a black band
  with large serif text, and a close photo of hands on uilleann pipes. No shamrock
  anywhere. [O, seen]
- For the WordPress logo the brief became "publishing as a personal, hand-made act":
  explorations ran through word balloons, comic-book type, metal type, typewriter keys;
  the mark ended up implying a typewriter key. He says the talks with Matt Mullenweg
  kept returning to letterpress, journals and "older representations of what it meant to
  publish something in a physical form". [R: AEA 2007; S: WordPress oral history]
- **Replaces.** The default of starting from a layout pattern or a trend, or from the
  category stereotype. He warns against "piggybacking on an idea, or, worse yet, a
  trend" because it "makes us look like anybody else" (the Double‡Dagger exercise). [R:
  AEA 2007]
- **When it fits.** Any page with a real subject. For an agent: name the physical
  artefact the content resembles (journal, field guide, broadsheet, gig poster, record
  sleeve), take two or three structural cues from it (numbering, rules, stamps,
  mastheads), and drop the cliché icons of the category.

### 1.2 Per-issue / per-piece variation inside a fixed, muted frame

- **What.** The ALA redesign kept a restrained, "very muted design" as the frame and
  changed two things per issue: a unique illustration per article (by Kevin Cornell) and
  a new accent colour each issue, chosen by mood. His stated aim was that each issue feel
  "a new thing", less templated, "something a little bit more important". [R: AEA 2007
  and @media 2007 liveblogs]
- His personal site added custom art direction per feature article from June 2008 (TGD
  caption, [R]); the "own" stream covers that work in detail.
- The 2008 24 ways article is the mechanism behind per-article layouts: a fixed column
  grid (example: seven 100px columns, 10px gutters) and composable classes for
  identifier / size-in-columns / placement / extras (`pic four left frame solo`, `inset`,
  `outset` as default) so each article can be laid out "how I saw fit" while every image
  still lands on the grid. He frames it as recovering the print WYSIWYG freedom on the
  web. [S: Making Modular Layout Systems]
- **Replaces.** One template applied identically to every piece; or, at the other
  extreme, one-off pages with no shared system.
- **When it fits.** Editorial/series content. Fix the frame (nav, masthead, body face,
  grid unit); vary accent colour, lead image treatment and image placement per piece.

### 1.3 One accent colour, used sparingly, as the thing the eye follows

- **What.** In the @media 2007 talk he walks site after site asking "what's the colour
  drawing you in?" and praises small doses of an unexpected or even "abrasive" hue (lime,
  electric blue, neon, a sudden pink) against a calm base; in photographs he looks for
  "that bit of colour" that moves the eye. [R: @media 2007]
- For the WordPress 2.5 admin he took out "as much of the blue as possible", made the
  comp predominantly white, and held on to a "really punchy" orange used only as "action
  colours" so that "when you needed a color it was going to be distinct". He believes it
  was the first non-blue colour in the WordPress admin. [S: WordPress oral history]
- He calls generic corporate blue "watered-down" and "emotionless" and says companies
  afraid of conveying the wrong message "convey no message" (citing Paula Scher's
  "blanding"). [R: @media 2007 Q&A]
- **Palette sources.** Fine art, photography, nature (butterflies), the subject's own
  material (Cork'd from wine labels; Absenter recolouring the site from each photo). [R:
  @media 2007]
- **Replaces.** Many evenly weighted colours, or one safe brand blue everywhere.
- **When it fits.** Nearly always: a quiet neutral field plus one deliberate, slightly
  surprising accent reserved for focal points and actions.

### 1.4 Cheat colour optically, not numerically

- **What.** Treat brand hex values as guidelines. A small element in the same hex as a
  large one reads lighter (light colours on light ground) or darker (dark on light), so
  he shifts small text and rules to restore the same perceived colour: #6699CC logo with
  #4477BB small text and rules; #330000 logo with #550000 small parts. On a dark ground
  the correction reverses. [S: Cheating Color]
- **Replaces.** Applying one token value to every element size.
- **When it fits.** Whenever an accent colour appears both large (logo, block) and small
  (links, rules, captions).

### 1.5 Grid as city plan: a uniform, highly divisible column grid, broken on purpose

- **What.** He compares a good grid to William Penn's Philadelphia (numbered streets,
  City Hall at the centre, hard to get lost) and a bad one to London (you must learn it
  first). "Grid systems are not a way to restrict your design but to order it." [R: both
  2007 liveblogs]
- AIGA (2007): a uniform 12-column grid that divides into six, four or three columns
  (the AEA liveblog says "120-column", likely a typo), with "supercolumns" breaking out;
  Clark notes not every element sits on the column guides. He calls the effect
  "subliminal", "carrying the eye down the page". [R]
- Vertical rhythm is about ratio, "a spatial ratio" rather than fixed pixel steps; fluid
  grids are ratios between columns. [R: @media 2007 Q&A]
- Plan image sizes to the grid; for AIGA he handed over an article page containing every
  allowed image size rather than a long style guide (style guides of "10 pages or less").
  [R: both liveblogs]
- He also criticised Wired for images that drift off its grid: details like that are
  "what keep a design together". [R: @media 2007]
- **When it fits.** Editorial sites. For an agent: choose a column count divisible by 2,
  3 and 4, size every image to a column span, then let one or two elements per page span
  the full width or sit in an off-grid "supercolumn" for contrast.

### 1.6 Big, full reading column, all the way down

- **What.** In the AIGA article page he criticised the old page as "anemic": small type,
  too much navigation, a redundant "Article" label, content "adrift in a sea of
  nothingness" further down. The redesign used a "nice big reading column, nice big
  text, a drop cap to pull people in", inline images placed where they support the text,
  and a page that "feels full all the way down to the bottom". It should feel "like
  something you're meant to read, not just supporting copy". [R: AEA 2007]
- "Bigger is better. Always err on side of making things bigger." 16px as a minimum in
  most cases (reader's summary). [R: LukeW 2011; R: Build talk summary]
- He does not design around the fold: "I actually don't really concern myself too much
  with the fold." [R: AEA 2007 Q&A]
- **Replaces.** Small body type with heavy chrome and a page that peters out into empty
  sidebar space.

### 1.7 Hierarchy by contrast; usually one serif plus one sans

- **What.** "Contrast is probably the most important thing" in pairing: two faces must be
  clearly different; faces too alike "weaken the message". [S: ALA 2009] "Contrast is the
  most important tenet of design... Everything is defined by its opposite." [R: LukeW
  2011]
- Default pairing: one serif, one sans, because they are inherently different and let
  readers "mentally group" content: "a map for them to understand everything". [S: Web
  Ahead 83; S: ALA 2009]
- Pairing logic: same designer (Perpetua + Gill Sans), designed-as-pair (Meta Sans + Meta
  Serif), shared construction (Futura + Bodoni, both geometric), or deliberate
  juxtaposition with strong contrast (Baskerville + Futura as old vs new). [S: ALA 2009]
- One face can be enough; two is usually plenty; more needs a reason such as quoting old
  boxing, film or music posters (Grindhouse example). Never two display or script faces
  together. [S: ALA 2009]
- Prefer big families: bold against light or italic, caps or small caps letter-spaced for
  subheads, instead of adding faces. Single-weight faces make contrast very hard. [S: ALA
  2009; R: LukeW 2011]
- **Hierarchy order.** He quotes Chip Kidd's Winter Sorbeck: "left to right, top to
  bottom, big and small", and insists on one focal point per page, "something that's
  bigger than everything else". [R: @media 2007]

### 1.8 Choose type from abstract qualities, test it small, then "mould" it

- **What, step by step (his own account).**
  1. Write down words for what the project must embody ("delicate and flowing", "strong
     and stable", "machined") before looking at any typeface. [S: Web Ahead 83; R: LukeW
     2011]
  2. Pick the body text face first, because that is where readers spend their time. [S:
     ALA 2009]
  3. Audition faces in a real setting, "trying typefaces on" like a dressing-room montage,
     starting with paragraphs at the smallest size they will appear; reject any face that
     only looks good once refined. [S: Web Ahead 83]
  4. Check history and connotation (Trajan on every film poster, blackletter for metal)
     and either avoid or exploit it. For a historical subject, look for faces from that
     period. [S: ALA 2009; R: @media 2007 Q&A]
  5. Then "molding clay": set the chosen face big, in caps, tracked, as paragraph, as
     headline, in colours, to find where its flexibility lies; spend most effort on
     paragraphs (measure, leading, colour). [S: Web Ahead 83]
- **Warning he gives.** Do not expect the typeface to be the design: "the design should
  come through in the setting, in the typography, rather than the typeface itself". [S:
  Web Ahead 83] Avoid "one-off splash fonts or ready-mades" that "tell you exactly what to
  think"; use classics and change them up. [R: LukeW 2011]
- **Replaces.** Picking a fashionable display face first and building the page around it.

### 1.9 Paragraph texture: measure, leading, grey value

- Longer lines need more line spacing; shorter lines can be set tighter. [R: LukeW 2011;
  R: AEA 2007 Q&A; R: Build summary]
- Each face has its own "gray value"; set spacing relative to it. [R: LukeW 2011]
- For text faces: sturdy, larger x-height, moderate stroke contrast, "some personality,
  but not so much" that it distracts; personality-heavy faces belong at display size. [S:
  ALA 2009; R: LukeW 2011]
- He cites the Wired redesign measure as an example: 115 characters at default size, 66
  when bumped up three sizes. [R: @media 2007]
- Never fake bold or italic: choose "a typeface that has everything that your content
  requires". [S: Web Ahead 83]

### 1.10 Controlled dis-fluency

- A slightly "off" or less fluent face can "stir interest" and even help comprehension;
  "typography is an art of contrast", an "art of imperfect precision". Rules are
  guidelines. [R: LukeW 2011] "Trust your gut... sometimes you'll be surprised what
  typefaces work together even when logic says they shouldn't." [S: ALA 2009]
- **When it fits.** Display settings and openers, not long text.

### 1.11 Navigation written as a sentence

- For Happy Cog's own site he noticed the tagline and the navigation said the same thing
  and merged them into sentence-based navigation, which also showcased the studio's
  writers. [R: AEA 2007] Seen 2026-11-23 (Wayback, 2007-04): one large Georgia sentence
  under the logo whose linked verbs, DESIGN, PUBLISH, SPEAK and ABOUT, are rust caps.
  [O, seen]
- **When it fits.** Small sites with few destinations where voice matters more than scan
  speed.

### 1.12 Typographic details as craft signal

- Real quotes and apostrophes, not inch and foot marks; he puts this on the same footing
  as clean semantic code. "No design is too small, whether it's a favicon or a line of
  text or a border." [R: AEA 2007]

## 2. Process and thinking

- **Design is intent.** "There's a message we're trying to communicate"; when details do
  not add up, "our message is lost". Clarity is the goal. [R: @media 2007] He learned art
  direction from a failed six-foot poster (Captain Noah) where he even got the rainbow
  order wrong: art direction is the concept plus all the pieces adding up. [R: both 2007
  talks]
- **Discovery first.** A space full of inspiring objects (books, skulls, sock monkeys), a
  sketchbook, and a "morgue file" of clippings, old advertisements, matchbooks, textures,
  weird old photos. Research is "the most important part". [R: AEA 2007]
- **Sketchbook.** "Designers need to draw to understand how they think"; the sketchbook is
  "the most useful visual thinking tool". [S: TGD 2012] Low barrier to entry on the web
  tempts people to jump straight to a finished product, "the very worst thing to do". [R:
  @media 2007]
- **Greybox comp.** Between sketch and Photoshop he makes a colourless "greybox" comp to
  settle hierarchy, type relationships and real pixel placement, because going straight
  to a polished comp makes him obsess over type details and lose the message. Clients
  usually do not see it. [R: both 2007 liveblogs]
- **Three directions, then revise.** He presented three distinct comps (for AIGA: one
  where he thought they "should already have been", one more Swiss/designerly on a
  two-column grid, one built around their Design Archives imagery on red), asked clients
  not to "Frankenstein" them, then spent round two on typography. About two weeks per
  direction. [R: AEA 2007; S: WordPress oral history describes the same three-comp setup]
- **Revise rather than create.** "It's often much easier to revise than to create", from
  Anne Lamott's "Shitty First Drafts". [S: TGD 2012; R: AEA 2007]
- **Blue-collar practice.** Skill matters, "but so much of learning is just about sweat";
  be aware of what worked and what made you uncomfortable. "There's not really time for
  perfection." [S: Web Ahead 83] Chuck Close: show up and work. [S: TGD 2012]
- **Working palette of faces.** Know a small set of typefaces deeply rather than switching
  each project; occasionally try one that is "a little different" to expand. He keeps a
  collection of candidate and purchased faces, asks friends, and requests comping
  licences from foundries. [S: Web Ahead 83; R: LukeW 2011; R: Build summary]
- **Empathy for the reader** is the only barrier to entry in typography. Readability asks
  "Do you want to read it?" (paraphrasing Stephen Coles), not just "can you". Test on the
  devices you actually read on. [S: Web Ahead 83; S: How We Read]
- **Clients.** Set expectations in the first meeting; reiterate their feedback at each
  stage; "It's their site; it's your job to steer them." Bland corporate design is "more
  of a people problem than a pixel problem". He prefers to keep design decisions with the
  designer rather than hand readers styling controls, except for accessibility. [R: both
  2007 Q&As]
- **Print to web.** Good typographic practice is "almost universal across mediums"; only
  the fine tuning differs. [S: Subtraction 2014] His SVA lecture was on print's influence
  on the web, arguing designers should adapt rather than treat the web's ephemerality as a
  roadblock. [S: comment on SvN 2010]
- **Book concepts** (reported only, from the paid book): "type for a moment" vs "type to
  live with", which Khoi Vinh names as distinctive ideas; Jason confirms the book is
  "one designer's process" rather than a manifesto. [R/S: Subtraction 2014]

## 3. Taste

- **Influences.** Old printed matter, "especially hand-set"; books (legibility,
  preservation, tactility); McSweeney's; Saul Bass titles (whitespace); Müller-Brockmann,
  Bringhurst, Lupton's *Thinking with Type*, Samara's grid books; Chip Kidd; Paula
  Scher; Hillman Curtis's *MTIV*; Anne Lamott's *Bird by Bird*. Started in the Ray Gun /
  Emigre grunge era and outgrew it as "not useful as a means to an end". [R: liveblogs; S:
  TGD 2012]
- **Faces he names.** Auto (Underware) is "one of my favorite sans serifs" (title slide).
  [S: SvN comment] In the system-font era: Georgia and Verdana the sturdiest, Arial "a
  mess" at small sizes, Trebuchet "kind of all right". [R: @media 2007] Proxima Nova cited
  as a useful big family; Skolar on Bobulate as a good text face. [S: ALA 2009]
- **Praises.** Restrained base with a vivid accent; real white space ("can be even more
  important than the places where you do have type"); spare, light interfaces over dense
  blue ones; work that "you have to stop and really absorb". [R: @media 2007; S: WordPress
  oral history; S: Web Ahead 83]
- **Dislikes.** Cluttered portal pages with type butting every margin (NYT home, NASDAQ,
  IBM, MSNBC as 2007 examples); generic corporate blue; category clichés; trend-chasing
  logos; fake bold/italic; low-quality free fonts; dumb quotes. "No typeface is evil";
  misuse is the problem (Comic Sans is right for comics). [R: liveblogs, LukeW, Build
  summary; S: ALA 2009]
- **On trends.** He does not mind everyone using the same good typefaces, "because the
  typography itself can be unique, not just the typefaces"; it bothers him only when
  leaders he looks to for the next thing fall into them. [S: Web Ahead 83]
- **Wishes.** Proper hyphenation and justification on the web, optical-size (caption /
  text / display) cuts used automatically by size. [S: Web Ahead 83]
- **Self-assessment.** He finds logo design stressful and calls himself "just an okay logo
  designer"; the WordPress mark aimed to be a mark rather than a typeset word so it could
  stand alone over time. [S: WordPress oral history]
