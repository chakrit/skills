# Accounts: what other people say about Jason Santa Maria's design

Stream: `accounts`. Raw records are in `/tmp/jsm/raw/accounts/`, one file per source.
Most pages were read through a fetch tool that returns summaries, so short quotes below
come from those summaries unless the raw file is marked as a full-text copy (Subtraction
and Tim Brown are full text). No visual judgement here rests on seeing the work; this
stream is entirely second-hand.

Claim markers: **[reported]** someone else describes it; **[stated, via X]** his own
words as relayed or quoted by someone else; **[observed]** seen in the work.

## 1. Moves

### A fixed frame, a free interior: one system, each story its own look

- **What.** The site keeps one consistent system for handling content (header, brand,
  navigation, the way content is stored) while each long post gets its own layout,
  colour, typefaces and placement and shape of text blocks.
- **Where.** His 2008 redesign of jasonsantamaria.com [reported, Zeldman 2008; stated,
  via Zeldman: each layout and story "can take on a look of their own" within one
  consistent system]. Earlier, A List Apart 4.0 (2005) applied a milder version: one
  branding system, but each issue gets its own colour scheme and each article its own
  Kevin Cornell illustration [reported, Zeldman ALA announcement; Clark liveblog].
- **Default it replaces.** The blog template: every post poured into the same layout.
  Zeldman framed the redesign as a challenge to template-driven publishing and named
  Derek Powazek's {fray} (1996) as the precedent [reported].
- **Why.** Magazine logic: the system is the magazine, the spread belongs to the story
  [stated, via Zeldman].
- **When it fits.** Long-form, infrequent pieces. Peers who copied it reported the cost:
  posting frequency drops, timely posts are hard, and the site can feel disjointed unless
  a constant element ties pages together [reported, Chapman 2011; Girard comment on
  CSS-Tricks 2013]. A middle path some peers took: shared layouts with custom colour,
  images and fonts per post ("semi-art-directed") [reported, Monk comment, CSS-Tricks].
- **Detail worth keeping.** Posts art directed under one version of his site stay served
  under that version after a redesign, so the old designs survive intact; his samples
  were the best survivors in Coyier's 2013 and 2016 audits [reported, Coyier].

### Type carries the art direction

- **What.** In his art-directed posts the distinct look comes mostly from typography,
  not from imagery or effects.
- **Where.** "uses typography almost exclusively to create his distinct style" [reported,
  Joy, Contently 2012]. Posts named by peers: "Chunky in Fury", "Sugar Smack", "The Candy
  Parenthesis", "All For Me", "Pixy Stix" [reported; no visual description in these
  accounts, so verify against the work stream].
- **Checked by eye, 2026-11-23** [observed (seen); fuller notes in `own.md`]. The claim
  holds for the quote posts and many essays, but photographs carry several posts as much
  as type does ("Royal", "Haunted", "Jackpot", "All For Me"). Three named posts in detail:
  - "Chunky in Fury" (v5, 2011): the title fills the first screen, red heavy sans caps
    and lowercase with a thick white outline, tilted uphill like a candy-wrapper logo, on
    a pale grey ground with faint ruled lines; a small centred byline on a rule; the
    story opens with a large red sans lead.
  - "Sugar Smack" (v4, 2010): black page; the title in pale-blue heavy italic condensed
    caps, with a photo of a red Life Saver hanging on a string between the two words;
    a pale-blue italic pull quote opens the left column, white serif text in two
    columns.
  - "The Candy Parenthesis" (v4, 2010): the page floods yellow; two giant black
    parentheses span the width around the byline, then the lead "I don't like candy." in
    huge white italic serif, then three columns of text.
  - "All For Me" (2010) floats two crossed chocolate bars on a peach ground matching the
    page under a brown script title; "Pixy Stix" (2009) pairs a marker-style title with
    a pink sugar-heart photo bleeding off the left edge.
- **Default it replaces.** A hero image or illustration as the per-post variable.
- **When it fits.** Pages where the story can be carried by face choice, scale and
  placement of text.

### Meaning first, styling second

- **What.** Andy Clarke cites Santa Maria (with Trent Walton) as a designer who "art
  directs his writing", then argues the distinctive per-post look is the result of art
  direction, not its meaning; art direction is distilling what a piece means and choosing
  how words and visuals convey it [reported, Clarke 2016]. This is Clarke's gloss, not
  Santa Maria's, but it matches how Santa Maria described his own work in 2007 (below).
- **For an agent.** Do not vary layout per page for variety's sake; vary it because the
  content's idea calls for a specific composition.

### Research the subject, refuse the cliché

- **What.** Before designing, immerse in the subject's actual culture and drop the
  obvious symbols.
- **Where.** Comhaltas (Irish traditional-music organisation): the team visited Ireland,
  rejected leprechauns and shamrocks, drew on Celtic knotwork and colour traditions, and
  aimed for "modern organisation with deep roots" [stated, via Clark liveblog 2007].
  Seen 2026-11-23 on Wayback (2007-06): knotwork appears only as a quiet interlace
  pattern in a saffron band; rust, saffron, black and a blue mark carry the page, with a
  photo of hands on uilleann pipes; no shamrocks [observed (seen)].
  Double Dagger: warns that borrowing current trends makes a brand look like everyone
  else's [stated, via Clark]. WordPress logo: explored motifs of publishing and personal
  writing such as typewriter keys [stated, via Clark].
- **Default it replaces.** Stock visual shorthand for the topic; trend-of-the-year styling.

### Model the site on a book or magazine, not on other websites

- **What.** Take the reference object from print, and borrow its devices.
- **Where.** A List Apart 4.0: looked to books and magazines such as McSweeney's rather than
  to websites, because books are read closely; borrowed numbered issues, banners, stamps,
  laurel wreaths and a bookish feel [stated, via Clark liveblog]. Seen 2026-11-23 on
  Wayback: laurel logo panel, a black serrated "No. 201" issue seal, centred serif
  titles, dashed rules, stamped-label promos, and issue-to-issue colour change (201
  orange-brown, 209 maroon and olive) [observed (seen)]. He told Andy Clarke the
  site was a book to "pore over", not a blog to skim [stated, via Clarke 2005]. A
  commenter read it as a "Magazine" rather than a "Blog" [reported]. Zeldman called the
  result "classic, almost scholarly" [reported].
- **Default it replaces.** Web conventions copied from other sites.

### No shouting focal point when the content has no single lead

- **What.** A home page with no dominant hero; elements held at even weight.
- **Where.** ALA 4.0. Andy Clarke criticised the home page for lacking "a strong visual
  focus point, a place to start reading". Santa Maria answered that nothing screams
  because it doesn't need to [stated, via Clarke 2005]; Dan Mall defended it because an
  ALA issue has no single feature article [reported].
- **Default it replaces.** The centred hero that announces one thing.
- **When it fits.** Issue-like pages with several peer items. It drew real criticism, so
  pair it with a clear reading order.

### Full, generous article pages

- **What.** Article pages that feel full from top to bottom: a generous reading column,
  large text, a drop cap, images set inline with the text, against what he called
  "anemic" pages of small type and excess navigation [stated, via Clark liveblog].
  Readers praised ALA 4.0 specifically for long reading [reported, SvN comments]; Zeldman
  noted its wide, comfortable columns [reported]. In a 2012 talk he urged erring on the
  side of large type, around 16px minimum for body, and more leading for longer lines
  [stated, via Go Make Things notes].

### Grids that are mathematically flexible

- **What.** AIGA site on a 120-column grid divisible by 3, 4 and 6, with image placement
  planned in advance [stated, via Clark liveblog]. Grids give readers a predictable visual
  language; he contrasted Philadelphia's legible street plan with London's [stated, via
  Clark]. Zeldman summarised his modular layout article as a way to "predictably tame
  content without becoming predictable" [reported, Zeldman 2008].

### Hierarchy through contrast; type for a moment versus type to live with

- **What.** Hierarchy made by contrast of size and style against the regular text
  [stated, via Go Make Things]. Distinguishes "type for a moment" (headings, menus,
  short bursts) from "type to live with" (long reading), each chosen by different
  criteria [reported, Russo 2017; named by Khoi Vinh 2014].
- **Headlines.** He notes many designers, himself included, set headlines in sans serifs
  because they pack tighter, allowing more characters per line at a larger size [stated,
  via Tim Brown's quoted excerpt].
- **Margins.** At least about 1.5 to 2 ems of margin around body text so it does not feel
  congested [stated, via Tim Brown's quoted excerpt].
- **Pairing.** Pairs should balance distinction with cohesion [reported, Russo].

## 2. Process and thinking

- **Morgue files and sketchbooks.** Keeps a sketchbook and "morgue files" of old ads,
  photos, matchbooks and textures; surrounds himself with inspiring objects [stated, via
  Clark liveblog]. Ran a "Pretty Sketchy" Flickr pool of designers' sketches in 2009
  [reported, Weychert].
- **Grey box before colour.** Sketch, then a grey-box comp that settles structure and
  hierarchy, only then colour, imagery and detail [stated, via Clark]. Zeldman hired him
  after reading the 2004 grey box post, for its reasoning: "Not everyone thinks to write
  a why" [reported, Zeldman 2011]. Brad Frost's team adopted it to check content and order
  of importance with a client [reported, Frost 2014].
- **Several directions, then rounds.** AIGA: three distinct directions, then refinement
  rounds, with typography the focus of the second round [stated, via Clark].
- **Choosing type by word association.** Instead of scrolling through catalogues, decide
  what the design should convey and list words for that mood, then look for faces that
  match [stated, via Tim Brown's quoted excerpt; reported, Russo: "easy and effective"].
- **Test in context.** Try faces and pairings on the real or a local version of the site
  [reported, Russo].
- **Working types.** Learn a small set of faces deeply across sizes and uses [stated, via
  Go Make Things].
- **Start from reading.** The book opens from how people read rather than from picking
  fonts; reviewers single out "How We Read" [reported, Russo, Shoaf]. Most of a letter's
  information is in its top half [stated, via Go Make Things].
- **Comfort with no single right answer.** He wrote the book to address the uncomfortable
  part: pulling faces apart and reasoning why one works better, where there are "different
  forms of good"; it became his personal process rather than an academic method [stated,
  via Khoi Vinh]. Typography combines history, writing, maths, artistry and craft; when
  the maths and the eye disagree, trust the eye [stated, via Tim Brown's quoted excerpt].
- **Typography is universal across media.** Fundamentals hold from a building's side to
  a phone; only the tuning per medium changes [stated, via Khoi Vinh].

## 3. Taste

- **Print, books and editorial design** as the source of reference, over websites
  [stated, via Clark]; Bringhurst and Lupton as recommended references [stated, via Clark].
- **Details:** real quotes and apostrophes, favicons, borders, single lines of text all
  count [stated, via Clark].
- **Dislikes:** anemic article pages; borrowed trends; visual clichés of a subject; small
  body text [stated, via Clark and Go Make Things].
- **"No typeface is evil"**: the use, not the face, is good or bad; "type is a beautiful
  group of letters, not a group of beautiful letters" [stated, via Go Make Things].
- **Playfulness:** peers describe his recent Megazoid microsite as "super-fun" and an old
  edition of his site as "gorgeously dreadful" (affectionate) [reported, Weychert]. The
  Lost World's Fairs Moon page imagined a 2040 World's Fair on the Moon, with Earth and
  Moon moving against each other through fixed positioning [reported, Walton].
- **Influence.** Peers credit him with popularising per-post art direction (Binary Moon:
  first such site the author saw; CSS-Tricks: "has championed this idea"; Coyier's
  commenters: with Trent Walton, "the best examples out there") [reported]. Tim Brown
  says much of his own work improved through Santa Maria's feedback or example at Typekit
  [reported].
