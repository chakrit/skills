# Rob Weychert: words

Stream `words`: what Rob Weychert says about his work, in his writing, the text of his
site and one interview transcript. Collected 2026-11-24 from live pages with `curl`.

Every claim is marked **[stated]** (Weychert said it), **[observed]** (a page shows it)
or **[reported]** (someone else said it), with the URL it comes from. Quotes are short;
the rest is paraphrase. `rw.com` stands for `https://robweychert.com`. Many posts carry
an "Also posted on" link to an earlier version of his site or to ProPublica; the date
given is the post's own date.

## Findings

### Process: what comes first, what is settled before layout, what a client sees

- **Research, then thumbnails, then a grid from the chosen thumbnail, then grayscale,
  colour last** (2007). [stated] He begins with research (a day to two weeks), including
  the information architecture and the client's brand. Then thumbnail sketches on paper,
  "anywhere between ten and a hundred", and he keeps sketching after he thinks he has
  the answer so as not to get attached. He then does "some math to figure out a rational
  grid structure" for the chosen sketch, blocks every element in grayscale in Photoshop
  or Illustrator, refines "hierarchy, balance, whitespace, typography", and "Color is
  usually the final detail". (2007-interview)
- **Black and white, then grayscale, then colour mapped onto the grayscale values**
  (2018, 2019). [stated] "The first steps of my color process are unchanged: solidifying
  the layout in binary black and white before layering in more nuanced contrast in
  grayscale." Black and white is for size, shape and position; grayscale adds contrast;
  then he extracts a palette of named values (six on V6) to map colours onto. (v6-color)
  For Tinnitus Tracker he "began my color exploration like I usually do, by working out
  contrast in grayscale", arriving at six values tested for WCAG contrast. (date-color)
- **Inventory the content, then the scale, then typefaces, then columns** (2017).
  [stated] For V6 he listed "pretty much every species of text" the site would hold,
  reduced it to seven classes, and found they fit four sizes. "Most of what I design that
  incorporates type has a typographic scale as its foundation, which informs the typeface
  choices and layout proportions." The scale's creation "begins by asking what the type
  needs to do". Typefaces were then evaluated against the scale, and the column
  proportions were taken from the same interval. "The content is the arbiter of the
  layout." (v6-type)
- **Content priorities and structure before visual design** (2020). [stated] V7 started
  from bare-bones HTML with a 75ch max width; he settled which content mattered most
  (projects over the timeline) before "the visual part of the design process", because
  "their structure is the site's structure". (v7-content) He took a content inventory by
  data category and source (v7-inventory), then made sketches, wireframes in Sketch, a
  functional prototype, and planned "light user testing". (v7-timeline)
- **Start from the convention, then ask how it falls short** (2018). [stated] "What is
  the most conventional way of solving that problem? Once we have applied that
  convention, how is that convention insufficient". For an article: flow text into a
  standard layout, read the story, ask "How might I hear it better?", and add or subtract
  to answer "the very specific problem to be solved as opposed to the category of
  problem". (ub-podcast)
- **Concepts shown as rough wireframe mockups, then a prototype** (2018). [stated] For
  ProPublica's Lost Mothers he worked with the reporter and editors "almost like a client
  relationship": initial concepts, talking through benefits and drawbacks, "very basic
  wire framey mockups", then a prototype. He rarely makes high-fidelity mockups and is
  looking for "some kind of middle ground between wireframe and the finished piece and
  code". He called diving into code early "a bad habit" he is trying to break with more
  sketching. (ub-podcast) [reported] Andy Clarke relays that ProPublica design director
  David Sleight said Weychert moves "very quickly from sketches into code". (ub-podcast)
- **How a design changes: the Lost Mothers iterations** (2018). [stated] A planned
  yearbook-style gallery became a dot grid of 800 slots; a side list was folded into the
  grid; plain dots looked static, so they tried hinting at the contents, then showing
  photos; a prototype tested the feel; the grid collapses into a bar showing position in
  the chronology while reading. (snd-talk)
- **Client stakeholder process** (The Markup, undated project page). [stated] He started
  from reader-survey data and the client's list of ambitions, ran exercises to reach
  consensus on priorities, agreed three jobs for the homepage, and went through "several
  rounds of mockups and discussion". Deliverables were documentation, Figma mockups and
  an HTML/CSS template, in two weeks. (p-markup)
- **Some systems must be planned before coding** (2019). [stated] His show-list grid
  partitions are "not the kind of thing that can be designed in the browser. It needs to
  be carefully planned out before it's coded." (partitioning)
- **Reduction** (undated, about page). [stated] "I tend to keep taking pieces away until
  the thing breaks, and then put the last one back in." (about)
- **Letting ideas percolate** (2025). [stated] On V7 he stepped away "for months at a
  time" and each return made the vision "both more expansive and more refined".
  (v7-launch)
- **Art-directing a collaborator** (2020). [stated] For Robtober 2020 he gave letterer
  Jen Mussari seven reference logos with a note on what he liked in each (size contrast,
  upward-sloping baseline, sharp terminals, dry-brush texture), then gave round-by-round
  feedback on numbered concepts until one was chosen and cleaned up. (mussari)
- **Starting points other than layout.** [stated] The Beyond Tellerrand 2022 sequence
  "started with the music", which led him to Reid Miles and Saul Bass. (p-bt2022) The
  All Day Hey! 2023 sequence took the mantra "show your work" and exposes its grid in
  its first frames. (p-adh2023)

### Reasons given for particular moves

- **A 66-character line; elastic layout** (2007). [stated] A 45–75 character range,
  "Many typesetters revere an approximately 66-character line as ideal, and I am among
  them". He rejected liquid layouts (lines too long when wide) and fixed layouts
  (horizontal scrolling), and chose em-based elastic width; when it must give, "a
  too-short line is easier to read than one that is too long". (2007-chapter)
- **Indents, not blank lines; never indent the first paragraph** (2007). [stated] An
  indent marks a new passage "while maintaining the text's cohesion"; a square indent
  (2.1em in Georgia); indenting the first paragraph or combining indents with blank
  lines is redundant. On the web indents suit short texts, because long web text needs
  the relief a blank line gives. (2007-chapter)
- **Drop cap, then all caps, then lowercase text** (2007). [stated] A drop cap
  ("versal") introduces the text "with dignified fanfare"; a short run of letterspaced
  all caps (0.13em) bridges the drop cap and the small body text; titling figures in
  caps; faux small caps kept away from full caps so their artifice shows less.
  (2007-chapter)
- **Two typefaces split by what the text does** (2017). [stated] V6 text was either
  "structural, navigational assistance" or "speaking more expressively in my voice"; a
  serif (Freight Text) "reserved exclusively for when I'm speaking directly to the
  reader", a slightly condensed geometric sans (Ciutadella) for metadata, labels,
  headings and block quotes, condensed "so big headings wouldn't wrap too aggressively
  on small screens". (v6-type)
- **One sans with consistent stroke, weight inverse to size** (2026). [stated] "I want
  all the type on my site to look like it was drawn with one of two technical pens."
  Reason: a big site with much text needs a limited set of sizes, and a limited set of
  stroke widths extends that; high-contrast faces are out; geometric sans is too
  "sterile" for his own prose, so Degular "splits the difference". Base 24, interval
  1 1/3, weight divided by the interval as size rises; borders at 1/16 rem; icons drawn
  on a 16×16 grid. The result is "essentially invisible to most viewers" but brings "a
  certain calm". (v7-type)
- **Few sizes on a scale** (2019). [stated] "More is less. The more sizes you use, the
  less they'll mean." Too small an interval loses distinction; too large makes display
  type "more graphic than textual". (modern-scale) On V6 most text used two sizes, with
  two more "used sparingly for special emphasis". (v6-type)
- **Break the system by eye when it looks better** (2017). [stated] He raised the
  smallest size from 12px to 13.5px off-scale: "The system is the foundation, but it
  doesn't have the final say—I do." (v6-type)
- **rem sizing that respects the reader's browser setting** (2019, 2026). [stated] "I
  very rarely use px units"; body text is "the core unit of measure", as in a printed
  book. (v7-type, modern-scale)
- **Responsive over adaptive, with column proportions from the type scale** (2017).
  [stated] He usually prefers responsive layout because adaptive designs "feel at least
  a little bit retrofitted"; V6 columns used the scale's numbers (padding 12, gutter 27,
  column and margin 40.5); text maxes at eight columns "to keep the line length in
  check", outer columns hold figures and asides. (v6-type)
- **ProPublica framework: centred text, elements placed on a 4–14 column grid** (2022).
  [stated] Text spans the middle eight of 14 columns for comfortable line length.
  Elements take one of 15 sizes, align centre/left/right, can be pushed in or pulled out
  up to three columns, wrap or interrupt text. Layout "works its magic by modulating
  visual rhythm and contrast"; an image "small, off-center and surrounded by generous
  white space offers a different kind of contrast and tells a different story".
  (pp-framework)
- **Layout that carries the story's structure** (2018). [stated] For a story reported
  from Colorado and Washington, the headline "juts left and right from a central axis"
  and the text column shifts right, then left, between the two, converging at the end: "a
  dead simple minor deviation from a well-worn convention". (snd-talk)
- **Empty space as the message** (2018). [stated] The Lost Mothers grid is mostly empty
  because the missing data "was itself a big part of the story". (snd-talk, ub-podcast)
- **Don't let information type dictate form** (2018). [stated] From Eric Meyer: CSS can
  make a table look like something other than a table; "Don't get stuck on what
  different types of information are 'supposed' to look like." Under the dot grid "it's
  just a regular old bullet list". (snd-talk)
- **Handbill-like grid for show lists** (2019). [stated] His first, orderly table designs
  were "all head and no heart"; he took the dense, haphazard Trocadero handbills as the
  model. Partition patterns aim at roughly equal whitespace per cell (narrow cells span
  two rows next to wide ones, since text is centred), "the appearance of randomness"
  through long repeat cycles, and no orphan cells. Headliners are set in a much bigger
  display face for contrast, wider widths in wider cells. (partitioning)
- **Listings designed for scanning, without one focal point** (2018). [stated] Each
  scanning category (image, artist, date, day, genre, price, venue) gets its own style and
  place; "the listings have no obvious focal point" on purpose, so readers can pick out
  what matters to them. Images get "a uniform square crop" because unpredictable sizes
  disrupt scanning. (listings)
- **Colour tied to date** (2019). [stated] Tinnitus Tracker hues follow the seasons around
  the wheel and pages fade to grayscale with age, so each entry is "kind of like a poster
  for the show it documented". Saturation is hand-tuned per month and lightness adjusted
  by computed luminance for uniform contrast. (date-color)
- **White frames images** (2018). [stated] V6's white "isolating their colors from mine".
  (v6-color)
- **Identity concepts.** [stated] PhilaMOCA's "high-contrast black and white" identity
  draws on "the austere aesthetics of some corners of the late '70s post-punk movement".
  (p-philamoca) Solid Product Design's stylized O depicts "something solidifying".
  (p-solid)
- **Motion is optional** (2021). [stated] Video, animation and scroll effects sit behind
  `prefers-reduced-motion: no-preference`. (robtober-2021)

### What he refuses or dislikes, and why

- **Heavy frameworks and toolchains** (2020). [stated] "I can't abide frameworks like
  React and Bootstrap" because the user shares their complexity cost; he avoids the
  "modern dev stack" because he does not understand it well enough; he avoids remote
  CDNs and hosted font services like Typekit to own every piece. (v7-dependency)
- **Generic design systems and frameworks** (2018). [stated] Generic pattern libraries
  are "outsourcing the work" to someone who doesn't know the problem; the web "was taken
  over by bootstrap layouts" and got "a bit mannered". His own tool, Column Setter, does
  not touch your HTML and is "not prescriptive". (ub-podcast)
- **Committee design** (2018). [stated] "everybody's needs are met on the stakeholder's
  side, but nobody's needs are met on the user's side". (ub-podcast)
- **Superficial interactivity** (2018). [stated] "I'm a big believer in simplicity"; he is
  less interested in "superficially forward thinking" effects, though he worries he
  misses chances by thinking so. (ub-podcast)
- **Functional absolutism and slop** (2025). [stated] Agreeing with Adam Stoddard that
  tech's "functional absolutism" leaves "no room for beauty"; the industry is "rife with
  KPI-obsessed, cookie-cutter, vibe-coded, careless slop". (lowriders) The web is
  "over-engineered, overrun with slop". (about)
- **Third-party platforms** (2017). [stated] Ephemeral and "one-size-fits-all":
  "whatever you're publishing is mostly at the mercy of a designer who's never met you".
  (v6-archive)
- **Craft that shows off** (2011). [stated] "craft is only as valuable as the art it
  serves"; virtuosity is a problem "when it wants its hard work to be appreciated as much
  as (or more than) the product". (2011-artifice)
- **Specific typographic failures.** [stated] Centred blobs of listings where "each line
  begins in a different place"; red headliners with lower luminance than white text that
  undercut emphasis; unneeded detail like "Presented by". (listings) Redundant paragraph
  marking (2007-chapter). Browser default list markers and table styles (v7-inventory;
  his post "Customizing ordered list styles", 2026-06-24, title and excerpt only).
- **Unexplained rules** (2006). [stated] A typography rules book with "barely a sentence
  of explanation" for each rule. (2006-commandments)

### How he works with a client, and how he treats phones

- **Client work.** [stated] Research into the client's goals and brand first
  (2007-interview); survey data, consensus exercises, ranked goals, mockup rounds
  (p-markup); reporters and editors treated "almost like a client relationship" with
  concepts weighed openly (ub-podcast); a brand guide "with helpful guardrails" (p-solid);
  CMS controls usable "without a design degree or coding skills" (p-propublica). Rule of
  thumb for the screen: "Guide your users; don't smother them." (2007-interview)
- **Phones.** [stated] "the vast, vast, vast majority" of readers are on "a tiny little
  screen", yet designers, himself included, "fetishize" large layouts; the phone is
  "still very much this just tall stacked thin layout" and he expects more can be done
  there (ub-podcast, 2018). In the ProPublica framework the grid drops to four columns on
  phones, indents scale away, text wrap is ignored when there isn't room, small images
  can be forced full width, a different crop can be served (wide for large, close for
  small; a bar chart turned vertical), and rows of images become stacks (pp-framework).
  His concert-listing proposal drops the image column on small screens (listings). His
  show-list grids stack in one column on very small viewports (partitioning). For
  Robtober 2022 he rearranged the title letters into a vertical version for tall
  viewports (robtober-2022); in 2021 he accepted that mandatory scroll snap may cut off
  content "on tiny phones held sideways" (robtober-2021). He also asks how a page reads in
  an RSS feed, Instapaper or Pocket (ub-podcast).
- **Exception.** [stated] For the one-night game show he did not have to "worry so much"
  about responsive design and accessibility, "for once" (lifetime).

### What he believes about design in general

- [stated] Interface design's success is measured by "its invisibility" (2014-pixels).
  He has asked whether design should sometimes "obstruct rather than elucidate", since
  people retain what they work for (2012-obstruction).
- [stated] Print is "a wise old man", the web "a child, unformed and full of potential";
  the job is teaching the child the old man's lessons without "mere transferral"
  (2007-interview, 2007-chapter).
- [stated] Constraints define shape: "shapes are defined by boundaries" (v6-type); design
  within constraints (2007-interview).
- [stated] Systems should welcome tweaks: "Have a system in place that welcomes them"
  (v6-type).
- [stated] Simple informational websites "tend to be the best kind"; handmade sites built
  from simple shared ideas "recombin[ed] ... with rigorous specificity" (v7-dependency).
- [stated] Art direction on the web now has to accommodate readers "who might not be able
  to even see what we're designing for them" (ub-podcast).
- [stated] Craft for its own sake has a place: "we could use more lowriders" (lowriders).
- [stated] He aims for work that serves "the common good" and "underserved audiences"
  (about).

### Where he changed his mind, with dates

- **From self-expression to clarity, and partly back.** [stated] With client work his
  priorities shifted "from manic self-expression to straightforward clarity" (about,
  undated). On 2026-06-03 he wrote that his talk title "From Self-Centered to
  User-Centered" now "strikes me as a false dichotomy"; "my work's function flattened its
  form" (moving-on). His art practice now leaves room for "more messy and organic
  impulses" (about).
- **Colour.** [stated] Long his weakest area, avoided on V5; by 2018 he built a system
  with HSL and Sass and random schemes (v6-color).
- **Typeface for his own voice.** [stated] In 2017 a serif was reserved for speaking to
  the reader (v6-type); in 2026 he chose a low-contrast sans, Degular, for that prose
  (v7-type).
- **Scale interval.** [stated] 1.5 on V6 (2017, v6-type); 1 1/3 from a 24 base on V7
  (2026, v7-type).
- **Mockup tools.** [stated] In 2018 Sketch was "a real stumbling block" for type
  (ub-podcast); in 2020 he was "finally coming around" to Sketch and Figma "after years
  of avoiding them" (v7-timeline).
- **Code early vs. sketch first.** [stated] 2007: many paper thumbnails before anything
  else (2007-interview). 2018: diving into code too early had become "a bad habit"
  (ub-podcast).
- **Fixed print-like layout.** [stated] 2018: he misses designing the web "the way that I
  would a print design" but has "really come to like" responsive design (ub-podcast).
- **CSS layout method.** [stated] 2017: Grid "is undeniably the future" (v6-type); 2018:
  Grid cannot float, a "nontrivial impediment" for editorial layouts (grid-floats).
- **Short posts and publishing tools.** [stated] 2012: hand-editing files made each post
  "more consequential" (2012-inefficiency); 2020: a page for a few words "doesn't bother
  me so much anymore" (v7-structure); 2023: laborious files "discourage smaller posts",
  so he built a local CMS-like UI (v7-procrastination).
- **Career.** [stated] 2023: shifting focus "from design to art" (groome); 2026: plans to
  decouple art from paid work and explore archival work (moving-on).
