Bring me a book that has come apart, and I will sew it back through the holes it already has.

I rebind ships’ logs, account books and family Bibles, mostly from the Maine coast and mostly damp. I resew each one on its original sewing stations, so the paper gets no new holes. A careful rebinding takes about thirty-one hours at my bench, spread over three or four weeks while things dry.

Here is one book going through the shop, the Lettie B.’s log, from the morning it came up the stairs to the afternoon it went home. Hours are counted at the bench; drying time is not.

Taking it apart

Hours 0 to 3 · scalpel, pencil, a sheet of graph paper

I lift the old covers and cut the rotten thread, then count every leaf and draw a map of how the sections nest. Someone in 1846 sewed this on three tapes, with the stations about two inches apart. I write those positions down, because they decide everything later.

Washing

Hours 3 to 9 · trays, blotters, a weighted board

Salt water leaves a tide line on paper. The sheets go into a bath of plain water, one section at a time, and come out between blotters under a board. The ink in this log is iron gall and holds. Drying takes a week; I count only the hours I’m standing over it.

Mending the folds

Hours 9 to 14 · Japanese tissue, wheat starch paste

Where a fold has split I lay a strip of thin tissue along it with paste cooked that morning. Mended folds are a little thicker than the paper around them, so I guard every section in the book to keep the spine even.

Sewing

Hours 14 to 20 · the frame, linen tapes, 18/3 linen thread, beeswax

The frame holds three linen tapes upright, set to the positions I wrote down on the first day. Each section goes on in turn and the needle passes through the old holes, round the tape, and back in. This is the step in the photograph at the top of the page. A log of this size has forty-two sections, and I sew it in one sitting if nobody calls.

Rounding and backing

Hours 20 to 23 · glue, a hammer, backing boards, the press

The sewn spine is glued, tapped round with a hammer, then clamped between bevelled boards so the outer sections fold over a little and make shoulders for the covers to sit against. That curve is what lets a ledger lie open on a chart table.

Covering

Hours 23 to 28 · new calf, the old leather, paring knife

New boards, new calf. What survives of the original leather I pare thin and set back over the new, so the owner still sees the scuffs their great-grandfather made. The book sits under weight for two days afterward.

Lettering

Hours 28 to 31 · brass type, gold leaf, a small stove

The title goes on the spine by hand, one letter at a time, with heated brass type pressed through gold leaf. For this one: LOG, the ship’s name, and the year. Then it goes home in a box.

Most steps wait on drying, so I keep the tide table on the wall and plan by it: paste down on one high water, open the press on the next.

Bring a book

Write to ruth@tidewaterbindery.com or call 207 555 0148 and tell me what it is and what has gone wrong with it. A photograph of the spine helps.

Then bring it up: 58 Commercial Street, the door beside the chandlery, second floor. I’m there Tuesday to Saturday, nine to four. Carry it in a paper bag or a pillowcase rather than plastic, because old damp paper sweats in plastic and grows mould by the next morning.
