Two acres of oysters, worked in the order the water allows.

Low water

05:12Low water, 0.4 m, slack

We are on the flats by five with head torches and a sack of spare clips. At low water the trestles stand clear of the sea in rows of forty, and every mesh bag on them gets turned by hand: lifted, shaken, flipped, clipped back down. Turning knocks off the thin new edge of each shell, so the oysters grow deep instead of long.

Kerra counts bags; Dan checks for the ones the storm on Tuesday worked loose. There are 1,140 of them, and an hour and a half before the water comes back.

The water comes back over the flats and we carry the last sack up the slip.

Flood

08:123 h after low water, 2.1 m, rising

While the tide fills the cove we grade in the shed. Every oyster goes through a sizing riddle and back into a bag with others its own size, so the small ones are not crowded out by their neighbours.

An oyster from Pennick takes three years to reach the plate. It is the same animal in each drawing below, kept track of through every grading, one year apart.

High water

11:31High water, 4.1 m, slack

The lease is under four metres of sea. Nothing on it can be reached, so nobody tries.

Ebb

14:313 h after high water, 2.0 m, falling

By the afternoon ebb the shed on Pennick Quay has shut for the week. It opens on Saturdays from nine until one, and we sell what came out of the water that week and nothing older; when it is gone, the door shuts.
