# Yugo Nakamura: composition of logics

Design a page by building one behaviour first and letting the layout grow out of it.
Distilled from Yugo Nakamura's (tha ltd.) interviews, tha's own sites and project texts,
the work that can still be seen, and other people's accounts. Written in our own words.

A page in this style is a quiet, exact frame around one thing that moves for a reason
you can feel: the pointer, the minute, a beat, the visitor's own data. Monochrome ground,
small neutral type, colour only where the piece is. It suits businesses with something
that runs, repeats or keeps time (shops with hours and queues, makers with a process,
studios, schedules, anything with a counter or a clock) and brand pages where a strong
mark can become material. It suits pages poorly where the visitor must read a long text
or finish a form fast; there the expressive layer shrinks to almost nothing.

Work from the brick up. Each section below is a step; do them in order.

## 1. Find the brick

Do not start from a sitemap, a hero and sections. Start from one small thing that is
interesting on its own, and ask what page would make it shine.

Look for the brick in this order:

1. **A familiar object, driven by a foreign rule.** Take something everyone reads at once
   (a clock, a timetable, a queue ticket, a logo, a list, a calendar, a receipt, a map)
   and give it one behaviour from somewhere else. The familiar form makes the new rule
   legible.
2. **The business's own process, made literal.** What runs inside the business: dough
   proving, drums spinning, ink drums printing in order, water heating, orders queueing.
   That process can be the force behind the page.
3. **The business's mark as a unit.** A strong, simple mark can become one cell that is
   tiled, scaled, rotated and recoloured into the page.
4. **The visitors as material.** Many small contributions (names, choices, check-ins,
   orders) can add up to one picture on one shared surface.

His bricks, for calibration:

| Familiar thing      | Foreign rule                                     | Piece                   |
|---------------------|--------------------------------------------------|-------------------------|
| A clock             | numerals dropped into water each minute          | DropClock               |
| A screensaver       | moved by live wind data in your city             | Kaze to Desktop         |
| A message board     | each message becomes a leaf on one growing tree  | ecotonoha               |
| A search result     | the results laid out in the letters of the query | amaztype                |
| A square logo       | one shared board of logo tiles everyone moves    | UNIQLO GRID             |
| A clock             | spun from the colours of your own photos         | LIFE COLOR CLOCK        |
| A phone home screen | icon, widget and app as S, M and L of one module | iida UI for INFOBAR A01 |

Write the brick in the brief as one line: *the familiar thing* + *the rule*.

## 2. Name the force

Every movement on the page is the visible effect of a cause. Name the cause before
drawing anything:

- the pointer or finger (position, speed, a drag, a tap);
- the time (the second, the minute, the hour, the day of the week, the season);
- a beat or a schedule the business keeps;
- the visitor's own input or data;
- a physical law applied to the elements (gravity, springs, flocking, friction).

Then caption the piece the way his lab-notebook site did: an all-caps line that names the
logic first and the instruction second, for example `HOURS DRAWN AS A FILLING BATH. MOVE
ACROSS TO STIR.` Keep the caption on the page as a quiet label beside the piece.

A movement without a named force is decoration; remove it. No scroll-triggered fades, no
eased entrances, no parallax for its own sake.

## 3. Sketch in running code

He cannot draw, so his sketch is a program. Do the same: build the brick in the browser,
alone on a blank page, before any layout.

- Get the behaviour right first: does it feel like a consequence of the force?
- Make the timing exact. If it follows a beat or a clock, it lands on the beat or the
  second, not near it.
- Keep a few gestures with physical meaning (drag to move, tap to flip). One or two verbs
  are enough.
- A first version is a demo to show, not a layout to defend. Keep the ones that do not
  make it; they are stock for later.

## 4. Multiply it

Go from part to whole: one brick, then two or three side by side, then imagine a hundred.

- Let the multiplied brick decide the house. If twelve of it want a ruled board, the page
  is a board. If it wants to fill the screen behind everything, it fills the screen.
- Allow the house to come out larger or odder than the brief imagined. Breaking the
  arranged harmony is the point.
- When the units vary in size, design them as one family (S, M, L) that looks right in
  any order. Align their widths and stack them downward; let the column count change
  with the width (two to five). Draw units with alignment, space and their own content;
  never put each in a filled, bordered or shadowed box with an icon, a title and a blurb.

## 5. Lay the plain layer beside it

Expression and information run as two layers in parallel. Do not fuse them.

- **The expressive layer** is the brick, multiplied. It may sit behind the content,
  beside it, or be the content. It may lean toward the computer's side and ask a little
  of the visitor.
- **The plain layer** is the business's information: what it is, hours, prices, where,
  how to reach it. Set it as a quiet list in small type, left-aligned, in reading order.
  Navigation lives here and behaves exactly as people expect.
- Prefer the list as the interface. A stack of names, times or items in small type can
  be the whole navigation, with the expressive layer reacting to it (hovering an item
  plays or highlights its part of the piece). The list stays put while the work moves.
- No hero banner, no intro paragraph about the business's passion, no section of
  feature blurbs. The brick is the first screen.
- Keep the frame plain. The business's index does not perform; the piece does.

## 6. Choose type late, by swapping it in

The behaviour exists first. Then try faces in it until one fits.

- Use one family, two at most, from the quiet ones that "have nothing unpleasant": a
  neo-grotesque with a thin and a regular weight (his own site uses Helvetica Neue Thin
  and Roman), a business-like geometric sans, a DIN-like engineered sans, or one calm
  Mincho when the subject asks for a serif. Avoid faces with a strong personality.
- Small sizes carry the frame: labels, list items, captions and navigation at about
  13–15 px, often in all caps, tracked a little, in a grey a step back from the ground.
  Body text, where there is any, at 16 px or larger in sentence case.
- Large type, when it appears, is thin and very large: a numeral, a time, a single name.
- When the site uses a non-Latin script, choose the Latin face to sit with it (matching
  weight, x-height and colour), not the other way round. Japanese and Latin may mix in
  the same list.
- Type may move, as display, a title, a wordmark, a caption or an idle state. A short
  all-caps line may decode itself from random characters into its text, one character
  stepping into place per frame. Body text never moves.
- Letters may be the material of the picture: items arranged into the shape of a word, a
  numeral built from the units of the brick. Treat the whole word or numeral evenly.

## 7. Colour from the work

- The ground is black, white or a near-neutral. The frame's type is grey on it.
- Colour belongs to the expressive layer. Take it from the business's real material (an
  ink, a tile, a crust, a fabric) and use it flat and saturated, or not at all.
- A graphic piece may use several flat colours as cells on a neutral board, like the
  UNIQLO GRID board. A photographic or physical subject keeps to one or two.
- Do not paint the physical product on the screen. No textures, no fake materials, no
  skeuomorphic chrome. The virtual layer stays neutral, ordered and fast.

## 8. Nudge, then subtract

- Go over every distance, size and timing: tighten here, loosen there, until it sits
  exactly right. Redo motion details many times.
- Remove what irritates: a jump, a flicker, a label that collides, a step that lags.
  Comfort comes from taking away the conspicuous faults one by one.
- Then pull something out on purpose: an element, a colour, a section, a feature. If the
  page is better without it, leave it out.
- Do nothing unnecessary. Placement and setting carry the design more than the amount of
  content.

## 9. Check the measure

- About seven parts of the page should be understood at once and three should not, until
  the visitor plays. Fully explained is boring; fully opaque fails the business. The
  plain layer guarantees the seven.
- Keep slightly betraying expectation: the brick should do one thing the visitor did not
  predict.
- Keep the charm. Fun, light, a little whimsical, never solemn about itself. No copy
  explaining the meaning of the piece.

## Phones

- The finger replaces the pointer. A force driven by hover needs a touch or time-driven
  form: tap to trigger, drag to move, or the clock drives it on its own.
- Stack modules downward with widths aligned; drop the column count, keep the family.
- The brick survives at full strength on a phone; it is the first screen there too, under
  at most a short header (the name, a line of facts, the piece's caption). The rest of the
  plain layer follows below or sits over it as the same small list.
- A gesture can produce the composition (his UTme! rearranges a drawing when the phone is
  shaken); use one only when the brick suggests it.

## Still state

When the visitor prefers reduced motion, stop the movement and show the piece at one
meaningful moment of its force: the clock still tells the right time, the board shows its
current state, the decoded line shows as text. The force must still read without motion.

## Refuse

- Motion with no cause: fades and slides on scroll, eased entrances, parallax, hover
  lifts.
- A layout composed first with motion sprinkled on after.
- Hero, three feature cards, testimonials, a call-to-action band.
- Filled or bordered tiles holding an icon, a title and a blurb, in any arrangement.
- A small tracked caps label or a number stamped above a section heading. Small caps
  belong to the list, the captions, the navigation and the meta.
- Any emphasis on a word inside a headline. Give the whole line one treatment.
- Copy that explains what the piece means, or a setup with an emphasised payoff. Name
  what it does and how to use it, as a caption does.
- Painted materials, textures and fake hardware.
- Expressive tricks in the navigation or the forms. The plain layer stays plain.
- More than two typefaces; faces chosen for personality.

## Judge it

Answer each in one line:

- What is the brick: which familiar thing, which foreign rule?
- What force moves each moving thing? Is anything moving without one?
- Does the motion land exactly on its beat or second?
- Are the expressive layer and the plain layer separate, and is the plain one fully
  usable on its own?
- Is the brick the first screen at desktop and phone widths?
- What would a visitor not predict? Is about a third left to discover, and no more?
- What was pulled out, and is anything left that is unnecessary?
- Is colour only in the piece, taken from the business's own material?
- Could this belong to another business in the same trade? If yes, the brick is generic.
