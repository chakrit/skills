## 1. Moves

### 1.1 Start from the convention, then ask how this specific story breaks it

- **What.** His stated method, in order: name the problem; apply "the most conventional
  way of solving that problem"; then ask "how is that convention insufficient", and add
  or subtract until the design answers "the very specific problem to be solved as opposed
  to the category of problem". For an article: flow text into a standard layout, read the
  story, then ask "How might I hear it better? What if this happened?" [S: Unfinished
  Business 2018]
- **Replaces.** Either inventing a layout from scratch or stopping at the category
  default (the template, the framework).
- **Why he says.** Generic frameworks and pattern libraries are "outsourcing the work" to
  someone who does not know the problem. His complaint is not sameness as such but that
  people skip finding "what makes the thing that they're working on unique and special".
  [S: Unfinished Business 2018]
- **When it fits.** Any page with a real subject. For an agent: build the plain version
  first, read it as a reader, and change only what makes this content clearer.

### 1.2 Make the layout enact the story's structure, with one small deviation

- **What.** In the ProPublica / Marshall Project rape-investigation story (12,000 words,
  two reporters, two states) the design team argued for keeping the narrative split by
  place. The headline "juts left and right from a central axis"; the text column starts
  centred, then shifts right for one state's sections and left for the other's, "east
  and west between Colorado and Washington", until the threads "converge and intertwine"
  at the end. He calls it "a dead simple minor deviation from a well-worn convention
  (the single text column)" that was "surprisingly effective". [S: SND 2018 talk]
- **Replaces.** The single centred reading column used for every long read, which he
  says persists partly because "we've learned to assume that's just how it is". [S]
- **When it fits.** Long text with two (or more) parallel threads, places or voices.
  Move the column, not the whole page; one variable carries the structure.

### 1.3 Information takes whatever shape you need: restyle the plain element

- **What.** The talk's thesis, adapted from an Eric Meyer line ("CSS doesn't care what
  you think an element looks like"): "Design doesn't care what you think information
  looks like." His demo takes a plain HTML table of his most-shared link topics, styles
  it cleanly, then restyles the same table as a bar chart because the data is ordered
  by frequency and meant for comparison. [S: SND 2018 talk]
- The Lost Mothers grid of 800 dots is, "under the hood, when we take away the styles,
  … just a regular old bullet list". [S: SND 2018 talk]
- **Replaces.** "Tunnel vision" that a table must look like rows and columns and a list
  like bulleted lines. [S]
- **When it fits.** Any list or table whose order, count or proportion is the point.
  Keep semantic markup; let the visual form follow what the data is for.

### 1.4 Show the absence: empty slots as the message

- **What.** For Lost Mothers (US maternal deaths in 2016, est. 700–900; researchers
  identified about 134), the brief was a gallery of faces. He and David Sleight judged a
  "yearbook page of unfamiliar faces" unengaging and mostly empty, and realised the lack
  of data was itself the story. The design became an 800-dot grid where filled dots are
  the known women, outlined in red where a vignette exists; the grid is also the
  navigation, and once the reader scrolls into the vignettes it collapses into a
  horizontal bar at the top showing where they are in the year's chronology. [S: SND
  2018 talk; S: Unfinished Business 2018]
- He traces the iteration: dot grid plus a side list (list "wasn't much of an
  improvement"); dots alone (looked static, not obviously interactive); a rotating
  animation surfacing names and photos; finally photos shown permanently in the grid,
  then a prototype "to see how the interaction would feel". [S: SND 2018 talk]
- The aim, he says, was "not to shock people necessarily" but to show "how little is
  really known". [S: Unfinished Business 2018]
- **When it fits.** Data where coverage is partial. Draw the whole population at full
  scale and fill only what is known; do not hide the gap.

### 1.5 A grid you define, not one you are handed

- **What.** Column Setter (ProPublica, 2017–18): four ratio variables (outer margin,
  column, gutter, padding; example 4 : 4 : 2 : 1, so margins equal columns, columns are
  twice the gutter, gutters twice the padding) and named breakpoints with column counts
  (example 4 / 8 / 12 at 0 / 30em / 60em). One `colspan()` function returns percentage
  widths; elements may span wider than their container (8 columns centred inside 4).
  [S: Column Setter article]
- Two rules he set for it: it must not "touch your HTML" (no `six-col` classes) and must
  not be "prescriptive" like a 960 grid you "shoehorn your stuff into"; you "define the
  parameters … within some certain constraints". [S: Unfinished Business 2018]
- Grids "help ensure decisions aren't made arbitrarily". [S: Column Setter article] He
  also built ProPublica's first type scale; Sleight says it removes "lots of ad-hoc
  decisions" so new features have "some harmony between the pieces". [R: Source Q&A
  2017]
- In 2007 he described the same habit by hand: after choosing a thumbnail, "do some math
  to figure out a rational grid structure for it". [S: Design Interviews 2007]
- **Replaces.** Off-the-shelf 12-column framework classes.
- **When it fits.** Always set the grid from proportions chosen for this design (margin,
  column, gutter, padding as small-integer ratios) and derive widths from it.

### 1.6 Grey first, colour last

- **What.** His 2007 process: research; thumbnails; a grid from the chosen thumbnail;
  block in every element "in grayscale"; "Refine, refine, refine: hierarchy, balance,
  whitespace, typography. Color is usually the final detail to be dealt with." [S:
  Design Interviews 2007]
- **When it fits.** Settle hierarchy and spacing before colour, so colour cannot prop up
  a weak structure.

### 1.7 Constraints as the engine

- **What.** "Use restrictions and limitations" was a section of After the Brief; the
  examples (a daily haiku in 5-7-5 using the word of the day; a 48-hour film made with
  whatever is at hand) are his own practices, since his blog ran a "haiku" series and his
  events list includes the 48 Hour Film Project, 2004. [R: LukeW notes; O: slide titles
  "Five Seven Five", "48-Hour Films"; O: his blog and events page]
- He gives a concrete image: driving a ten-lane highway without lane lines would be a
  nightmare, a reminder of "the value of designing within constraints". [S: Design
  Interviews 2007]
- **Link to later work (inference, not stated in these sources).** The same instinct
  runs through the rule-set-and-enumerate method of Plus Equals; the `own` and `work`
  streams cover that directly.

### 1.8 Strip to the material, then decide what to add

- **What.** "I'm a big believer in simplicity … just get rid of any extra stuff."
  For him that often means "boiling things down past like fancy interactivity" to "Here's
  the stuff. Here's the imagery. Here are the colors and the shapes and the type". He
  distrusts work that is "superficially forward thinking" (bells and whistles) but
  worries this makes him miss chances, so he aims for a base layer, "Here is the thing,
  unadorned", with occasional real experiments on top. [S: Unfinished Business 2018]
- "Guide your users; don't smother them." His one rule of thumb for screen design. [S:
  Design Interviews 2007]

## 2. Process and thinking

- **Print lessons, taught to the web.** "Print is a wise old man who is set in his ways,
  whereas the web is still a child"; his job is "taking the lessons I've learned from
  that old man and teaching them to the child", working out where the two "converge and
  diverge". [S: Design Interviews 2007] His education was all print; he was drawn to
  editorial design and inspired by David Carson as a student. [S: Unfinished Business
  2018]
- **Responsive as fulfilment, not loss.** Responsive design finally let the web reconcile
  flexibility with typographic principles "like line length". He misses designing a fixed
  page like print, but likes thinking about how a piece reads in an RSS feed or Pocket,
  and calls that "the promise of the web … coming to people on their terms". [S:
  Unfinished Business 2018] He thinks designers still "fetishize" big-screen layouts when
  most readers are on phones, where layouts remain "tall stacked thin", and expects
  better small-screen storytelling ideas to come. [S: Unfinished Business 2018]
- **Art direction redefined.** It used to mean control and self-expression; now it must
  accommodate a fluid medium and readers "who might not be able to even see what we're
  designing for them". [S: Unfinished Business 2018]
- **Sketch versus code.** In 2007: thumbnails in quantity (10 to 100), and keep sketching
  after you think you have the answer "to avoid getting too attached to an idea". [S:
  Design Interviews 2007] By 2018 he admits a "bad habit of just diving into code way too
  early" and wants to return to sketching and rapid iteration; he rarely makes
  high-fidelity comps and is looking for a "middle ground between wireframe and the
  finished piece". [S: Unfinished Business 2018] Sleight told Clarke he moves "very
  quickly from sketches into code". [R: Clarke, relaying Sleight]
- **Working with editors.** For Lost Mothers the reporter and editors were treated
  "almost like a client relationship": initial concepts, pros and cons, a few wireframe
  mockups, then a prototype; the concept came together in about a week. [S: Unfinished
  Business 2018] He values the editorial habit of several people bringing expertise to
  one story, and contrasts it with committee design where "everybody's needs are met on
  the stakeholder's side, but nobody's needs are met on the user's side". [S]
- **Ideas from outside the discipline.** The Lost Mothers dot grid came partly from the
  dot-grid floor of the elevator in ProPublica's building: "Oh! That could be an
  interface." He calls this "visual thinking": making non-obvious associations from the
  city, a commute, nature, the art world. He cares less about a designer's cultural
  literacy than this "visual thinking acumen". [S: Unfinished Business 2018; S: SND 2018
  talk]
- **Inspiration is lived, not switched on.** After the Brief's structure: define
  (inspiration vs influence; "the sum of your experiences"), find (design history, "turn
  off your computer" for letterpress and screen printing, storytelling in the physical
  object), maintain (Alan Fletcher's "Design is not a thing you do. It's a way of life",
  *The Art of Looking Sideways*, image archives, sketchbooks, exercises, puzzles, "find a
  muse"). [O: slide text; R: LukeW notes] He says the place inspiration is least likely to
  strike him is "the chair in front of my computer". [S: Design Interviews 2007] He keeps
  design, art and writing undivided: "my creative process really depends on stimuli
  gathered from pretty much everything I do and see", and writing clarifies his thinking
  about design. [S: Design Interviews 2007]
- **"Be a design vigilante."** Redesign bad everyday design you meet (the example was a
  lost-dog flyer) as practice. [R: LukeW notes; O: slide title] (Which speaker owned this
  section is not recorded.)
- **Critique protocol (with Jason Santa Maria).** Before: review everything, specify
  roles, keep attendance small. During: recap past/present/future, manage expectations,
  state the design strategy and the goal, set a time limit; introduce each design "like
  you are introducing a friend at a party"; keep talk on problems, not solutions (the
  designer solves back at the studio); cover every direction ("No Child Left Behind");
  never "Frankenstein" parts of different directions together; avoid jargon, point at a
  printed piece instead of saying "saturation". After: evaluate, document, follow up.
  Joke slides list the top five client requests ("Can't you make the logo bigger?",
  "There's so much empty space. Can't you fill it?"). [R: attendee notes; O: slide
  titles]
- **Design history as a requirement.** "If anyone that doesn't know history is doing
  important work, they're doing it by accident." Designers and developers must speak
  each other's languages, or "the details that make great web design will either fail to
  surface or be lost in translation". In a graduate's portfolio he wants to see "how their
  imagination works in both heavily restrained and wide-open atmospheres". [S: Teach the
  Web 2009]
- **Chess as a design discipline (abstract only).** Knowing how pieces move does not beat
  a master, as knowing a site's building blocks does not make it remarkable; the talk
  promised lessons from "reiterating fundamentals" to "deconstructing problems and
  solutions in unexpected ways". The Chicago version drew on the Blues Brothers, Ira
  Glass and the 1893 World's Columbian Exposition. [S: abstract and blog post] Content
  beyond this is unknown.

## 3. Taste

- **Typography is core.** He could not adopt Sketch partly because "type is so core to
  what I do" and he found it hard to work with type there. [S: Unfinished Business 2018]
  His Web Standards Creativity chapter aimed "to spread principles of timeless, classic
  typography further into the web design world". [S: hunt-at-sxsw post] Design
  Interviews quotes a Web Standards Award citation praising his personal site's "eclectic
  mix of classic and contemporary typefaces and type treatments". [R]
- **Praises (SND 2018 notes).** Scott Stowell / Open's ACLU identity: GT America
  display partly from protest posters, engraving-style images from paper money, Century
  for the "informational voice" because Supreme Court briefs must be set in Century, red
  and blue used together so neither reads partisan, a pocket-sized handbook echoing the
  pocket Constitution ("a damned fine job"). De Correspondent's calm design and its
  sidenotes giving links context. Jennifer Maravillas's trash maps; Candy Chan's subway
  drawings; Greg Lamarche's typographic collage. Allison McCann's Guitar Hero-inspired
  data visualisation for TV, noting that on TV "the designer is scrolling for the
  viewer". [S: SND notes] The pattern: identities whose every choice is drawn from the
  client's own world, and quiet, contextual reading interfaces.
- **Dislikes.** "Bootstrap layouts" taking over the web; "mannered" tunnel vision in the
  industry [S: Unfinished Business 2018]; pixel-perfect comps as a goal; style that
  "overwhelms the substance" (his criticism of one SND speaker); a panel on logos being
  irrelevant with no graphic designer on it [S: SND notes; S: March 2008 tweet, saved
  in raw `v7_blog_2008_03_.txt`]. Generic, prescriptive tools.
- **Influences named here.** Eric Meyer (the CSS line he built a talk on); Jeffrey
  Zeldman's *Designing with Web Standards* ("that book changed my life"); Alan Fletcher;
  Andrew Haslam's *Book Design*; Design Observer and A List Apart; David Carson in his
  student years; John Allsopp's "A Dao of Web Design". [S: Design Interviews 2007;
  Unfinished Business 2018]
- **Voice.** Self-deprecating humour runs through every source (beard jokes, "kung fu
  homicide detective", the Windhammer air-guitar alter ego, a haiku about goats). The
  Happy Cog-era bios stress "neurotically meticulous attention to detail" alongside "a
  unique sense of humor". [S; R: AEA and Design Interviews bios]
- **The open web.** In 2018 his main worry was strengthening the open web against
  people relying on a Facebook page instead of a site. [S: Unfinished Business 2018]
