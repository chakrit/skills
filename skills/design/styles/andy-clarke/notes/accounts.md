# Andy Clarke: outside accounts (stream `accounts`)

What clients, collaborators, reviewers, conference writers and critics say about working
with Andy Clarke and about his design approach. Third-party material on him is thinner
than expected: most of what exists is short client testimonials, book reviews that dwell
on CSS, and blog comment threads from his 2008-2010 "open redesign" projects. Those
comment threads turned out to be the richest outside record of how he runs and defends a
design, so they get the most space here.

Tags used on every claim:

- **[him]** stated by Clarke himself (including his replies inside comment threads)
- **[seen]** observed by me in the work or in the record
- **[other]** reported by someone else (client, reviewer, attendee, critic)

Raw material is in `tmp/andy-clarke/raw/accounts/`. Several sources could only be read
through a summarising fetch tool; those raw files say so on their second line.

## 1. Moves

### 1.1 Lead with an idea that dramatises the client's real promise, then illustrate it

- **What:** Rather than show the product, he finds one metaphor that carries what the
  client actually sells and builds bespoke artwork from it. For Codeboxx, a supplier of
  thermal printheads, he reasoned that thermal printing is tiny black marks carrying
  information. So he rebuilt famous paintings (the Mona Lisa, The Scream, Van Gogh) from QR-code
  textures of graded density to say "keeping production lines moving is an art"
  [him; seen in raw/accounts/pc-codeboxx.txt]. For Lockrose, an energy consultancy
  trying to win transport and public-sector work, he made one large illustrated SVG
  landscape that mixes hydro, nuclear, solar and wind energy, in the manner of mid-century
  modernist magazine covers [him, portfolio-lockrose].
- **Default it replaces:** product photography and a services or catalogue grid. He
  calls this industry's product shots "dull" and the manufacturers' own photos
  "dreadful" [him].
- **What the client said:** Codeboxx: "stand out in our industry with a fresh and unique
  website... bold and different" (Peter O'Flynn). Lockrose: "a breath of fresh air...
  far exceeded our expectations" (Lyndsey Evans) [other, testimonials on his site, so
  self-selected].
- **When it fits:** when the client's real offer is intangible (trust, reliability,
  expertise) and the product looks like everyone else's. The concept came from the
  content audit, not from a style. He says the assumption that changed everything was
  that the problem was trust, not organisation [him].

### 1.2 Make the site a world to explore (toys, mascots, scenes)

- **What:** For creative or personality-led clients the home page becomes an illustrated
  scene the visitor explores. Moonwalk Audio, a game-audio studio, posted him physical
  toys (vintage Japanese robots, figures, cardboard scenery, a plastic dragon). He
  sketched them into one landscape where objects stay muted until hover and then play
  the studio's sounds [him, portfolio-moonwalk-audio]. Mountain Duck, a file-transfer
  app, got its mascot and an animated "file transfer machine" in place of framework
  components; the client's own site "looked like countless other software websites"
  [him]. Mike Worth, a composer, got a Hanna-Barbera-style animated set with an
  orangutan adventurer, looping backgrounds and a quicksand 404 page [him, Smashing 2025].
- **What the clients said:** Mike Worth: "Boom! God, it looks 100% AWESOME!... super
  light and very mobile friendly". Begoña Pereda: "a true representation of who I am...
  a fun and engaging space to explore". Ryan Knaggs: "translated my musical style into a
  brilliant, authentic brand... Everyone mentions how his design perfectly tells my
  story" [other, from raw/accounts/sn-help.txt].
- **Pattern I see:** the testimonials praise *recognisability* ("true representation",
  "tells my story", "unique") far more than conversion or usability [seen]. Clients
  describe the result as a portrait of themselves.
- **Fits / doesn't:** individuals and small studios whose personality is the product.
  Clarke turns a budget limit into the style: there was no money for real animation, so
  he borrowed Hanna-Barbera's cheap-animation tricks and made them the look [him]. These
  are built from SVG and CSS rather than video, and the clients call them "light" and
  "mobile friendly" [other].

### 1.3 Break elements out of the grid, and defend it under critique

- **What:** In the public critique of the CannyBill home page, John Leschinski disliked
  "the logo being on top of the language bar". Clarke replied: "We will have to fight
  over that one John, as I'm a huge fan of making elements break out of the grid to make
  designs look a little less boxy and more fluid" [him, cb-home-2010.txt #23].
- **Default it replaces:** every element neatly inside its row. Even in 2009, with a
  fixed 960px grid set as a background image, he placed the brand mark across a boundary
  on purpose [seen].
- **Note:** this is one of the few places where he gave way on nothing. He accepted most
  public feedback (toning down the background, link contrast, adding a lower call to
  action) but held firm on the overlap and on the site not copying the app's style. See
  1.6.

### 1.4 Structure first in black and white, colour late, theme last

- **What:** On CannyBill, day 1 was content questions sketched on paper, then plain
  elements laid on the grid; he works "in black-and-white as it helps me to focus on
  typographic and layout structure (infact they're the same thing)". Day 3 turned the
  grid off and brought in a palette generated from two colours sampled from the old
  brand. Day 4 added a texture made from a thousand copies of the logo symbol. On day 6
  he "had no preconceived idea" of a dark design but forked into light and dark
  versions after sleeping on it [him, cb-home-2010.txt].
- **Habit worth copying:** he leaves a design open on a second monitor overnight to get
  a fresh first impression. A commenter named it "The Morning After Test" [him / other].
- **What the outside view adds:** public critique converged on the texture being too
  loud: several readers saw flowers, not cogs, and asked what flowers had to do with
  invoicing. He agreed to tone it down [other / him]. So his decorative layer was the
  part most open to negotiation; the structure and the overlap were not.

### 1.5 One look-and-feel comp, then show the client a working page

- **What:** He makes one static look-and-feel design (Fireworks at the time), keeps it in
  the studio, and shows clients a working HTML/CSS page. "At most I will create one page
  as a static image and then I start working with markup and CSS" [him, fabw comment
  #6]. Wireframes come first, "mostly... pencil and paper", sometimes Keynote; detailed
  artwork is still made in Photoshop or Illustrator [him, #14].
- **Why, in his words:** comps teach clients that a page is "a facsimile of a frozen
  image". He asked whether fixed-width centred layouts were "a direct result of clients
  signing off fixed width design visuals". "What you see is what you sign off." Car
  analogy: he wants to "smell the leather", not read the brochure [him].
- **Outside view:** Jeremy Keith: "makes a great case" [other]. Disagreements in the
  44-comment thread [other, fabw-static-visuals.txt]:
  - Not realistic on tight deadlines; layout changes are faster in a graphics tool
    (Martin Ringlein, nclud), and teams of specialists can't do it (Frédéric Hewitt,
    Sam Barnes: "a one man band", Leeanne Lowe: coding unapproved pages is not
    "financially practical").
  - Clients confuse a working mock-up with the finished site and ask why the links don't
    work or why it is in Latin (Carissa).
  - **The design criticism that matters most for us (Dan, Richard, and Jon Hicks
    separately):** sites designed straight in HTML "tend to be very simplistic, using
    only lines, boxes and simple layouts". Clarke conceded "That can be true" and said
    he sometimes thinks it of 37signals' work. His answer was the hybrid: one rich comp
    for look and feel, then build [him].
  - Ross Bruniges and Matt Wilcox worried that clients on old browsers would approve a
    reduced "lo-fi" version.
- **Fits / doesn't:** fits a single designer who builds (his set-up; he says HTML/CSS
  "is part of design", development is the CMS work). Rich art-directed artwork still
  starts in a graphics tool. For AppBaoBao the lesson is the hybrid, not "skip the
  comp".

### 1.6 A marketing site is not bound by the product's look

- **What:** Readers pointed out that the new CannyBill site looked nothing like the app.
  He: "it is really important for me not to be limited by the style of the app... the
  site [has] a very different and distinct purpose and should accomplish its own goals,
  not limited by established aesthetics" [him, cb-home-2010.txt #28].
- **Related:** "when I'm designing, I often don't take too much notice of a company's
  peers or competitors" [him, cb-live-2010.txt, quoting his own "peer research" post].
  But he did deliberately copy Basecamp's pricing-page pattern, because CannyBill's
  audience matched 37signals'. He openly asked when *not* reinventing a convention is
  the right call [him, Unmatched Style].
- **Pattern:** he reinvents the brand-facing surfaces and leaves familiar mechanics
  (pricing tables, calls to action, mental models) alone. That matches his 2017 line that
  art direction "amplif[ies] the content" while keeping familiar mental models and clear
  CTAs [him, Caffeine & Concrete].

### 1.7 Same house system, a different feel per story (from the talk write-up)

- **What:** In the Direction 16 keynote he showed three ProPublica features that keep the
  house fonts and grid while each article gets its own treatment. Layout separates
  voices in "An Unbelievable Story". Commissioned illustration in "El Chapo" conveys the
  violence more strongly than photos could. Image size and placement are pushed in
  "Busted" to stress story points [other: Allsopp write-up, raw/accounts/wd-direction16.txt].
  David Sleight of ProPublica adds the book's opener: ProPublica and The Atlantic treated
  the same cartel story differently, illustration versus documentary evidence, and both
  were valid for their audiences [other, Stuntbox].
- **Grid content reported by Allsopp:** reduce a 12-column grid to six usable columns
  (WWF UK fundraising pages); compound grids "rarely used on the web"; modular grids
  from horizontal fields crossing columns; golden-ratio placement; CSS shapes and "ghost
  type" to sculpt text columns; styled captions that need not sit under the image
  [other].
- **For us:** a template system can stay fixed while each client gets a distinct art
  direction. His phrase for this: art direction is "the gravity that pulls atoms
  together" [him, quoted by Allsopp].

## 2. Process and thinking (as others record it, plus his own replies)

- **Start with questions, not visuals.** Today's pitch: "every project starts with a
  conversation... I'll sometimes ask awkward questions and challenge your assumptions,
  but I'll always explain the thinking behind every design decision". He stresses "no
  layers between us" [him, sn-help.txt]. On CannyBill he spent "several days on the
  site" and wrote three goals, then wrote in his sketchbook "But why I should use
  CannyBill?" [him]. On the Smashing podcast he starts from purpose ("is it to inspire
  other speakers to get onstage?") [him].
- **Content and copy before layout.** "You need the copy before you can really do a
  great job at the design"; he writes placeholder copy himself rather than design
  against lorem ipsum [him, Unmatched Style]. For Codeboxx he rewrote the copy and
  reorganised content "before thinking about colours, illustrations, or layouts" [him].
  Reviewers of his first book name "content-out" as its first lesson (Moll, Stocks)
  [other].
- **Group workshops on large teams.** For the Smashing Magazine redesign: workshops to
  find the message, "we sat around a big table for a week", the words became design
  principles, and Dan Mall designed against them [him, podcast]. Search summaries say
  Clarke set the starting direction and Mall pushed it bolder [other, unverified]. The
  launch post only lists him as a collaborator.
- **He presents and persuades as one author.** He develops an idea alone and then sells
  it, crediting years in camera sales [him, Caffeine & Concrete]. He describes himself
  as "often a bit of a dictator... I have strong opinions on the ways that things should
  be done", and as making himself design in the open on New Internationalist to counter
  that [him, ni-help.txt].
- **Open, public critique as a working method (2009-2010).** CannyBill and New
  Internationalist were designed in public, with markup and CSS iterations posted,
  Flickr groups and comment rounds [seen]. Client staff took part:
  - Phillip Smith of New Internationalist offered to promote the process in the
    subscriber newsletter.
  - Adam Ma'anit of New Internationalist joked that "Oh fuck yes!" is "how you win
    contracts".
  - CannyBill's Wladimir: "over the moon... can't thank or recommend you both enough"
    [other].
  - Paul Annett asked for the success criteria (conversion targets); no answer is
    recorded [other].
- **Treats requirements as starting points.** "I'm treating these as starting points
  for the new design, rather than as a checkbox" [him, New Internationalist].
- **Sketch small, then hand off or build.** For his own book he drew "about half a dozen
  very simple box layout sketches" that his son Alex turned into pages. For So Foot he
  made about six layout templates on one CSS base, split into colour, theme and layout
  layers [him, podcast]. Recent projects run pencil sketch, then vector, then Eleventy
  build, delivered in two weeks to two months [him, portfolio pages].
- **Trusting judgement over testing.** He argues that risk-aversion lets testing
  outweigh an individual's judgement [him, reported by Allsopp].

## 3. Taste (outside view)

- **What others single out as distinctive:**
  - Cameron Moll: markup paired with "fine art techniques" and a "technical and visual
    duality" [other].
  - Elliot Jay Stocks: grids from printed magazines, scrapbooks and mood boards, "Create
    the right mood" [other].
  - Laura Kalbag: the detective-noir theming of Hardboiled, "scary-in-a-good-way" (and
    a bit much) [other].
  - Suleiman Leadbitter on CannyBill: "It's got your style written all over it" [other].
- **Recurring look in the client work:** bespoke illustration in place of stock imagery
  or product photography; a mascot or character; retro sources (mid-century magazine
  covers, 1960s Hanna-Barbera, Basecamp-era app sites); playful animation; texture built
  from the brand mark [seen].
- **Dislikes (stated, and echoed by whoever interviews him):** framework defaults,
  Bootstrap or Material clones, Squarespace-style themes, "jigsaw pieces" of known
  patterns; sites "not a gajillion miles away from framework default" [him]. A web that
  has lost its "joyfully naive" creative streak and its "soul" [him, as reported by Chip
  Cullen].
- **Personality in the record:**
  - Profane, joking and combative in the comment threads (the "cricket bat" for clients
    who won't upgrade; hiding his birthday 201165 in every design "in the vain hope that
    a client might notice and then send me a cake").
  - Concedes small points quickly and holds big ones [seen].
  - Clients repeatedly use "fun", "joy" and "enthusiastic" [other].

## 4. Critiques and disagreements (consolidated)

| Critic                                | Target                                       | Point                                                                                                                   |
|---------------------------------------|----------------------------------------------|-------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------|
| Dan, Richard, Jon Hicks (2008)        | Designing in the browser                     | Code-first design drifts to boxy, simple layouts; rich artwork needs a graphics tool. Clarke partly conceded.           |
| Ringlein, Lowe, Hewitt, Barnes (2008) | Showing HTML instead of comps                | Uneconomic for specialist teams and tight deadlines; works for a one-person designer who codes.                         |
| Carissa, bottleHeD (2008-09)          | HTML mock-ups                                | Clients mistake the prototype for the product and fixate on links and copy.                                             |
| CannyBill commenters (2009)           | Logo-pattern background                      | Too loud; read as flowers; hurt legibility. He agreed to tone it down.                                                  |
| Leschinski (2009)                     | Logo overlapping language bar                | Disliked it; Clarke defended breaking the grid.                                                                         |
| Rachel Andrew (2010)                  | Hardboiled keynote                           | Barebones IE experience undercuts the message; clients expect near parity in desktop browsers.                          |
| Mueggenborg, Sean Monstar (2009)      | Universal IE6 CSS                            | Unstyled fallback looks broken to clients and visitors.                                                                 |
| Chip Cullen (2015)                    | "Soul" talk                                  | Agrees the web lacks memorable design but blames immaturity, not lost creativity.                                       |
| Goodreads readers (2019-2025)         | Art Direction for the Web                    | Examples skew to editorial and blog layouts; "a print design manual awkwardly applied to the web"; no colour section; some layouts not realistic. |
| Amy (Goodreads, 2019)                 | Art Direction for the Web                    | Examples suit news and advertising, not government sites.                                                               |

The Goodreads criticism matters for us. Readers say his *book* examples are mostly
editorial or long-form. His recent *client* work (Codeboxx, Lockrose, Mountain Duck, the
composers) shows the method applied to ordinary businesses, and it gets there through
illustration and concept rather than magazine grids [seen]. For small businesses, the
client work is the better guide.

## 5. Open questions

- What exactly did Clarke contribute to the 2017 Smashing redesign before Dan Mall took
  over? Only his own podcast account and an unverified search summary exist.
- How does he present a concept to a client today: one direction or several, and as a
  sketch, comp or prototype? The 2008-2010 record says one look-and-feel comp, then
  HTML. His recent case studies say pencil, then vector, then build, but never describe
  a presentation meeting.
- There are no client-side accounts of a rejected concept or a hard negotiation. The
  testimonials are self-selected and all glowing.
- His Internazionale work with Mark Porter (2013) has no outside record; his own podcast
  page only quotes Porter on consistency across platforms.
- The SitePoint write-up calling the @media 2010 keynote "the most controversial" could
  not be read (403, and the Wayback Machine was rate-limiting). Contao 2019 "talk
  reviews" links were unreachable (connection refused).
- Mobile and small screens: no outside account says how his art direction survives on
  phones, beyond Mike Worth's "very mobile friendly" and Allsopp's note that he treats
  mobile-first and desktop-down as equally creative.
